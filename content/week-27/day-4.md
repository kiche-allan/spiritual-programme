## Revelation

John 12:24 is the principle that runs beneath the surface of every significant act of God in the biblical narrative — and it is stated so simply and so clearly that its radical content can be missed. Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone. But if it dies, it bears much fruit. The death is the condition of the fruitfulness. The burial is the prerequisite of the harvest. The breaking of the outer shell is what releases the inner life that the shell was containing and concealing.

This is the theology of brokenness — and it is not the brokenness that merely describes damage or defeat. It is the specific, purposeful, productive brokenness that Jesus is describing: the brokenness that is the pathway to fruitfulness precisely because of what it releases rather than despite what it costs. The grain that is never broken open never germinates. The jar that is never cracked never pours. The alabaster box that is never broken never releases the fragrance. In every case, the breaking is the beginning of the releasing — and the releasing is the purpose the thing was made for.

The three great images of productive brokenness in Scripture each illuminate a different dimension of the principle. Gideon's three hundred men held torches inside clay jars — and when they broke the jars, the light that had been hidden inside was suddenly, simultaneously, dramatically revealed. The breaking of the jars was the strategy of the battle. God specifically chose clay jars rather than held torches because the breaking was the point: the light that was hidden by the intact jar could only be seen when the jar was broken. The victory came through the breaking.

Mary's alabaster jar in Mark 14 gives us the most intimate version of the same principle. She broke the jar over Jesus's head — the Greek word is kataklao, to break completely, to break so that it cannot be repaired and the contents can be fully poured out. The fragrance filled the entire house. The disciples objected to the waste. But Jesus said she had done a beautiful thing — because the breaking that the disciples called waste was the releasing that He called worship. The brokenness was the completeness of the offering.

Jacob's hip at Peniel is the most personal version. He wrestled with the angel through the night and was winning — until the angel touched his hip and put it out of socket. And Jacob limped for the rest of his life. The wound was permanent. And it was also the mark of the encounter that changed his name from Jacob — the supplanter, the one who grabs — to Israel, the one who wrestles with God and prevails. The brokenness and the blessing came in the same moment, from the same touch. The limp was the evidence of the encounter. Jacob walked differently after Peniel — and he was a different man.

Psalm 51:17 gives us the theological summary: the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise. The brokenness that God values is not the brokenness of despair or defeat. It is the specific brokenness of the spirit that has stopped insisting on its own way, of the heart that has released the self-sufficiency and self-sovereignty that was preventing the fruitfulness. The broken and contrite heart is not the heart that has given up. It is the heart that has given over — that has released to God what it was previously gripping, and in the releasing has become the very thing God does not despise.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the theology of the grain of wheat — the truth that the breaking is not the end of the fruitfulness but its beginning, that the burial is not the defeat of the purpose but the condition of its fulfilment. Where I have been resisting the breaking, interpreting it as destruction rather than as the releasing of what You put inside me for this very purpose, give me the faith of the grain that trusts the ground.

I bring You the specific brokenness I am currently carrying — the cracked jar, the wounded hip, the thing that has not gone the way I planned. And I receive from John 12:24 the truth that the dying is the condition of the much fruit, not its cancellation. Let the breaking release what the intact shell was containing.

A broken and contrite heart You will not despise. Here is mine.

## Daily Practices

- Read John 12:20–26 in full — the full context of the grain of wheat. Note that Jesus says this in response to the Greeks who want to see Him — the moment when the full scope of His mission becomes visible. And His response to the request to see Him is the grain of wheat principle. Ask: what does it mean that Jesus chose to explain who He is through the image of the dying grain rather than any other image available to Him?
- Read Judges 7:15–22 — Gideon's battle with the jars. Note that the strategy required the breaking of something that was protecting something else. The light was already inside. The breaking was just the revealing. Ask: what is the light that is currently inside a jar that has not yet been broken — the gift, the calling, the life that is waiting for the breaking of what is currently containing it?
- Journal: "What is the specific brokenness in my current season — the cracked jar, the wounded hip, the thing that did not survive intact — that I have been mourning as loss rather than receiving as the releasing of something? What does the grain of wheat principle say about what that breaking might be producing underground that I cannot yet see above the surface?" Write the honest exploration.
- Read 2 Corinthians 4:7–10 in full — we have this treasure in jars of clay, we are hard pressed but not crushed, struck down but not destroyed, always carrying in our body the death of Jesus so that the life of Jesus may be revealed. Ask: where in my body and my life is the death of Jesus currently being carried — and where is the life of Jesus being revealed through exactly that carrying?
