## Revelation

The most liberating doctrine in the entire New Testament is the one that the performance mindset most consistently undermines — not through outright rejection but through the subtle, persistent, deeply ingrained tendency to treat the righteousness that has been given as something that still needs to be earned, maintained, or re-established after every failure. Imputed righteousness — the gift of the righteousness of Christ credited to the account of the believer through faith — is the theological foundation of the Christian life. But most believers who affirm it doctrinally do not inhabit it experientially. They know the doctrine and live by the performance. They confess the gift and spend their days trying to earn what has already been given.

Romans 3:21–22 announces the doctrine with the urgency and the relief of the most important news that has ever been delivered: but now — in contrast to the entire preceding argument about the failure of every human being to achieve the righteousness the law requires — apart from the law, the righteousness of God has been made known. Apart from the law. Not through the law, not in addition to the law, not by doing the law better. Apart from it — outside its jurisdiction, by a completely different mechanism. The righteousness that the law required and that no human being ever fully achieved has been made available through faith in Jesus Christ to all who believe. The achievement is His. The receipt is faith. The result is the standing before God that the law always required and that the law could never produce.

2 Corinthians 5:21 gives us the mechanics of the exchange in the most compressed and most staggering sentence in the Pauline corpus: God made Him who had no sin to be sin for us, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God. The exchange is complete and bilateral. Christ took what was ours — the sin, the guilt, the condemnation, the judicial standing of those who had broken every requirement of the law. And we received what was His — the righteousness, the acceptance, the standing before the Father of the One who had perfectly fulfilled every requirement of the law. Not a partial exchange. Not a transaction in which our debt was cancelled and we were left at zero. A full exchange in which His positive righteousness was credited to our account. We did not merely stop being guilty. We became, in Him, the righteousness of God.

Paul's testimony in Philippians 3:8–9 is the most personally honest account of what this doctrine means for a person who had previously built their entire identity on achieved righteousness. He had been a Pharisee of Pharisees, blameless under the law, a Hebrew of Hebrews — the most impressive religious CV available in the first century. And he counted it all garbage — the Greek is skubalon, which is considerably stronger than garbage in the polite sense — for the surpassing worth of knowing Christ and being found in Him, not having a righteousness of his own that comes from the law, but that which is through faith in Christ. The righteousness of my own is the thing Paul is most deliberately setting aside. Not because his achievements were worthless in themselves. But because the righteousness of God that comes through faith in Christ is so categorically superior to the best righteousness a human being can produce that the comparison makes the best human righteousness look like garbage.

The practical implication of imputed righteousness for the daily Christian life is the recovery of a specific kind of settled confidence before God that the performance mindset perpetually undermines. The person who is living from imputed righteousness does not approach God with the anxiety of someone whose standing depends on how well they have been performing. They approach with the boldness that Hebrews 4:16 commands — come boldly to the throne of grace — because the standing they have before God is not their own standing. It is Christ's standing. And Christ's standing before the Father is not affected by how the believer's week has gone. It is perfect. Permanent. Unaffected by failure. The righteousness that was given through faith is the righteousness that is kept through faith — not through performance, not through the maintenance of a sufficient spiritual track record, but through the continued resting in the One whose righteousness it originally was and whose it remains.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the righteousness that is not my own — the righteousness of Christ, credited to my account through faith, making me before You not merely forgiven but righteous. Not righteous in the performance sense — the achieved, maintained, vulnerable-to-failure righteousness of my own effort. Righteous in the imputed sense — clothed in the perfect righteousness of the One who perfectly fulfilled every requirement of the law and whose standing before You is now my standing through union with Him.

Let this truth change how I approach You today. Not the anxious petition of someone whose standing depends on how well the week has gone. The bold, settled, grateful approach of someone who knows that the standing they have is not their own and therefore cannot be lost by anything they do or fail to do.

I am found in Him. Not having a righteousness of my own. The righteousness that comes from God on the basis of faith. It is enough. It is more than enough. It is everything.

## Daily Practices

- Read Romans 3:9–26 in full — the full argument from the universal failure of human righteousness to the gift of divine righteousness through faith. Note the specific movement: all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, and all are justified freely by his grace through the redemption that came by Christ Jesus. The all who sinned and the all who are justified are the same all. Ask: what does it mean that the universality of the failure is matched by the universality of the offer?
- Read Romans 4:1–8 — Abraham's righteousness credited through faith, and David's description of the blessedness of the person whose sins are forgiven and whose righteousness is credited without works. Note especially verse 5: to the one who does not work but trusts God who justifies the ungodly, their faith is credited as righteousness. Ask: what does it mean that God justifies the ungodly — not the godly who need a little help, but the ungodly who have nothing to offer?
- Journal: "Where have I been treating the imputed righteousness of Christ as a starting point that I then need to maintain through performance — approaching God with anxiety about my standing rather than confidence in His gift? What would it look like to genuinely inhabit the settled confidence that comes from knowing my righteousness is not my own and therefore cannot be lost by my failures?" Write the honest exploration.
- Read Hebrews 10:19–22 in full. Note the specific basis of the bold approach to the throne: we have confidence to enter the Most Holy Place by the blood of Jesus. The confidence is not our own confidence — in our spiritual performance, our consistency, our holiness. It is the confidence that the blood provides. Practise approaching God today with the specific boldness that Hebrews commands — because the standing is His, not yours.
