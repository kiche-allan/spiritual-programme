## Revelation

The entire arc of Scripture is a homecoming story. It begins in a garden where God and humanity walked together in the cool of the day — where the relationship was unmediated, the provision was complete, and the distance between the Creator and the creature was exactly what God had designed it to be: none. And it ends in a garden-city where God and humanity dwell together permanently — where the relationship is again unmediated, the provision is again complete, and the distance is again what God designed it to be: none. Everything between Genesis 3 and Revelation 21 is the story of how the homecoming becomes possible from the ruins of the departure.

The week has given us five dimensions of the God who makes the homecoming possible. He is the God who comes down — who does not wait for us to reach Him but descends to reach us, all the way to the bottom, all the way to Calvary. He is the patient God — who holds the door open longer than justice alone requires, who renews the mercy every morning, who is not willing that any should perish. He is the God of the burning bush — who shows up in the ordinary wilderness, who meets us in the working hours and the daily routine and the far side of the desert where we have stopped expecting the encounter. He is the God who works through brokenness — who takes the cracked jar and the wounded hip and the buried grain and produces from them the fruitfulness that the intact thing could never have yielded. He is the friend of sinners — who sought out the company of the compromised and the corrupt, who went to the table of the tax collector and the house of Zacchaeus, who wore the accusation of the Pharisees as a mission statement.

And He is the God who leads by presence rather than plan — who goes before us in the cloud and the fire, who gives the word at the moment of the turning rather than the map of the whole journey, who requires the kind of following that trusts the Guide more than the route.

All of these are dimensions of the same God who is also the God of Revelation 21:3–4 — the God who will dwell with His people, who will wipe every tear from every face that has wept in the long middle of the story, who will make the former things pass away and the new things come in their fullness. The homecoming is not a distant hope disconnected from the present journey. It is the destination that gives the present journey its meaning, its direction, and its endurance.

Romans 8:19 tells us that creation itself knows the homecoming is coming — that the eager longing of the created order is the longing of something that knows what it was made for and has not yet arrived there. The groaning is not despair. It is the specific longing of the homesick, of the person who is on the journey and can feel the direction of the destination even when the destination is not yet visible. We are homesick. And the homesickness is the most reliable evidence that we have a home.

Luke 15:20 gives us the image of the homecoming that most completely captures what Revelation 21:3–4 is promising: the father who saw the son while he was still a long way off, who was filled with compassion, who ran. The running father is the image of the God who is coming toward us in the homecoming as urgently and as joyfully as the father ran toward the prodigal. He is not waiting for us to complete the journey on our own. He is running toward us. The distance between where we are and where we are going is being covered from both directions — our walking and His running — and the embrace at the end is not the stiff formality of a God who has agreed to receive us on condition but the joyful, tearful, robe-and-ring-giving reunion of a Father who never stopped watching the road.

The week closes here — not at the summit of theological achievement but at the road, still a long way from home, and the Father already running. Keep walking. He is running toward you. The homecoming is certain. And it will be worth everything the journey has cost.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I close this week with the great homecoming — the certain, coming, joyful reunion of Revelation 21, where every tear is wiped and every former thing has passed away and Your dwelling place is with Your people permanently. Let this be the destination that gives the whole journey its meaning today.

I am homesick. Let me receive that homesickness not as the grief of something lost but as the compass of something coming — the eager longing of the creation that knows what it was made for and has not yet arrived there. You are coming toward me the way the father ran toward the prodigal. Every step I take toward You, You are running.

The former things will pass away. The new things will come. And the voice from the throne will say: behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. I am walking toward that day. Let me walk with the joy of someone who knows the Father is already running.

## Daily Practices

- Read Revelation 21:1–22:5 in full — the complete new creation vision. Let it be vivid and specific — the river of life, the tree of life, the city with no temple because God Himself is the temple, the face of God seen directly, no more night. Ask: which specific detail of the new creation speaks most powerfully to the specific loss or longing you are currently carrying in this season?
- Review the entire week — the God who comes down, the patience of God, the burning bush, brokenness and fruitfulness, the friend of sinners, the cloud and the fire. Write one sentence for each day capturing what it produced in you. Seven sentences. This is your testimony of the week and of the homecoming journey you are on.
- Journal: "What does it mean to live toward the homecoming — to inhabit the present journey with the awareness of where it is going? What would change in how I endure the difficult, love the people around me, and hold the things of this world if the running Father and the Revelation 21 destination were the most practically relevant facts about my situation?" Write the honest exploration.
- Read Luke 15:11–24 one final time — the complete prodigal account. Read it as the story of where you are going rather than where you have been. You are on the road. The Father has already seen you. He is already running. Receive the robe and the ring and the feast not as future theological categories but as the present, incoming, certain reality of the homecoming that is already in motion. Walk toward it today.
