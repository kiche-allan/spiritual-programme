## Revelation

The most important question for understanding how a person lives in the present is the question of how they understand the future. What you believe about the end determines everything about how you inhabit the middle. The person who believes that history is going nowhere — that the universe is winding down toward meaninglessness, that death is the final word on every human story — lives differently from the person who believes that history is going somewhere, that the arc of creation bends toward redemption, that the God who spoke everything into existence at the beginning will speak everything into its final, perfected form at the end. Eschatology is not a peripheral doctrine for theological specialists. It is the framework that gives meaning to everything that happens before the end arrives.

Revelation 21:3–4 gives us the clearest picture of the end available in Scripture — and it is nothing like most people imagine heaven to be. The standard popular imagination of heaven is a disembodied existence on clouds, vaguely spiritual, somewhat passive, dimly pleasant. The Revelation picture is entirely different. Look, the voice says — as if the announcement is so surprising that it requires the deliberate direction of attention. God's dwelling place is now among the people. Not the people's dwelling place is now with God — as though the human beings have been transported to a spiritual realm far removed from creation. God's dwelling place is among the people — which means God has moved, not upward and away from creation but downward and into it, establishing His permanent residence in the renewed creation in a way that fulfils the pattern of the tabernacle, the temple, and the incarnation — the God who has been moving toward His creation from the beginning, finally, completely, permanently arriving.

He will wipe every tear from their eyes. The tenderness of this image is staggering. The God of the universe, the One before whom the seraphim cover their faces, who inhabits eternity and whose ways are higher than the heavens — this God will personally, directly, tenderly wipe the tears from the eyes of every person who has cried in the long middle of history. Not delegated to an angel. Not accomplished by a process. He will wipe. The hand of God on the face of the person who has wept. Every tear — not the significant ones, not the ones that were noble and theologically productive, but every tear, including the ones cried for reasons that felt too small to matter, the ones cried in the middle of the night when no one was watching, the ones that came from griefs that no one else knew about. Every single one.

There will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain, for the old order of things has passed away. The old order — the age of entropy, of loss, of the relentless diminishment that sin introduced into the fabric of creation — will have passed. Not merely improved. Passed away. Replaced by the new order, the new creation, the age in which what God always intended is finally, fully, permanently what is. Romans 8:23 describes the believer's current state as holding the firstfruits of the Spirit while groaning inwardly for the full harvest — the redemption of the body, the completion of the adoption, the arrival of what the Spirit has been the deposit and the guarantee of. The groan is not despair. It is the groan of someone who has tasted enough of what is coming to know that what is coming is far greater than what is currently available, and who cannot be satisfied with the partial when the complete has been promised.

1 Corinthians 15:58 gives us the practical implication of the eschatological hope — the reason it is not merely comfort for the dying but energy for the living. Therefore — because of the resurrection, because of the new creation, because of the certainty of the end that Revelation 21 describes — stand firm. Let nothing move you. Always give yourselves fully to the work of the Lord, because you know that your labour in the Lord is not in vain. The eschatological hope does not produce passivity — the temptation to sit and wait for the end to arrive. It produces the most energetic, most fully committed, most unshakeable form of present engagement available, because the person who knows that their labour is not in vain has the most compelling reason available to keep labouring. Nothing done in Christ's name is ultimately pointless. Nothing offered in faith is ultimately wasted. The small faithfulness of the ordinary day is being woven into a tapestry that will only be seen in full when the old order has passed and the new has come.

Living backwards from the end means carrying Revelation 21 into Monday morning — knowing that the tears God will wipe away are the tears currently being cried, and that the labour which is not in vain is the labour of this specific week. It means the hospital corridor has meaning because the God who will wipe every tear is the same God who is present in the corridor right now. It means the faithfulness in obscurity matters because the God who sees in secret is the God who will bring every hidden thing to light at the end. It means the grief is real and the hope is realer — and the person who holds both, without denying either, is the person who is living in the full theological reality of what the gospel has made available.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I close this week standing in the eschatological hope — the end that is certain, the new creation that is coming, the tears that You will personally wipe from every face that has wept in the long middle of history. Let this hope be the most practically energising truth available to me today — not a comfort that removes me from the present but the vision that makes the present worth fully inhabiting.

Let nothing move me. Not because my circumstances are stable but because my end is settled. Let me give myself fully to the work of the Lord this week, this day, this hour — knowing that the labour is not in vain, that nothing done in Your name is ultimately pointless, that the small faithfulness of the ordinary day is being woven into something that will only be seen fully when You make all things new.

There will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain. That day is coming. And the God who is coming with it is the same God who is here right now.

## Daily Practices

- Read Revelation 21:1–5 and Revelation 22:1–5 together — the full picture of the new creation. Note what is present: the river of life, the tree of life, the throne of God and the Lamb, the face of God seen directly, His name on their foreheads, no more night. Note what is absent: the sea (the symbol of chaos), death, mourning, crying, pain, the curse. Ask: how does this picture change the way you understand what you are living toward?
- Read 1 Corinthians 15:35–58 in full — the full resurrection chapter. Note the specific argument Paul makes about the resurrection body: it is sown perishable and raised imperishable, sown in dishonour and raised in glory, sown in weakness and raised in power, sown a natural body and raised a spiritual body. Ask: how does the certainty of the resurrection body change how you think about your current physical experience — your health, your weakness, your limitations?
- Journal: "What specific grief, loss, or difficulty in my current life is most in need of the eschatological perspective — the reminder that the old order is passing away and the new is coming? What does the promise of Revelation 21:4 say specifically to that situation?" Write the honest answer.
- Read the entire week's themes in sequence — the holiness of God, the hiddenness of God, the weight of glory, cruciformity, the intercession of Christ, theosis, and the eschatological hope — and write one sentence that captures what each day produced in you. Seven sentences. Then ask: how do all seven connect? What is the single thread running through them all? Write it — and let it be the prayer you carry forward.
