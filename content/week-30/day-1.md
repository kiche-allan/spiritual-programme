## Revelation

Have you ever stopped to think about who you are going to spend eternity with?

Not in an abstract, theological sense — but specifically. The actual people. The ones you know. Because here is something that nobody tends to say out loud but that is absolutely, unavoidably true: some of the people you find most difficult right now are among God's choicest servants. And you are going to be with them forever. In fact, there is every chance that God — with His particular sense of humour and His particular commitment to your sanctification — is going to give them the condo right next to yours.

I know that is not what you wanted to hear. But it is the truth, and it is worth sitting with.

The body of Christ is not a curated community of people who naturally fit together. It is not a fellowship assembled on the basis of compatible personalities, similar tastes, or matching levels of emotional intelligence. Paul says in 1 Corinthians 12:18 that God has placed each part in the body just as he wanted it to be. Not as you would have arranged it. Not as the people themselves would have chosen. As He wanted it. The diversity of the body of Christ — across background, temperament, culture, denomination, preference, and irritating personal habit — is not a design flaw. It is the design.

We are the fellowship of the Neapolitans — all kinds of flavours in one body. Some who grew up Catholic, some Baptist, some charismatic, some from traditions you have barely heard of. Some who worship loudly and some who are silent before God. Some who arrive early and some who arrive so late that the sermon is half over. Some who cry in worship and some who find public emotion deeply uncomfortable. Some who are driven and some who are gentle. Some who challenge you every time they open their mouth and some who encourage you every time they walk into the room.

And then there are the ones who rub you the wrong way. The ones who have an edge to them that catches on you every time your paths cross. The ones whose opinions land wrong, whose style grates, whose presence in the room produces a specific tension in your chest that you have learned to manage by keeping a certain distance.

Here is what Scripture will not let you do with those people: it will not let you like some and love others. It will not let you pick and choose from the body of Christ based on personal preference and call it fellowship. 1 John 4:20 is as direct as the New Testament gets on this: if anyone says I love God and hates his brother, he is a liar. Not mistaken. Not immature. A liar. Because the love of God, genuinely received, does not stay comfortably directed only toward the people who are easy to love. It flows outward — toward the ones who are hard, the ones who are different, the ones who occupy the condo next door in a floor plan you did not design.

Now, love does not mean agreement. Paul and Barnabas had such a sharp disagreement over John Mark that they parted ways — and both of them were right with God and effective in ministry on the other side of the separation (Acts 15:39–40). Love does not require the suppression of honest disagreement or the pretence that every difference of opinion is easily resolved. But it does require something more demanding than agreement — it requires the consistent, chosen, costly commitment to the good of the other person regardless of whether they are making it easy for you.

Lean in to the person who rubs you wrong. Get close enough to see them through the eyes of the God who placed them in the body exactly as He wanted. Let the friction of that relationship knock off some edges in your own character that you have not been able to see because you have been keeping your distance from the people who would reveal them.

You are going to be neighbours forever. You might as well start learning how to be good ones now.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to You today honest about the people in the body of Christ that I have been loving at a distance — the ones I keep a careful gap from, the ones whose presence produces tension, the ones I would not have chosen for the community You have placed me in. Forgive me for the theology that says I can love You without loving them. Give me the specific, chosen, costly love that You demonstrated toward me when I was not easy to love — the love that leans in rather than pulls back, that sees the person rather than the friction, that knows we are going to be neighbours forever and starts building that relationship now. Not because it is easy. Because it is Yours.

## Daily Practices

- Read 1 John 4:7–21 in full — the complete passage on love. Note that John grounds the command to love one another not in the lovability of the brother but in the nature of God: God is love, and whoever loves has been born of God and knows God (verse 7–8). Ask: if my love for others is a reflection of my knowledge of God, what does my current relationship with the difficult people in my life say about how well I know Him?
- Read Romans 15:1–7 — we who are strong ought to bear with the failings of the weak. Note verse 7: accept one another, just as Christ accepted you. Ask: how did Christ accept me — and is that the standard I am applying to the person I find most difficult?
- Journal: "Who is the specific person in the body of Christ I have been keeping my distance from — and what specifically about them produces the friction? What would it look like to lean in this week rather than pull back? What is the first practical step?" Write the honest answer and the specific step.
- Read Colossians 3:12–14 — clothe yourselves with compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness and patience. Bear with each other and forgive one another. And over all these virtues put on love. Ask: which of these garments am I failing to put on in my relationship with the difficult person — and what would dressing properly for that relationship look like today?
