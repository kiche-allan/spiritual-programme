## Revelation

In 1941, C.S. Lewis preached a sermon at the University Church of St Mary the Virgin in Oxford that has become one of the most important pieces of Christian writing in the twentieth century. He called it The Weight of Glory — and the argument he made in it has the quality of something that has always been true but has never been said quite this clearly before. He began with a question about desire. Why is it that human beings are persistently haunted by longings that nothing in this world can satisfy? The beauty that pierces you and then passes. The joy that swells and then deflates. The music that seems to carry you to the edge of something transcendent and then stops before you arrive. The nostalgia for a home you have never visited. The ache that the most perfect earthly moment produces — the ache of something that should be more than it is, that points beyond itself to something it cannot itself provide. Lewis's answer was simple and devastating: if we find ourselves with a desire that nothing in this world can satisfy, the most probable explanation is that we were made for another world.

This is the weight of glory. Not the burden of it but the gravity — the specific density of an eternal reality pressing itself into the present through the medium of human longing. 2 Corinthians 4:17 describes the present troubles as light and momentary — a description that only makes sense from the perspective of eternity, not from the perspective of someone inside the trouble. The glory that is coming does not merely outweigh the trouble. It far outweighs it — the Greek is kath huperbolen eis huperbolen, which means beyond all comparison, excess upon excess, a weight of glory so far beyond the weight of the suffering that the comparison is almost meaningless. What is coming is that much greater than what is currently being endured.

Romans 8:18 makes the same claim with the same confidence: the present sufferings are not worth comparing with the glory that will be revealed in us. Not merely to us, as spectators of something magnificent. In us — as participants in a glory that has been forming inside the sufferings, produced by them, refined by them, made possible by the very weight that has been pressing on the person who carries it. The glory that is coming is not unrelated to the suffering that preceded it. It is in some way its fruit — the thing the suffering was producing in the person who endured it faithfully, the weight of eternity pressing itself into the person through the medium of the difficulty they are walking through.

1 John 3:2 gives us the most staggering statement of the destination: we shall be like Him, for we shall see Him as He is. The telos — the final end, the destination toward which the whole Christian life has been moving — is likeness to Christ. Not merely being in His presence, though that is included. Not merely being forgiven and accepted, though that is the foundation. Becoming genuinely, completely, permanently like Him. Bearing the family resemblance in its fullest expression. The process that began at conversion — the renewing of the mind, the fruit of the Spirit, the gradual conformity to the image of Christ — will be completed in a moment, when we see Him as He is and the seeing produces the final, irreversible transformation that the whole journey has been building toward.

The ache you feel when beauty moves you — that is the ache of the weight of glory pressing against the limits of what this world can contain. The longing that no relationship has ever fully satisfied — that is the longing of a creature made for union with its Creator, settling temporarily for the partial satisfactions available in the interim. The sense that even the best moments of your life are pointing beyond themselves — that the joy is real but incomplete, that it is a signpost rather than a destination — that is the accurate perception of a person who was made for something eternal and has not yet arrived at it. The weight of glory is pressing on you right now, from the other side of what is coming, and the ache is the pressure of eternity making itself known inside time.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the weight of glory that Paul describes — the eternal reality that far outweighs the present trouble, the glory that is being achieved in me through the very difficulties that feel like they are working against me. Let my eyes be adjusted to the eternal perspective that makes 2 Corinthians 4:17 honest rather than merely theological. Let the ache I carry for what is not yet become the compass that keeps me oriented toward what is coming rather than the grief of what is absent.

And let the destination of 1 John 3:2 be the most practically relevant fact about my life today: I will be like Him, for I will see Him as He is. Everything I am walking through right now is moving me toward that. Let that truth press into my ordinary Tuesday with the weight of the eternal reality it actually is.

## Daily Practices

- Read 2 Corinthians 4:7–18 in full. Note the full contrast Paul establishes — jars of clay and the all-surpassing power, the outwardly wasting and the inwardly being renewed, the light and momentary and the eternal glory. Ask: which side of each contrast best describes where my attention has been this week?
- Read Romans 8:18–25 — the creation groaning, the believers groaning, the Spirit groaning. Note that the groaning is not despair. It is the groan of something waiting for what it was made for — the eagerness of a creation that knows what is coming and cannot wait for its arrival. Ask: can I identify the groan in my own life — the specific ache that is the longing of something made for eternity, pressing against the limits of what time can provide?
- Journal: "What are the specific longings in my life that nothing in this world has ever fully satisfied? What is each one pointing toward? What does it mean that these longings exist in me — what does their existence say about what I was made for?" Write the honest answer.
- Read C.S. Lewis's sermon The Weight of Glory if you have access to it — it is widely available online. If not, read the opening pages of his book Mere Christianity, specifically the chapters on hope. Let his argument about desire and destination sit with you through the day.
