Israel voted in late October and produced the result its politics keeps producing: nobody has 61. When the final count came in, Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc held 53 of the 120 seats in the Knesset. Gadi Eisenkot's bloc held 48. The two Arab-led parties, the Joint List and Ra'am, won a record 19 between them. Likud is still the largest single party with 27 seats, ahead of Eisenkot's Yashar with 21. Turnout, close to 78 per cent, was the highest since 1999.

On paper, the next stage went Eisenkot's way. After President Isaac Herzog finished his consultations, 67 lawmakers recommended Eisenkot and 46 recommended Netanyahu. Seven, from United Torah Judaism, recommended nobody. On 8 November, Herzog gave Eisenkot the mandate to form a government. He has 28 days, until 6 December, with a possible extension of 14 more. And yet Netanyahu is still prime minister. As caretaker, he keeps running the government until a successor is actually in place.

That gap between the headline and the arithmetic is the whole story. The 67 is not a coalition. It is Eisenkot's 48 seats added to the 19 held by the Arab-led parties, and those parties have not agreed to the terms Eisenkot has set. He has four conditions for any partner: recognition of Israel as a Jewish and democratic state, commitment to the values of the Declaration of Independence, acceptance of some form of military, national or civilian service for all citizens, and recognition of Hamas as a terrorist organisation along with a commitment to dismantle its military and governing capabilities. Ra'am's leader, Mansour Abbas, has condemned the attack of 7 October but has said he will not set out his position on Hamas in public. The Joint List is reported to be unlikely to accept the fourth condition. Without them, Eisenkot is stuck at 48.

There is a second route, and it is just as tight. Shas and United Torah Judaism together hold 13 seats, and 48 plus 13 is exactly 61. But the third condition, service for all, runs straight into their long opposition to drafting yeshiva students, even though Israel's Supreme Court has ruled that the blanket exemption has no legal basis. Both routes to a majority pass through a condition that one of the partners resists. Herzog himself said the numbers alone would not decide it, pointing to the "mutual vetoes between the blocs and within the blocs themselves."

Now look at what Netanyahu has. His bloc is larger than Eisenkot's, and Likud is the biggest party. He cannot reach 61 either, since United Torah Judaism declined to recommend him, but he does not have to win anything yet. He can wait, offer Eisenkot a national unity government, and look for defections from the other side, such as Naftali Bennett or Avigdor Lieberman, who have worked with him before. Eisenkot's allies have vowed not to sit with him. If Eisenkot fails, the president may hand the mandate to another lawmaker, most likely Netanyahu. If that fails too, the Knesset dissolves and elections follow within 90 days. Through all of it, Netanyahu stays in office until a replacement is in place. A failure for Eisenkot is not just a stalemate. It is a way back for Netanyahu, or a new election with Netanyahu in the chair.

Meanwhile, the pressure from outside is rising. On 10 November, Iran's Revolutionary Guards showed footage of what they called a new underground "missile city", holding ballistic and cruise missiles and launchers. Iran's armed forces chief of staff toured the site and said its advanced missiles would "surprise enemies", and that any new attack would be answered beyond the region. Analysts noted that the site cannot be independently confirmed as new, and some read the display as a rebuttal to Netanyahu's claim in March that Iran could no longer produce ballistic missiles. Whether it is preparation or deterrence, it sends one message to both Jerusalem and Washington: Iran expects another round and wants to be seen as ready for it.

Israel appears to be reading it the same way. On 11 November, Israeli media, citing the public broadcaster KAN, reported that the security cabinet, chaired by Netanyahu, had ordered the army to step up preparations for a possible attack on Iran. In October, Netanyahu said Israel was prepared to strike pre-emptively even without American support, according to Israel Hayom. Eisenkot has urged Netanyahu not to deploy the military without a direct and immediate threat. Qatar is mediating between Washington and Tehran, and President Trump has said he is ready to resume the war if that track fails.

This is the other half of the argument. In a security crisis, the person in the chair sets the agenda, and a challenger has to build a coalition while decisions are being made around him. That is why many observers think that removing Netanyahu will be hard in the near term. The counter-case is real too. Eisenkot has the mandate, the larger number of recommendations and several weeks to negotiate, and Netanyahu's own bloc has its fault lines. The honest summary is that neither man has 61. The difference is that one of them is already sitting in the chair.

The things to watch are simple. Will Ra'am or the Joint List find a formula for the fourth condition? Will the ultra-Orthodox parties move on national service? Will anyone cross between the blocs? Will events with Iran force a unity government before 6 December? Each answer could change the picture within days.

For those of us who pray, it is worth remembering the instruction of Psalm 122:6: "Pray for the peace of Jerusalem: 'May those who love you be secure.'" Whoever ends up governing, there are millions of ordinary people in Israel, in Iran and across the region whose lives depend on the decisions made in the coming weeks.
