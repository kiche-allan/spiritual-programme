## Revelation

John 4 is one of the most carefully constructed narratives in the Gospels, and it is worth reading slowly enough to notice everything that is happening in it. Jesus is travelling through Samaria — which Jewish men of His day typically avoided entirely, going out of their way not to pass through it because of the historical and religious hostility between Jews and Samaritans. He stops at a well in the middle of the day. A woman comes to draw water at noon — the heat of the day, when no one else would be there, which tells us immediately that she has been coming alone, avoiding the morning crowd of women who gather at the well in the cool of the day.

Everything about this encounter breaks the rules of the world it is happening in. Jesus is Jewish and she is Samaritan. He is a man and she is a woman. He is a rabbi and she is someone with a history so complicated that even the other women of her village apparently could not bear her company. The disciples are astonished when they return and find Him talking with her — not because of anything she has said or done but simply because of who she is. She is the wrong person in the wrong place at the wrong time. And Jesus is already in conversation with her.

The first lesson of the woman at the well is that Jesus crosses every boundary to get to the one person who needs Him. He did not accidentally pass through Samaria. John 4:4 says He had to go through Samaria — a phrase that implies more than geographical necessity. There was a divine must in the route. He had to go through Samaria because there was a woman at a well at noon who was about to have her life changed, and nothing about the social or religious conventions of His world was going to prevent Him from getting there.

The second lesson is the distinction between the water that keeps you thirsty and the water that becomes a spring. Everyone who drinks this water will be thirsty again. Jesus is not only talking about the water in the well. He is describing every substitute for Himself that human beings construct — every relationship, achievement, pleasure, or identity that promises to satisfy the deepest thirst and cannot. The woman had tried five of them. The sixth was not working either. And Jesus offers something categorically different — not better water from the same well, but a different kind of water entirely: water that becomes in you a spring, welling up to eternal life. The person who has drunk of this water does not keep returning to the same wells. The source has moved inside.

The third lesson is the power of the honest question. He asked her to call her husband, which she knew was a tender and exposed place. She answered honestly — I have no husband. And Jesus affirmed the honesty: what you have said is quite true. He did not shame her. He did not condemn her. He named the reality — you have had five husbands, and the man you now have is not your husband — and He named it in a way that was not a verdict but a recognition. He saw her. Completely. Without the performance, without the managed presentation she had presumably offered everyone else. And He did not look away.

The fourth and most important lesson is the water jar. She came to the well to fill a jar. She left the jar behind. This is the clearest sign in the text that something has genuinely happened — the thing she came for has been replaced by something so much greater that she forgot to take it. The person who has had a genuine encounter with Jesus does not leave the same way they arrived. They leave something behind — the old thirst, the old identity, the old way of managing the emptiness — and they go running toward the people they had been hiding from, saying: come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. The testimony is not polished. It is immediate, personal, and irresistible — and the whole town came.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to You today as the woman came to the well — with my own version of the water jar, carrying the thirst I have been trying to satisfy at the wrong wells. I bring You the five husbands of my own history — the things I have tried before You, the substitutes that promised more than they delivered, the relationships and achievements and identities I have built around the emptiness where Your water was always supposed to be.

Give me the water that becomes a spring. Not better versions of what I have already been drinking. Something categorically different — something that wells up rather than runs out, that comes from inside rather than needing to be fetched, that makes me stop coming back to the same old wells because I have found the source.

And let me leave my water jar. Let the encounter with You today produce in me the same forgetfulness it produced in her — the joyful, astonished forgetfulness of someone who has received something so much greater than what they came for that the original errand simply ceased to matter.

## Daily Practices

- Read John 4:1–42 in full — the complete account. Read it slowly enough to notice every detail: the time of day, the geography, the specific questions Jesus asks, the specific things the woman says, the disciples' reaction, the town's response. Let the specificity of the narrative make it feel like a real event rather than a theological illustration.
- Journal: "What are the wells I keep returning to — the things I keep drinking from that leave me thirsty again? What would it look like for me to leave my water jar at this particular well and walk away from it toward the living water Jesus offers?"
- Note verse 4 — He had to go through Samaria. Ask: is there someone in your life who is the wrong person in the wrong place at the wrong time — someone you have been avoiding or overlooking — who Jesus may be specifically directing you toward?
- Read the woman's testimony in verses 28–29 and 39. Note how simple it is: come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. Ask: what is the equivalent sentence that tells your story with Jesus? What did He tell you, show you, or give you that you could say as simply and honestly as she said this?
