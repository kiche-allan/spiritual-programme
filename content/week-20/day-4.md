## Revelation

The names of God in Scripture are not titles assigned by theologians in a study. They are testimonies generated in the field — the specific, crisis-born declarations of people who encountered a specific dimension of God's character in a specific moment of need and gave what they found a name that would outlast the moment. Jehovah Jireh — the Lord will provide — was not a theological proposition Abraham formulated in a quiet hour of reflection. It was the name he gave the mountain where he had raised the knife over his son and found a ram in the thicket at the last possible moment. Jehovah Rapha — the Lord who heals — was the name given at the waters of Marah, when Israel was dying of thirst and the water was bitter and Moses cried out and God showed him a piece of wood to throw in, and the water became sweet. Every name is a story. Every title is a crisis resolved.

Exodus 17:15 gives us Jehovah Nissi — the Lord is my Banner. Moses built an altar and called it this after the battle with the Amalekites, where Israel prevailed exactly as long as Moses held his staff in the air and began to lose the moment his arms dropped and Aaron and Hur had to hold them up for him. The banner in the ancient world was the standard raised over the battlefield — the flag that said whose army this was and under whose authority they were fighting. When Moses named the altar Jehovah Nissi, he was declaring that the victory had not belonged to Israel's military strength. It had belonged to the One under whose banner they were fighting — the One who was the army's authority, the army's identity, the army's source of power. The battle was the Lord's. The banner was His. The victory was the evidence.

Judges 6:24 gives us Jehovah Shalom — the Lord is Peace — in one of the most unlikely contexts available. Gideon has just been visited by the angel of the Lord, has just received the commission to deliver Israel from the Midianites, and is immediately terrified — not by the Midianites, but by the realisation that he has seen the angel of the Lord face to face. In the ancient world, to see God was to die. And the Lord says to him: peace. Do not be afraid. You are not going to die. And Gideon built an altar there and called it The Lord Is Peace. The peace is not the absence of the battle that is coming. The peace is the presence of the God who has already declared the outcome. Shalom — the comprehensive, multi-dimensional wellbeing of a person who is in right relationship with God — declared into the middle of a situation that had every external reason for fear.

Psalm 23:1 gives us Jehovah Rohi — the Lord is my Shepherd — in the most compressed and most complete devotional poem in Scripture. The Lord is my shepherd, I lack nothing. The claim of Psalm 23 is not that the life of the one being shepherded is free from difficulty — the psalm contains the valley of the shadow of death and the table prepared in the presence of enemies. The claim is that in the middle of every difficulty, the shepherd is present, the rod and the staff are comforting, the oil is applied, the cup overflows. The shepherding does not remove the valley. It accompanies the person through it.

These names are not ancient relics. They are the living testimony of the character of a God who has not changed since He provided on the mountain, healed the bitter waters, led the battle, declared peace to the terrified, and shepherded the psalmist through the valley. The same God who was Jehovah Jireh for Abraham is Jehovah Jireh for the person facing financial impossibility today. The same God who was Jehovah Rapha for Israel at Marah is Jehovah Rapha for the person lying in the hospital bed today. The names are the track record. And the track record is perfect.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to You today by the names You have given Yourself in the crises of the people who needed You — Jehovah Jireh, the God who provided when provision was impossible. Jehovah Rapha, the God who healed when healing was impossible. Jehovah Nissi, the God who won the battle when victory was impossible. Jehovah Shalom, the God who declared peace into situations that had every external reason for fear. Jehovah Rohi, the God who shepherds through the valley rather than removing it.

I bring You my specific crisis today and I address You by the name that corresponds to it — the name that was given in a situation like mine, by a person who was where I am, who found You to be exactly what the name declares. You have not changed. What You were for them, You are for me.

The Lord is my shepherd. I lack nothing. Let me live today like that is true.

## Daily Practices

- Read Exodus 17:8–15 — the battle with the Amalekites and the naming of Jehovah Nissi. Note the specific detail of Moses's arms and the role of Aaron and Hur. Ask: who are the Aaron and Hur in my life — the people who hold up my arms when my strength fails? And who am I holding up in the same way?
- Read Genesis 22:1–14 — the binding of Isaac and the naming of Jehovah Jireh. Note verse 14: on the mountain of the Lord it will be provided. The provision was on the mountain — at the highest point of the crisis, at the moment of maximum cost. Ask: is there a mountain I am currently on where the provision has not yet appeared but where I am being called to trust that it will?
- Journal: "Which name of God speaks most directly to my current situation? Am I in need of Jehovah Jireh's provision, Jehovah Rapha's healing, Jehovah Shalom's peace, Jehovah Nissi's victory, or Jehovah Rohi's shepherding? Address God by that name today and bring your specific need to the specific dimension of His character it requires."
- Read Psalm 23 aloud — slowly, as a personal declaration rather than a recitation. Let each phrase land specifically: He makes me lie down, He leads me, He restores my soul, He guides me, I will fear no evil, You are with me, You prepare a table, You anoint my head, surely goodness and love will follow me. Which phrase most needs to be received today as a present reality rather than a general truth?
