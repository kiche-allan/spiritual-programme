## Revelation

If you were to survey the New Testament and count every time Jesus spoke about hell, you would find something that surprises most modern Christians: Jesus spoke about hell more than He spoke about heaven. More than He spoke about money. More than He spoke about prayer. He used the word Gehenna — the Greek transliteration of the Hebrew for the valley of Hinnom, a place outside Jerusalem where fire burned constantly — eleven times in the Gospels. He described it as a place of weeping and gnashing of teeth, a place of outer darkness, a place where the fire is not quenched and the worm does not die. He warned about it with an urgency and a frequency that is impossible to explain away as peripheral or incidental.

This matters because the church has largely stopped talking about hell. Not because the doctrine has been disproven — it has not — but because it is uncomfortable, because it offends the sensibilities of a culture that has decided that a loving God would not permit such a thing, and because the people in the pews are generally more interested in hearing about the blessings available in this life than the consequences awaiting in the next. The result is that millions of people who attend church regularly have never been given a serious, sober, theologically honest account of what Jesus said was at stake — and have therefore never made a decision about their own lives that was fully informed by the actual reality Jesus described.

The dread of hellfire is not a medieval manipulation tactic. It is not the invention of power-hungry clerics trying to control populations through fear. It is the specific, urgent, repeated warning of the Son of God — the One who created every human being He was warning, who loved them enough to become flesh and die for them, and who loved them too much to stay silent about what awaited those who rejected the rescue He was offering. Matthew 10:28 reframes the entire orientation of our fear. We spend our lives afraid of the things that can harm us in this world — illness, financial ruin, rejection, failure, death. Jesus says: reorient your fear. The things that can destroy your body are not your greatest threat. Be afraid of the One who can destroy both soul and body in hell. Not in a paralysing, performance-driven terror — but in the reverential, life-reorienting recognition that eternity is real, that its outcomes are permanent, and that the decisions you make in this life have consequences that extend infinitely beyond it.

Luke 16 gives us the most detailed portrait of hell in the Gospels — the rich man and Lazarus. The rich man is in torment. He is conscious. He remembers his brothers. He feels the agony. And there is a great chasm fixed between where he is and where Lazarus is, and no one can cross it. This is not a parable designed to terrify — it is a parable designed to inform. To give the listener a picture detailed enough to make the choice that is still available to them while there is still time to make it.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to You this morning with the weight of what Your Son said about eternity pressing on me in a new way. I do not come in paralysing fear — but in the sober, reverential recognition that eternity is real, that its outcomes are permanent, and that the decisions being made every day by the people around me have consequences that extend infinitely beyond what any of us can see right now.

I receive the warning of hell not as a weapon of manipulation but as the most serious expression of love available — the warning given by someone who knows what is coming and refuses to let those He loves walk into it without being told. Let that same love move in me. Let the urgency that caused Jesus to speak about this more than any other subject become an urgency in my own heart — for my own life, and for the people around me who have not yet made the decision that changes everything.

I choose today to fear the right things. Not the things that can harm my body. The things that matter to eternity.

## Daily Practices

- Read Luke 16:19–31 in full — the rich man and Lazarus. Read it slowly, as if for the first time. Note every specific detail: the consciousness, the memory, the torment, the chasm, the impossibility of crossing it. Let the specificity of the portrait inform your understanding rather than sanitising it into abstraction.
- Read Matthew 5:22, 5:29–30, 10:28, 18:9, 23:33 — five of Jesus's direct references to hell. Note that in every case He is speaking to religious people, to His disciples, to the crowds — not primarily to the most obviously wicked. Ask: what was He trying to do with these words?
- Journal: "What does the reality of hell change about the way I should be living? What does it change about the urgency I bring to my own faith, and to the people around me who do not yet know Jesus?" Let the answer be honest and specific.
- Read 2 Peter 3:9 — God is not willing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance. The God who warns about hell is simultaneously the God who is doing everything possible to ensure that no one goes there. Hold both truths together today.
