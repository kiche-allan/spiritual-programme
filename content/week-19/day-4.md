## Revelation

The cross is not only the instrument of salvation. It is the pattern of the Christian life. This is one of the most important and most consistently underemphasised truths in the New Testament — that the shape of the life that Christ produces in the person who follows Him is not the shape of increasing power, comfort, and success, but the shape of the cross. Descent before ascent. Death before resurrection. Losing before finding. Emptying before filling. This pattern — which the theologians have named cruciformity, the cross-shaped formation of the believer — runs through every significant New Testament letter and is stated most directly by Jesus Himself in Luke 9:23: whoever wants to save their life will lose it, but whoever loses their life for me will save it.

Galatians 2:20 is the most compressed expression of what cruciformity actually means: I have been crucified with Christ and I no longer live, but Christ lives in me. Paul is not using metaphor loosely. He is describing a genuine spiritual reality — that at the moment of conversion, the old self that was under the power of sin and death was co-crucified with Christ. Not reformed. Not improved. Not educated into better behaviour. Crucified — which means the old life, the life of self-sovereignty, the life organised around personal desire and personal agenda and personal glory, died. And what lives in the body now is not the old life resurrected. It is Christ Himself, living in the person who has been emptied of the old life that used to occupy the space.

The daily nature of this is what Luke 9:23 insists on: take up your cross daily. Not once, at the moment of conversion. Not at the annual retreat or the significant spiritual milestone. Daily — on the ordinary Tuesday, in the unremarkable circumstance, in the moment when the old self would have reasserted its agenda and the cross-shaped life requires it to be denied again. The cross is not a one-time event in the believer's history. It is the shape of the believer's ongoing existence — the daily, repeated, sometimes costly refusal of the self-life in favour of the Christ-life that the death of the self makes possible.

Philippians 3:10–11 gives us Paul's own aching expression of what cruciformity looks like from the inside: I want to know Christ — yes, to know the power of his resurrection and participation in his sufferings, becoming like him in his death, and so, somehow, attaining to the resurrection from the dead. The somehow is one of the most honest words in the entire Pauline corpus — the acknowledgment that the path from the participation in the sufferings to the attainment of the resurrection runs through territory that cannot be fully mapped in advance, that requires a trust in the destination that exceeds the available visibility of the route. The cruciform life is not a life that always knows where the cross is taking it. It is a life that trusts the One who was crucified and rose — and follows the pattern of His death because it knows the pattern ends in His resurrection.

What cruciformity produces is the character that no other process can produce. The patience that comes from having had your agenda stripped away and discovering that God's agenda is better. The humility that comes from having had your self-sufficiency exposed and discovering that the sufficiency of Christ is more than adequate. The love that comes from having had the self-centred life die and discovering that the Christ-life that replaces it is oriented outward rather than inward. The cross does not produce these things as side effects. It produces them as the direct result of the death that it requires — because it is only when the old life has genuinely died that the new life has genuine room to grow.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the cross as the pattern of my life — not only the instrument of my salvation but the shape of my ongoing existence. I take up my cross today — not the dramatic cross of martyrdom, but the daily cross of the denied self, the surrendered agenda, the preference released for the sake of another, the moment when the old life would have asserted itself and instead I choose the death that produces Your life in me.

I have been crucified with Christ. I no longer live. But Christ lives in me. Let that be the most practically relevant sentence about my day — the truth that governs every decision, every relationship, every moment where the self-life wants to reclaim the space that the cross claimed for Christ.

And let the somehow of Philippians 3:11 be enough for the road ahead. I do not need to see the whole route. I need to know the destination. Resurrection follows crucifixion. Always.

## Daily Practices

- Read Philippians 2:1–11 in full — the kenosis passage, the hymn of the self-emptying Christ. Note the sequence: He emptied Himself, He humbled Himself, He became obedient to death — therefore God exalted Him. The exaltation follows the emptying. The ascent follows the descent. The name above every name follows the death on a cross. Ask: where in my life is God calling me to the emptying that precedes the exaltation?
- Read Romans 6:1–14 — the theology of co-crucifixion and co-resurrection with Christ. Note verse 11: count yourselves dead to sin but alive to God in Christ Jesus. The counting is an act of faith — the deliberate reckoning of what is spiritually true as the basis for how you live practically. Ask: what would it look like to count myself dead to the specific sin or self-pattern that is currently most active in me?
- Journal: "What is the specific shape of the cross in my life right now — the specific thing I am being asked to deny, release, or surrender? What is the resurrection that the crucifixion of that thing would make possible?" Write the honest answer.
- Identify one specific moment today where you will deliberately choose the cross-shaped response rather than the self-shaped one — a moment of genuine self-denial, however small, in the name of the Christ who denied Himself completely for your sake. Let it be specific rather than general.
