## Revelation

Myles Munroe taught that purpose is discovered, not invented. You do not sit down one day and decide what your purpose will be, in the same way that you do not decide the colour of your eyes or the language your heart speaks when it is most alive. Purpose is already inside you — it has been there since before you were born — and the work of your life is not to construct it but to uncover it. And one of the most reliable compasses for that uncovering is this: pay attention to what moves you without your permission.

The Samaritan did not consult a purpose statement before stopping on the road to Jericho. When he saw the man bleeding in the dust, something happened in him that the Greek describes as splanchnizomai — a gut-level, visceral compassion that originates not in the mind but in the deepest part of a person. This is the same word used repeatedly in the Gospels to describe what Jesus felt when He encountered the suffering crowds, the leper, the widow at Nain. It is not sentiment. It is not pity from a comfortable distance. It is the kind of response that bypasses your rational calculus and moves you to action before you have decided to move. The Samaritan did not debate the geopolitical implications of helping a Jew. He moved. The movement itself was the clue.

What moves you like this? What suffering in the world produces in you a response that does not wait for your permission — an anger at injustice, a tenderness toward a specific kind of person, an inability to ignore a particular kind of need? These are not random emotional responses. They are compass readings. Munroe said your natural passions and the things that break your heart are clues to your calling. The things you love doing effortlessly, the needs you cannot walk past without feeling something, the people whose stories you cannot hear without being moved — these are the fingerprints of the purpose that was placed in you before you were born, pressing outward, trying to get your attention.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I confess that I have sometimes dismissed my deepest responses as emotional reactions rather than purposeful signals. I have been trained by a world that values the rational over the intuitive, the calculated over the compassionate — and so I have sometimes talked myself out of the very movements that were pointing me toward my calling.

Today I ask You to help me pay attention. Not to manufacture passion, not to perform compassion for an audience, but to notice genuinely what moves me without my permission. Where do I feel the splanchnizomai — the gut-level response that I did not choose and cannot fully explain? Where do I find myself caring about something more than seems reasonable? Those places are clues. Let me take them seriously.

Give me the courage of the Samaritan — to stop when something in me says stop, to act when something in me says act, and to trust that the movement itself is the beginning of the discovery.

## Daily Practices

- Read Luke 10:25–37 in full — the complete parable including the lawyer's question that prompted it. Note that Jesus does not answer "who is my neighbour?" directly. He tells a story and then asks: which of the three was a neighbour? The answer is found in the doing, not in the defining.
- Journal: "What suffering in the world produces in me a response I did not choose — anger, grief, tenderness, urgency? What people, what problems, what injustices am I unable to remain neutral about?" Write the list without evaluating whether it is spiritual enough or practical enough.
- Identify one person in your life this week who is the equivalent of the man on the road — not necessarily physically injured, but in need of someone to stop. What would it look like to be the Samaritan in their specific situation?
- Read Micah 6:8 and sit with the three requirements: act justly, love mercy, walk humbly. Which of the three comes most naturally to you? Which is hardest? The one that comes most naturally is often the one closest to your purpose.
