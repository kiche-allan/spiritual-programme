## Revelation

Theosis is the most ambitious claim the Christian faith makes about the destination of the human person — and it is also the most consistently underdeveloped doctrine in Western Christianity. The Eastern Orthodox tradition has always kept it at the centre of its understanding of salvation. The Western tradition has tended to reduce salvation to justification — the forgiveness of sins and the securing of heaven — without fully developing what justification is the beginning of. Theosis is the full answer: the process by which the human person, through genuine participation in Christ, becomes genuinely, increasingly, permanently like God — not in essence, not in omnipotence or omniscience or any of the incommunicable attributes — but in character, in love, in holiness, in the relational and moral qualities that God has always intended to share with the creatures He made in His image.

2 Peter 1:4 is the most explicit statement of theosis in the New Testament: through these very great and precious promises you may participate in the divine nature. Participate — the Greek is koinonos, which means sharer, partner, one who has a genuine stake in and genuine experience of the thing being participated in. Not observe from a distance. Not intellectually affirm. Participate — actually, genuinely, experientially share in. And what is being shared is the divine nature — the character, the love, the holiness, the inner life of God Himself, made available to the creature through the promises of the gospel. This is what the Christian life is moving toward. Not merely forgiven sinners who have been granted access to heaven. Participants in the divine nature — people who have been so thoroughly transformed by genuine participation in Christ that what they are, by the completion of the process, genuinely reflects who God is.

2 Corinthians 3:18 describes the process: we all, who with unveiled faces contemplate the Lord's glory, are being transformed into his image with ever-increasing glory. The transformation is by contemplation — by the sustained, attentive, unveiled gazing at the glory of the Lord that produces in the gazer a gradual conformity to what they are gazing at. This is one of the most important principles in the spiritual life: you become what you behold. The person who is consistently, attentively, genuinely gazing at the glory of Christ — in prayer, in Scripture, in worship, in the contemplative attention that the spiritual disciplines are designed to produce — is being transformed into His image by the gazing itself. Not by effort. By exposure. The light that falls on the person who stands in it changes them in the same way that sunlight changes a plant that is consistently turned toward it.

The transformation is ever-increasing — not in the sense of constant emotional intensity, but in the sense of a direction that is consistently moving from less glory to more, from partial conformity to increasing conformity, from the partial reflection of the divine image that every human being bears to the full, complete, unobstructed reflection that will be the final state of every person who has been genuinely transformed by the gazing. 1 John 3:2 gives us the destination of the ever-increasing: we shall be like Him, for we shall see Him as He is. The seeing and the being like are not sequential — the seeing produces the being like, in the same way that the contemplation of 2 Corinthians 3:18 produces the transformation. But the final seeing — the face-to-face vision of God that has been promised to those who are pure in heart in Matthew 5:8 — produces the final likeness. We shall be like Him.

This is the most radical claim available in the Christian vocabulary. And it is not a marginal doctrine invented by medieval mystics. It is the explicit testimony of 2 Peter, Paul, and John — the three greatest theologians of the New Testament, all pointing to the same destination. The human person, made in the image of God, marred by sin, restored by Christ, and genuinely participating in the divine nature through the gospel — that person is on their way to becoming like God. Not in omnipotence or omniscience. In love. In holiness. In the character that 1 Corinthians 13 describes, in the fruit that Galatians 5 names, in the glory that 2 Corinthians 3:18 says is already being produced by the contemplation that the Spirit makes possible.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the most ambitious claim Your gospel makes about where my life is going — not merely forgiven and admitted to heaven, but genuinely, increasingly, permanently participating in Your divine nature. The image in which I was made is being restored. The likeness to Christ is being formed. The ever-increasing glory is being produced by the gazing that Your Spirit enables.

Let me gaze today with an unveiled face — without the veil of self-preoccupation, without the veil of distraction, without the veil of the unconfessed sin that obscures the vision. Let me contemplate the Lord's glory in whatever form it presents itself today — in Scripture, in prayer, in the beauty that points beyond itself, in the face of another person who bears Your image. And let the gazing do what the gazing has always done — produce in me the likeness of the One I am looking at.

We shall be like Him. That is enough.

## Daily Practices

- Read 2 Peter 1:1–11 in full. Note the specific list of qualities Peter says should be added to faith: goodness, knowledge, self-control, perseverance, godliness, mutual affection, love. Ask: is this list a description of what I am currently adding — the progressive, intentional cultivation of the divine character in my daily life?
- Read 2 Corinthians 3:7–18 in full — the full contrast between the fading glory of Moses's face and the ever-increasing glory of the believer who contemplates the Lord with an unveiled face. Ask: what are the veils that are currently preventing me from gazing at the glory of the Lord with full, unobstructed attention?
- Journal: "What does it mean for my daily life that I am a participant in the divine nature — not eventually, but now, through the promises of the gospel? What specific aspect of the divine character am I most aware of growing in me? And what is most absent?" Write the honest answer.
- Practise theosis today in its most concrete form: choose one quality of the divine character — love, patience, kindness, generosity — and look for one specific opportunity to express it. Not as a performance of virtue but as the genuine, Spirit-enabled expression of a nature that is genuinely becoming more like God's.
