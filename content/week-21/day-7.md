## Revelation

Maranatha. It is one of the oldest words in the Christian vocabulary — an Aramaic phrase that appears in 1 Corinthians 16:22 and in the Didache, the earliest Christian document outside the New Testament, where it appears in the context of the Lord's Supper. It means either the Lord has come or come, Lord — the ambiguity is deliberate and theological: the One who came is the One who is coming, and the cry that welcomes His past coming is the same cry that longs for His future coming. Maranatha. Come, Lord Jesus.

The early church prayed this prayer constantly. Not because they were morbid or escapist or unengaged with the world they were living in. Because they understood that the most important event in history had happened — the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead — and that its implications were so radical that the only appropriate response was to long for the full expression of those implications. The resurrection had declared that death was defeated. But death was still present. The resurrection had declared that the new creation had begun. But the old creation was still groaning. The resurrection had declared that the kingdom of God was at hand. But the kingdoms of this world were still very much present. The tension between the already and the not yet — between the kingdom inaugurated and the kingdom consummated — produced in the early church a sustained, earnest, expectant longing for the return of the One whose return would resolve the tension permanently.

Revelation 22:20 gives us the final exchange of the entire Bible — the last words before the closing benediction. He who testifies to these things says: yes, I am coming soon. And the response of the church: Amen. Come, Lord Jesus. The Bible ends with a promise and a prayer. The promise is His: I am coming soon. The prayer is the church's: come. The whole Bible — from the garden of Genesis to the city of Revelation, from the first creation to the new creation, from the first Adam to the last — ends with the church crying out for what has not yet fully arrived, trusting the promise of the One who is faithful, and saying amen to the coming that has not yet come.

1 Thessalonians 4:16–17 gives us the clearest description of what the return will look like in the New Testament: the Lord Himself will come down from heaven with a loud command, with the voice of the archangel and with the trumpet call of God. The dead in Christ will rise first. Then we who are still alive and are left will be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air. And so we will be with the Lord forever. The most important phrase in this passage is the last one. Not the mechanics of the event — the trumpet, the archangel, the clouds — but the destination: we will be with the Lord forever. The return is not primarily about the rescue from the world. It is about the reunion with the Person. We will be with the Lord forever. The communion that was begun in conversion, deepened through the Spirit's fellowship, expressed in prayer and worship and the community of the church — that communion will be perfected and permanent and unmediated and forever.

Titus 2:13 gives us the title that places the return in its proper frame: the blessed hope — the appearing of the glory of our great God and Saviour, Jesus Christ. Blessed hope. Not dreadful certainty, not grim inevitability, not the end of history as a neutral fact to be theologically processed. Blessed hope — the most positive, most energising, most forward-looking description available. The same Jesus who walked the roads of Galilee, who sat with the disciples at the table, who was crucified and buried and raised, who ascended into the cloud and will return in the same way — that Jesus is appearing. Not a principle or a force or a theological abstraction. A Person. The Person. Appearing in glory.

The recovery of the Maranatha cry changes five things in the Christian life. It changes how you pray — because prayer addressed to the One who is coming soon has a different quality from prayer addressed to a historical figure. It changes how you endure — because the suffering of Romans 8:17 is placed within the context of a return that will reveal a glory that outweighs it. It changes how you serve — because the labour in the Lord is not in vain, and the not in vain is grounded in the certainty of the One who is coming to complete what He has begun. It changes how you hold the things of this world — because the person who is genuinely expecting the return holds everything temporal with the lightness of someone who knows what is coming. And it changes how you love the people around you — because the return of Christ is the deadline by which every person will have made or not made the decision that Revelation 22:17 describes as the final invitation: whoever is thirsty, let him come; and whoever wishes, let him take the free gift of the water of life.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I close this week with the oldest prayer of the church and the final prayer of the Bible: Maranatha. Come, Lord Jesus. I receive today the blessed hope — the appearing of the glory of the One who promised He is coming soon, whose soon is calibrated to a wisdom that exceeds my timeline, whose faithfulness in every promise He has already kept is the guarantee of the one that is still outstanding.

Let the Maranatha cry change the quality of this day. Let it change how I pray — with the earnestness of someone addressing the One who is actually coming. Let it change how I endure — with the steadiness of someone who knows the suffering is not the last word. Let it change how I love the people around me — with the urgency of someone who understands the stakes and the timeline.

Yes, I am coming soon. Amen. Come, Lord Jesus.

## Daily Practices

- Read Revelation 22:6–21 in full — the closing passage of the entire Bible. Note how many times the promise I am coming soon is repeated: verses 7, 12, and 20. Ask: what does the triple repetition of this promise in the closing verses of Scripture say about its importance as a motivating truth for the Christian life? And what does it say about how quickly the church has allowed it to fade from its consciousness?
- Read 1 Thessalonians 4:13–5:11 in full — Paul's complete teaching on the return of Christ and its practical implications. Note that Paul moves immediately from the theology of the return in chapter 4 to the practical implications in 5:1–11: be alert, be self-controlled, encourage one another, build each other up. Ask: how does the expectation of the return shape the practical posture Paul is describing?
- Review the entire week — the fear of the Lord, the sovereignty of God, lament, the new creation, the communion of the Spirit, suffering and glory, and Maranatha — and write one sentence for each day that captures what it produced in you. Seven sentences. Then ask: what is the single thread running through all seven? Write it. Let it be the prayer you carry into the week ahead.
- Pray the Maranatha prayer today — out loud, with full intention, as the genuine longing of a person who has understood what the return means. Not a rote repetition. The genuine cry of a church that has been formed by the truths of this week and is now facing forward, toward the One who is coming, saying: yes. Come. We are ready. Come, Lord Jesus.
