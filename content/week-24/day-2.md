## Revelation

Job's friends are famous for being wrong. The long middle section of the book of Job — chapters 4 through 37 — is a sustained theological conversation in which Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar offer increasingly sophisticated and increasingly incorrect explanations for Job's suffering, and in which God eventually tells them that they have not spoken what is right about Him the way His servant Job has. Their theology failed. Their explanations were inadequate. Their attempts to account for Job's suffering within the framework of a cause-and-effect moral universe produced some of the most confident and most incorrect religious speech in the entire biblical record.

But before they opened their mouths, they did something that the rest of the book obscures — something that deserves its own reading, its own reflection, and its own recovery as a model for how to be with people who are suffering. Job 2:11–13 tells us that they came, that they wept, that they tore their robes, that they sprinkled dust on their heads, and then — they sat down on the ground with him for seven days and seven nights. No one said a word to him, because they saw how great his suffering was. Seven days. Of silence. Of presence. Of sitting on the ground with a man who had lost everything and was sitting in the ash heap scraping his sores with broken pottery. They did not come with answers. They came with themselves. And for seven days, the coming with themselves was the most appropriate, the most helpful, and the most genuinely comforting thing they could have done.

They became unhelpful the moment they started talking. Every word they subsequently spoke, however theologically sophisticated, moved them further from Job and closer to their own need to make sense of what they were witnessing. The talking was for them — it was the management of their own discomfort at the unanswerable, the attempt to bring the inexplicable within the reach of their explanatory framework. The sitting was for Job. And the sitting was harder than the talking, because the talking filled the silence with something and the sitting required them to remain in the silence with nothing except their presence.

Romans 12:15 gives us the simplest and most demanding instruction in the Pauline ethics of community: rejoice with those who rejoice; mourn with those who mourn. The second half is the harder half. Rejoicing with the rejoicing requires some suppression of competitive instinct and genuine love — but it moves in the direction of something pleasant. Mourning with the mourning requires the deliberate, voluntary, costly choice to enter a place of pain that is not yours, to sit in a grief that you do not have to sit in, to remain present in the difficult emotion of another person without the rescue of fixing or explaining or spiritually reframing it. Mourn with those who mourn. Not comfort those who mourn — which would at least give you something to do. Mourn with them. Be in the grief alongside them. Let it cost you something.

John 11:35 — Jesus wept — is the shortest verse in the Bible and one of the most theologically significant. Jesus knew He was about to raise Lazarus. He already knew the outcome of the situation before He arrived. And He wept anyway. Not because He had lost hope — He had specifically said this sickness will not end in death. But because the people He loved were suffering, and the suffering of the people He loved moved Him. He did not skip to the solution. He entered the grief first. He wept with those who were weeping before He acted on behalf of those who were weeping. The ministry of presence came before the miracle. The mourning with the mourners preceded the raising of the dead.

This is the God who is with us in our suffering — not the God who observes from a safe theological distance, not the God who skips immediately to the solution without entering the pain, but the God who weeps before He acts, who sits in the ash heap before He speaks, who is acquainted with grief not only in the sense of having encountered it but in the sense of having entered it. And because He is this kind of God, the ministry of presence that He models and calls His people to is not a second-rate substitute for the more impressive ministry of the miraculous solution. It is the most accurate representation of who He is available to the person who is currently in the ash heap.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the ministry of presence — the most demanding and the most God-like form of ministry available to me. Teach me to sit in the ash heap with the people I love who are suffering — to stay in the silence when the talking would be for my comfort rather than theirs, to mourn with those who are mourning rather than rushing to the reframing that would make me more comfortable but would not serve them.

Thank You that You are this kind of God — the God who wept at the tomb of His friend before raising him, who enters the grief before resolving it, who is acquainted with sorrow from the inside rather than the outside. Let me represent You accurately today to the people in my life who are in pain — not with impressive answers but with genuine, costly, sustained presence.

Who in my life today needs me to sit on the ground with them and say nothing?

## Daily Practices

- Read Job 2:11–13 again — slowly, meditatively, focusing specifically on the seven days of silence. Ask: what was it costing Job's friends to sit in silence for seven days? What were they resisting? What would they have preferred to do? And what made the seven days of silence, despite its cost, the most genuinely helpful thing they did in the entire book?
- Read John 11:1–44 in full — the complete Lazarus account. Note that Jesus deliberately delayed His arrival by two days after hearing that Lazarus was sick. He could have prevented the death. He chose not to — and then He wept at the tomb. Ask: what does the weeping of the One who chose not to prevent the death say about the nature of divine empathy? How does this change how you understand God's presence in your own suffering?
- Journal: "Who in my life right now is in the ash heap — suffering in a way that my words cannot fix and my presence can reach? Have I been avoiding them because the sitting is harder than the talking, because their pain makes me uncomfortable, because I do not know what to say? What would it look like to go and simply be there — without an agenda, without a prepared speech, without the need to fix or explain?" Write the honest reckoning.
- Reach out to one person in your life who is currently suffering — not with a theological explanation, not with practical advice, not even with a carefully worded message of encouragement. Simply be present. A visit if possible. A phone call if not. The message: I am here. I am not going anywhere. I do not have answers. I am with you. Let that be enough, and trust that it is more than enough.
