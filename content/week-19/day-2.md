## Revelation

There is a name for the experience that no one in the church talks about enough: Deus Absconditus — the hidden God. It is the Latin theological term for the seasons when God seems absent — when prayer feels like speaking into an empty room, when the Scripture feels like words on paper rather than the living voice of Someone who is there, when the warmth and nearness of previous seasons has given way to a grey, flat silence that no amount of spiritual effort seems capable of penetrating. The songs do not lift you. The prayers do not connect. The quiet is the loudest thing available. And the question that every believer in this season eventually asks — is something wrong with me, or has God gone somewhere? — is one of the most important questions in the spiritual life.

Isaiah 45:15 gives us the most honest theological statement about this experience available in the Old Testament: truly You are a God who has been hiding Himself, the God and Saviour of Israel. This is not the complaint of a backslidden believer. It is the recognition of a prophet — someone with genuine, sustained, documented experience of God's presence — that the hiddenness is a real attribute of God, not a symptom of inadequate faith. God hides Himself. Deliberately. Seasonally. For reasons that are not always immediately available to the person in the season of concealment.

The cloud that led Israel through the wilderness is the most vivid image of this in the Old Testament. Exodus 13:21 says the Lord went ahead of them in a pillar of cloud by day and a pillar of fire by night. The presence that guided them was simultaneously the presence that concealed. You cannot see through a cloud. The pillar that said I am here was also the pillar that said you cannot see Me clearly from where you are standing. The presence and the concealment were the same thing, experienced differently depending on what you were looking for. Israel experienced the cloud as guidance when they were following it and as frustrating obscurity when they wanted to see what was beyond it. The cloud did not change. Their relationship to it changed.

Psalm 22 gives us the most searing articulation of the experience of divine hiddenness — and its authority comes from the fact that it was prayed by Jesus Himself from the cross. My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? The Son of God, in the most important moment in the history of the universe, experienced the hiddenness of God in its most absolute form. The Father turned His face away. The felt absence was complete and the darkness was literal. And yet the same psalm that opens with why have you forsaken me closes thirty-one verses later with for he has not despised or scorned the suffering of the afflicted one; he has not hidden his face from him but has listened to his cry for help. The hiddenness and the listening were simultaneous. The felt absence and the actual presence were coexisting realities. The cross looked, from the outside, like the total abandonment of the Son by the Father — and it was simultaneously the most intimate cooperation in the Trinity's eternal history.

What the hiddenness is producing is the key. The mystics who wrote most clearly about this — John of the Cross, Thomas à Kempis, Madame Guyon — consistently describe the dark night of the soul not as the absence of God but as the stripping away of the consolations of God so that the person learns to love God for who He is rather than for what He provides. The felt presence, the emotional warmth, the sense of God near — these are gifts, but they are not God Himself. The person who can only love God when the gifts are present is not yet loving God. The person who continues in faithfulness through the season when the gifts have been withheld — who keeps praying when the prayer does not connect, who keeps reading when the text feels flat, who keeps coming when the coming feels unrewarded — that person is learning a love that does not depend on the feeling of the love returned. And that love is far more durable, far more genuinely God-centred, and far more capable of sustaining the believer through every season than the love that only flourishes in the warmth.

Deuteronomy 29:29 holds the tension with the most theologically honest sentence available: the secret things belong to the Lord our God, but the things revealed belong to us and to our children forever. There are things God has not revealed. This is not a failure of communication. It is the deliberate reservation of a wisdom that exceeds what the creature can currently receive — the same wisdom that answers Job's sustained lament not with an explanation but with a question: where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? The hiddenness is not a gap in God's knowledge or a failure in His communication. It is the appropriate boundary between the infinite and the finite, maintained not to frustrate the creature but to protect it from what it is not yet equipped to receive.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I bring to You today the season of hiddenness — the silence that has been louder than the answers, the prayers that have felt unanswered, the distance that has felt like absence. I bring it honestly, the way Psalm 22 brings it — not pretending it is comfortable, not performing a faith I do not feel, but addressing You directly with the reality that You have felt hidden. And I receive today the truth that Your hiddenness is not Your absence. The cloud that concealed You from Israel was also the cloud that was leading them. The darkness of Holy Saturday was also the silence of a tomb about to be vacated.

Let the hiddenness do what it was designed to do — strip away my dependence on the feeling of Your presence until what remains is a love for You that does not require the feeling to sustain it. You are the God who hides Himself. You are also the God and Saviour of Israel. Both things are true simultaneously. Let me hold both today.

## Daily Practices

- Read Job 23:1–10 — the most sustained description of divine hiddenness in the poetic literature. Note Job's response: if I go to the east, he is not there; if I go to the west, I do not find him. But he knows the way that I take; when he has tested me, I will come forth as gold. The hiddenness and the knowing exist simultaneously. Ask: what would it mean for me to hold both of those realities at once?
- Read 1 Kings 19:9–13 — Elijah at Horeb, the still small voice after the wind and earthquake and fire. Note that God was not in the dramatic manifestations. He was in the sound of a gentle whisper. Ask: have I been so focused on the dramatic presence that I have missed the gentle whisper that has been present all along?
- Journal: "What has the current season of hiddenness been producing in me — even if what it has been producing has been invisible and painful? What qualities of character, depth of faith, or independence from feeling have been growing underground during the silence?" Write honestly.
- Read John 11:1–6 and 11:17–44 — Lazarus's death and resurrection. Note verse 6: when Jesus heard that Lazarus was sick, he stayed where he was two more days. The delay was deliberate. The hiddenness had a purpose. And the purpose exceeded anything that a quicker response would have produced. Ask: is there a Lazarus situation in my life where the delay is deliberate and the purpose exceeds what I can currently see?
