## Revelation

Who are you trying to please?

It is one of the most clarifying questions in the Christian life — because the honest answer reveals the actual governing authority of your decisions, your speech, your silence, and your willingness or unwillingness to say the things that need to be said in the moments when saying them will cost you something.

Paul asks it with the sharpness of a man who has already decided the answer for himself: Am I now trying to win the approval of human beings, or of God? The or is the key word. It is not a question about how to balance the two — how to keep people reasonably happy while also generally honouring God, how to say enough truth that you feel honest while saying it carefully enough that no one is significantly uncomfortable. Paul is not describing a spectrum. He is describing a binary. You are either seeking the approval of human beings or you are seeking the approval of God. The person who is genuinely committed to the second cannot simultaneously be governed by the first — because there will always be moments when what God requires and what the audience wants are not the same thing. And in those moments you will serve whoever you have decided holds the final authority over your words and your life.

The pull toward people-pleasing is not primarily a character flaw. It is a theological error — the practical expression of a misbelief about where safety and significance and approval actually come from. Proverbs 29:25 names it for what it is: the fear of man is a snare. Not a weakness. A snare — a trap designed to catch you and hold you in a position where you cannot move in the direction God is calling you. The person trapped by the fear of man will say what the audience wants to hear when the audience is watching, and will stay silent when the silence is costly, and will water down the truth when the undiluted version might produce discomfort — and will call all of it pastoral sensitivity when it is actually the snare of the approval of human beings dressed in spiritual language.

The consequences of this snare go beyond the personal. Paul writes that God will hold many pastors and churches responsible for changing the message of the Gospel because they sought the affirmation of the culture over the approval of God. The adjustment of the message of holiness and righteousness to seek cultural acceptability — the softening of what the Scripture says about sin, the redefining of what faithfulness requires, the accommodation of the surrounding culture's standards because the congregation's approval feels more immediate and more necessary than God's — is not a neutral pastoral choice. It is a betrayal of the commission.

When you must have the applause of others, you will only tell people what they want to hear. Two things happen then — and both of them are destructive. Either you stay silent when you should speak, because speaking might cost you the approval you cannot afford to lose. Or you say what you know will be received rather than what you know is true, and you call it encouragement when it is actually accommodation.

God wants your yes to be yes and your no to be no. He wants the clarity that comes from a person who has settled the question of whose approval governs them and has landed on the only answer that is finally safe: His. His approval — which is not won by performance but received by faith, confirmed by faithfulness, and expressed in the willingness to stand in the place where His truth needs to be spoken and speak it with boldness and love regardless of how the room receives it.

Heaven is applauding. Let that be enough.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to You today with the honest confession that I have been governed more than I should be by the need for the approval of the room — that I have stayed silent when I should have spoken, that I have softened what You made sharp, that I have called it wisdom when it was actually the snare of the fear of man. I repent. I choose today to seek Your approval over every other approval that is available to me. Not because I do not care about people — but because the most loving thing I can do for the people around me is to tell them the truth, and the only way to do that consistently is to be free from the need for their applause. Loosen the snare. Give me the boldness of someone who knows whose servant they are. Let heaven do the applauding.

## Daily Practices

- Read Galatians 1:1–12 in full — the complete opening of the letter in which verse 10 appears. Note that Paul is defending the gospel he preached against those who were distorting it — and that his defense is grounded entirely in the source of his gospel, which was not received from human beings but revealed by Jesus Christ (verse 12). Ask: in what specific area of my life or ministry am I currently shaping the message based on the audience rather than on the source?
- Read Acts 4:18–20 — Peter and John before the Sanhedrin. Which is right in God's eyes: to listen to you or to him? We cannot help speaking about what we have seen and heard. Note that their boldness was not disrespect — it was the natural overflow of people who had decided whose authority was final. Ask: is there a specific situation in my life where I need the Peter and John clarity — where I know what God requires and have been staying silent because of what the room might think?
- Journal: "Where am I currently seeking the applause of people more than the approval of God — in my relationships, my work, my speech, my silence? What is the specific thing I have been unwilling to say or do because of how it would be received? What would it look like to say or do it today from the freedom of someone who has already received the only approval that ultimately matters?" Write the honest answer.
- Read Matthew 10:26–33 — do not be afraid of those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Note that Jesus grounds the courage to speak in the specific knowledge of the Father's attention and care: even the hairs of your head are all numbered. The person who knows they are that known and that valued by God has the specific freedom from the fear of man that truth-telling requires. Ask: how deeply do I actually believe that I am that known and that valued — and how does that belief (or lack of it) show up in my willingness to speak truth?
