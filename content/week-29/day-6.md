## Revelation

The writer of Hebrews does not call you to a stroll. He does not call you to a comfortable, well-paced walk through the Christian life with frequent rest stops and minimal exertion. He calls you to a race — and specifically to a race that requires the two things that most distinguish serious runners from casual participants: the willingness to strip off what slows you down, and the discipline to fix your eyes on the finish line rather than on the obstacles on the track.

Throw off everything that hinders — not everything that is sinful, but everything that hinders. This is a category worth thinking carefully about, because it is wider than the category of sin. There are things in a runner's life that are not wrong in themselves but are wrong for the race. Extra weight that a person who was not running could carry perfectly well becomes a liability for the person who is trying to cover ground. The comfort that is appropriate in one context becomes the compromise that costs you in another. The relationship, the habit, the entertainment, the distraction — none of these may be sins, but they are weights. And the runner who wants to run well asks not just is this wrong, but does this slow me down?

And then the sin that so easily entangles — the specific word is euperistatos in the Greek, the sin that has a special ability to wrap itself around the legs of the runner and bring them down. Not a vague category of moral failure but the specific, recurring, personally targeted temptation that each believer knows by name — the one that has tripped you before, that knows where your weakness is, that arrives with particular force at the moments when you are most tired or most unguarded. The runner who does not deal with that specific entanglement is the runner who will trip at the worst possible moment on the track.

But the most important instruction in the passage is not about what to throw off. It is about where to look. Fixing our eyes on Jesus — the pioneer and perfecter of faith. The word fixing implies a deliberate, sustained, active orientation. Not a glance in His direction between glances at everything else. A fixed gaze — the eyes of the runner locked on the One who ran the race before them and ran it to completion, who endured the cross because He could see past it to the joy set before Him. The runner who fixes their eyes on the obstacles will be governed by the obstacles. The runner who fixes their eyes on Jesus runs differently — not because the obstacles are not real but because what they are fixed on is more real than the obstacles.

Philippians 3:13–14 gives us the posture of the seasoned runner: forgetting what is behind and straining toward what is ahead. The race is forward. The failures behind you are not the terrain you run on today. The victories behind you are not the finish line. Forgetting is the discipline of the runner who understands that looking back — whether at the stumble or the milestone — takes the eyes off what matters right now, which is the ground between here and where you are going.

Run your race. Not someone else's race — the race marked out for you, the specific course that was designed for your specific stride and your specific assignment. Run it with perseverance, because the race worth running is never short. And run it with your eyes fixed on the One who ran it first.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to You today as someone who has a race to run and does not always run it like I know I have one. Show me today what needs to be thrown off — not just the sin but the weight, the thing that is not wrong in itself but is slowing me down for the specific race I have been called to. And fix my eyes on Jesus — not on the obstacles, not on the ground I have already covered, not on the stumbles I am carrying from previous miles. On Jesus. The pioneer and perfecter. The One who ran first and finished. Let me run today with the perseverance of someone who knows the finish line is real.

## Daily Practices

- Read Hebrews 12:1–3 in full — note verse 3: consider Him who endured such opposition from sinners, so that you will not grow weary and lose heart. The antidote to losing heart mid-race is to consider Jesus — to look carefully at what He endured and how He endured it. Ask: where am I currently most in danger of growing weary and losing heart, and what does considering Jesus say to that specific place?
- Read Hebrews 11 in full — the cloud of witnesses. These are the people in the stands who have already run their portion of the race. Note their diversity — shepherds and kings, the celebrated and the forgotten, those who received the promises and those who died without seeing them. Ask: which of these runners speaks most directly to the specific challenge of the race I am running right now?
- Journal: "What is the weight — not the sin but the weight — that is currently slowing my race down most? What is the entanglement — the specific, recurring temptation that I know by name — that I need to deal with before it trips me? And what does it look like practically to fix my eyes on Jesus today rather than on the obstacles?" Write the specific, honest answer to each question.
- Read 1 Corinthians 9:24–27 — Paul's own description of his discipline as a runner. Note that he does not run aimlessly, does not box like someone beating the air. There is intentionality, direction, discipline. Ask: how intentional am I currently about the specific disciplines that keep me running well? What needs to change to run with more purpose?
