## Revelation

The Pharisees meant it as an accusation. Friend of tax collectors and sinners — spoken with the contempt that the religiously respectable reserve for the description of someone who has made themselves ritually and morally unacceptable by association. In the purity code of first-century Judaism, the person who ate with the unclean became unclean. The person who reclined at table with the sinner had chosen to identify with the sinner's defilement. To call Jesus a friend of sinners was to say: this man has disqualified himself.

Jesus did not dispute the accusation. He received it. And in receiving it without correction or qualification, He effectively wore it as a title — the most accurate single description of His mission available in the vocabulary of His opponents. He was a friend of sinners. Not a distant benefactor of sinners. Not a theological advocate for sinners. A friend — the specific, personal, socially intimate relationship of someone who chose the company of the compromised and the corrupt and the morally complicated not as a condescension but as a genuine preference for their table over the table of the respectable.

The tax collectors of first-century Palestine were not merely people who collected taxes. They were collaborators — Jews who had entered the service of the Roman occupying power, who collected more than the legal amount and kept the difference, who were regarded by their own people as traitors and extortionists. They were ceremonially unclean, socially ostracised, and religiously excluded from the synagogue. And Jesus sought them out specifically. He called Levi from the tax booth. He invited Himself to Zacchaeus's house. He told a parable in which the tax collector's prayer was received and the Pharisee's was not.

What was He doing at these tables? Mark 2:17 gives us His own explanation: it is not the healthy who need a doctor but the sick. I have not come to call the righteous but sinners. The table was the clinic. The meal was the medicine. The friendship was the treatment. Jesus ate with sinners not because He was indifferent to the sin but because the eating was the form that the healing took — the specific, embodied, relational presence of the One who could make whole, in the exact location where the brokenness was most concentrated.

Luke 19:5 gives us the most revealing detail of the Zacchaeus encounter: I must stay at your house today. The must is the same divine necessity we encountered in Luke 4:43 — the compulsion of mission, the language of something that has to happen because the nature of the One who is speaking requires it. Jesus did not pass by Zacchaeus's house as a courtesy. He stopped there because the mission required it — because the friend of sinners cannot pass the sinner in the tree without stopping, cannot walk past the lost without seeking, cannot be who He is without going to the specific table where the specific person most needs the specific friendship He offers.

What does the friend of sinners mean for the church that bears His name? It means that the community formed by the friend of sinners cannot become the community that befriends only those who are already like us. It means that the table of the church should look more like Levi's banquet and Zacchaeus's house than the dinner party of the respectable. It means that the person who is morally complicated, religiously excluded, socially ostracised, and spiritually far is not the person the church reaches at the end of a long programme of reform — they are the person Jesus went to first, stayed with longest, and defended most publicly against the criticism of the people who thought the association was beneath Him.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the title that the Pharisees meant as an accusation and Your Son wore as a mission statement: friend of sinners. Let me understand today what that friendship actually looked like — not the distant benevolence of the theologically correct toward the morally compromised, but the specific, personal, table-sharing, house-staying presence of the One who sought out the company of the people that religion had written off.

And let the church I am part of be the kind of community that looks more like Levi's banquet than the Pharisee's dinner — where the tax collector is not the project but the guest, where the person furthest from the standard is not the last to be welcomed but the first to be sought. Let me be a friend of sinners the way You are a friend of sinners. Starting today.

## Daily Practices

- Read Mark 2:13–17 in full — the calling of Levi and the dinner that followed. Note that Levi's response to being called was to throw a banquet — and that the banquet was full of tax collectors and sinners. Jesus did not call Levi away from his community. He entered it. Ask: who is the community I need to enter rather than invite to mine?
- Read Luke 7:36–50 — Jesus at the Pharisee's table when the sinful woman anoints His feet. Note the contrast between the Pharisee's treatment of Jesus and the woman's. Ask: which posture is more characteristic of my own approach to Jesus — the Pharisee's correct but cold hospitality, or the woman's extravagant, tearful, costly love?
- Journal: "Who is the specific person in my life who occupies the tax collector position — the person I have been treating as the project rather than the friend, the person whose table I have not yet been willing to sit at, the person whose company I have been avoiding because of what the association might cost me? What would it look like to be their friend the way Jesus was a friend of sinners?" Write the honest reckoning.
- Read Luke 15:1–2 — the opening frame of the three parables of lostness: the Pharisees and teachers of the law muttered that Jesus welcomes sinners and eats with them. And Jesus responded with three parables about the joy of finding what was lost. Ask: what does the joy of the shepherd, the woman, and the father say about how God feels about the person I have been treating as the project?
