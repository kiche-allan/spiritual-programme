I recently watched a short video of Dr James Tour, a chemist who has spent his career making molecules in the laboratory. His argument is simple and it made me stop. He says science is still very far from explaining the origin of life.

[[youtube:XAIOWPBvwFw]]

Let me be clear about what I am saying and what I am not. I am not claiming that a gap in science proves God. I am reporting what a working chemist says, and many of his colleagues disagree with him. But his points are worth hearing, especially for those of us who have been told that this question was settled long ago.

## The experiment everyone remembers

In 1953, Stanley Miller and Harold Urey built a closed system to imitate the early Earth. They put water vapour and gases such as methane, ammonia and hydrogen into it, and passed electric sparks through it to copy lightning. The result was exciting: it produced amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. It showed that some of the simple chemicals of life can form from non-living material under the right conditions.

[[miller-urey-diagram]]

That was a real discovery, and it created great optimism. Many people thought the origin of life would be explained within a few years. But amino acids are not a cell. It is a long way from a few building blocks to a living thing, and that is where Dr Tour presses his point.

## What he says is still missing

First, the cell is far more complex than that early optimism assumed. Even the simplest cell needs many working parts, including machinery to copy and repair its DNA.

Second, he says researchers have not managed to make these essential parts from scratch, and they have not managed to put them together into a working cell, even in a carefully controlled laboratory where clever people design every step.

Third, he argues that the problem is getting harder, not easier. Every new discovery about what happens inside a cell, such as its layers of signalling or the strange effects linked to the handedness of molecules, adds new questions that nobody knew to ask in 1953. In his view, the finish line keeps moving away. After 75 years, he says, we still do not have a convincing explanation.

He also speaks of what he calls the code problem: how the specific, working arrangement of molecules that life needs could come about in the first place.

## A fair word

Honesty matters here. Other scientists would say that the field is young, that progress has been made, and that "not yet explained" is not the same as "cannot be explained". Some also point out that the early Earth's atmosphere may have been different from the mixture Miller used. A believer does not need to hide these points. If our faith is true, it can stand beside honest questions.

## What this means for us

For me, the lesson is humility, not victory. A cell is not a simple thing, and no one has yet built one. Nobody in any laboratory in the world can switch on a living cell from dust, and yet every mother in Kenya carries one, then trillions, in a baby growing in her womb.

The Bible says it better than any lecture. "The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork" (Psalm 19:1). And David wrote, "I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made. Wonderful are your works; my soul knows it very well" (Psalm 139:14). Paul tells us that God's invisible qualities have been clearly seen in what He has made (Romans 1:20). The writer to the Hebrews adds that "by faith we understand that the universe was created by the word of God" (Hebrews 11:3).

So do not be afraid of science, and do not use it as a stick to beat others. Ask good questions, stay curious, and let the wonder of life lead you to the One who gives it. The more we learn about the cell, the more reason we have to say with David, "My soul knows it very well."
