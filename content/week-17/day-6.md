## Revelation

There is a season in the spiritual life that very few people talk about honestly because it sounds too much like a loss of faith to name it publicly. It is the season when God feels absent — when prayer feels like talking to a wall, when Scripture feels like words on a page rather than the living voice of Someone who is there, when the warmth and nearness that once characterised the relationship has given way to a grey, flat silence that no spiritual effort seems to penetrate. The worship does not lift you. The prayer does not connect. The silence is the loudest thing in the room.

This experience has a long history and a proper name. The mystics called it the dark night of the soul. The psalmists called it being forgotten, being hidden from, being forsaken. And crucially — they called it these things in prayers addressed to the God they felt had gone silent. They did not walk away. They brought the silence itself to God. Psalm 22 — the psalm Jesus quoted from the cross — opens with one of the most honest prayers in all of Scripture: my God, my God, why have you forsaken me? I cry out by day but you do not answer. This is not a failure of faith. This is faith in its most stripped-down, most honest, most costly form — the continued addressing of a God who feels absent, by a person who refuses to stop addressing Him even when the feeling of His absence is the dominant reality.

Isaiah 45:15 gives us the theological category for this experience: truly you are a God who hides Himself. God's hiddenness is not incidental to the biblical portrait of Him. It is part of it. He is a God who sometimes veils His presence — not because He has withdrawn, but because the hiddenness is doing something that the felt presence cannot do. The faith that operates in the felt presence of God is real faith. The faith that continues in the felt absence of God is a deeper faith — the faith that trusts the character of God when the experience of God is not available to confirm it.

Habakkuk 2:3 gives us the most practical instruction available for the season of silence: wait for it. The revelation awaits an appointed time. It will certainly come and will not delay — which, given that it is described as lingering, means not that it will come quickly but that when it comes it will be exactly on time, not late in any meaningful sense. The appointed time is God's time, and God's time is not calibrated by the clock we are watching. The silence is not the end of the story. It is a chapter in it — and the chapter has an appointed end, a specific moment when the silence breaks and the revelation arrives, with the precision of a God who is never late to His own appointments.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I bring to You today the silence — the season when You feel absent and prayer feels thin and the warmth I have known in other seasons has been replaced by a quiet that I cannot pray my way out of. I bring it honestly, the way the psalmist brought it — not pretending it is not there, not performing a faith I do not currently feel, but addressing You directly with the reality that You feel hidden right now.

I receive today that You are a God who hides Himself — that the hiddenness is part of Your character, part of Your way, and that it is doing something in me that the felt presence cannot do. The faith that continues in the absence is the faith that is being formed into something more durable than the faith that only functions in the presence.

The revelation awaits its appointed time. I will wait for it. It will certainly come. And when it does, I will know that the silence was not Your absence but Your hiddenness — and that You were present the whole time in a form I could not see but that was forming me nonetheless.

## Daily Practices

- Read Psalm 22 in full — all thirty-one verses. Note how it begins — my God, my God, why have you forsaken me — and how it ends: he has not despised or scorned the suffering of the afflicted one; he has not hidden his face from him but has listened to his cry for help. The psalm that begins with forsaking ends with listening. The arc from the beginning to the end of the psalm is the arc of the dark night to the dawn. Ask: where in this arc am I currently?
- Read the account of Mary Magdalene at the tomb in John 20:11–16. Note that Jesus was present — standing right in front of her — and she did not recognise Him. She was weeping, convinced He was absent, looking for Him in the wrong direction. And then He said her name. The presence was there before she perceived it. Ask: is it possible that God is present in my silence in a way I am not yet perceiving?
- Journal: "What does my current season of silence feel like specifically? How long has it lasted? And what has it been producing in me — even if the producing has been invisible and painful — that the season of felt presence might not have produced?" Write honestly.
- Read Habakkuk 3:17–19 — Habakkuk's final declaration after receiving the answer to his waiting. Though the fig tree does not bud, yet I will rejoice in the Lord. Let this be the posture of the silence — not the performance of a joy that is not felt, but the deliberate, willed choice to declare the faithfulness of God regardless of what the present circumstances are producing.
