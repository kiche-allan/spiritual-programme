I have had this conversation more times than I can count.

Someone discovers which church you attend and their face changes slightly — not with curiosity about your faith, but with a kind of calculation. *Oh, that church? I hear there are a lot of professionals there.* Sometimes it is more specific. *Plenty of business owners.* *Good for making connections.* And occasionally it is said without any embarrassment at all: *You should really start attending there. Lots of eligible sisters.*

I say this with some humour because the desires underneath are not entirely wrong. People need community. Single Christians may reasonably hope to meet a spouse in church. Young professionals need mentors. There is nothing shameful about any of that. The church should be a place where those things happen — and frankly, we should be better at facilitating them rather than pretending everyone will meet their future spouse through an unexpected divine encounter in the cereal aisle.

But something has gone wrong when a church is evaluated primarily by the calibre of its members. When we choose congregations the way we choose gyms — by the amenities and the clientele. When membership feels like a networking subscription with worship music included.

---

## The Sorting Happens. We Just Do Not Announce It.

Nobody tells anyone to stand at the back. Nobody says out loud that the man who arrived late in a worn jacket is less important than the woman who walked in with the kind of confidence that comes from a job title. We would never say that.

But the sorting happens anyway.

It is visible in who gets remembered by name after a few Sundays. Who gets a call when they miss two weeks. Whose opinions carry weight in a planning meeting. Who is invited to the dinner, and who is asked to stack the chairs after. After the service, the person with an interesting answer to *what do you do?* is in conversation for forty minutes. The man who has been out of work for eight months puts on his coat and leaves unnoticed.

Nobody was unkind to him. He was simply not the person anyone was hoping to meet.

James saw this clearly. He described a congregation where a man in fine clothes gets the good seat while the poor man is told to stand. And he asked, with the kind of directness that makes you uncomfortable: *Are you not then partial in yourselves?* He was not describing a secular event. He was describing a worship gathering. The sorting was happening in the assembly, at the moment of worship.

We have not improved on this. We have just made it more polite.

---

## A Network Runs Upward. The Body Is Supposed to Run the Other Way.

Here is the structural problem. A network is organised around value — your connections naturally flow toward people who can open doors, improve your standing, advance your cause. The weak and unimpressive are not despised in a network. They are simply irrelevant to it. There is no malice. There is no room.

The body of Christ is designed to run the other direction. Paul says in 1 Corinthians 12 that the members that seem to be more feeble are necessary, and that God gives more abundant honour to the parts that lack it. In a network, honour flows toward the impressive. In the body, it is deliberately redirected toward those without it.

These are not two versions of the same thing. They are opposite structures. And a church that has quietly adopted the logic of the network while maintaining the language of the body is not a body. It is a social club with communion.

The people who are being sorted always know. The refugee whose qualifications are not recognised here. The elderly widow who has nothing to offer anyone's career. The young man with nowhere interesting to point when someone asks what he does. Nobody is cruel to them. They simply notice that they are never the person anyone was hoping to meet. That noticing does something to a person over time.

---

## Even the Wealthy Lose Something in This Environment

There is a particular loneliness that belongs to the person who is always being leveraged. The successful professional who cannot tell, in a church full of warm faces, whether they are loved or useful. Whether the pastoral attention is because they matter or because they tithe significantly. Whether the friendship is genuine or strategic.

Church should be the one room in their week where they are not a resource. Where the relationship is not conditioned on what they can provide. Where someone asks how they are actually doing rather than positioning for an introduction.

A church full of talented, influential people where no one is truly known is not a success story. It is a particular kind of failure dressed in impressive clothing.

---

## The Test Is What Happens When Someone Stops Being Useful

The clearest test of whether a relationship is genuine or transactional is simple: what happens when the usefulness ends?

The successful professional loses their job. The generous friend begins to struggle financially. The influential member retires or falls ill. Does the interest remain, or does it quietly disappear with the circumstances that made them interesting?

You can sit next to someone in church for three years, know exactly what they do, where they work and who they know — and have no idea who they are. The relationships are wide but not deep. People know your professional biography. They do not know your actual life.

It is far easier to exchange contacts than to carry someone's burdens. Far easier to take a photograph with a speaker than to sit quietly with the struggling member who has nobody else to talk to. A church can be exceptionally well connected and still be profoundly lonely. The connections are real. The community is not.

---

## The Jesus Guest List

Jesus addressed this directly and in a way that leaves very little room for reinterpretation. When you make a feast, do not call your friends, your brothers, your relatives, or your rich neighbours — because they will invite you back and you will be repaid. Instead, invite the poor, the maimed, the lame, the blind. And you will be blessed because they cannot repay you.

Ordinary society runs on reciprocity. You invite me, I invite you. You recommend me, I remember you. Jesus calls His followers into a love that cannot be explained by mutual advantage. If we only love the people who can love us back in useful ways, we have simply reproduced ordinary social ambition inside a religious building — with better music and a more respectable excuse.

The question that I think we need to sit with honestly is this: if the people in your life at church could never advance your career, introduce you to anyone important, or improve your standing in any way — would you still want to know them?

If the answer is yes, you have something real. If the answer requires a long pause, you may have built an impressive network while missing the body.

---

## What This Actually Looks Like

I am not arguing against friendships forming in church, or mentorship, or marriages. I am not arguing against the diaspora community that holds you together when you are far from home and everything is unfamiliar. Those things are beautiful when they happen.

I am arguing against the calculation that precedes the relationship — the evaluation of a congregation by its professional demographics before you have sat through a single Sunday. The conversation that is friendly until you reveal you cannot help with what was actually being sought. The church culture where the worn coat waits longer for a hello than the gold ring.

It starts small. A different question after the service — *how long have you been coming?* rather than *what do you do?* Noticing who leaves without speaking to anyone and going to find them. Watching where the pastoral time actually goes, because that tells the truth about a church faster than anything in its vision statement.

A church should be a place where you might meet someone who changes your career or introduces you to your future spouse. But it should also be a place where you meet people who will never improve your circumstances in any measurable way, and you are glad you know them anyway. Not because of what they carry, but because of Whose they are.

A network asks what you can do for me. The body of Christ asks what I can do for you.

We should know the difference. And we should choose accordingly.
