## Revelation

The cross is the most powerful force for reconciliation in the history of the universe. Ephesians 2:14–16 says Christ destroyed the barrier, the dividing wall of hostility — and the word destroyed is the Greek katalyo, which means to tear down completely, to reduce to rubble, to leave nothing standing. The dividing wall Paul is describing is the wall between Jew and Gentile — the deepest, most ancient, most theologically loaded relational divide in the first-century world. And he says the cross destroyed it. Not weakened it. Not created the conditions under which it might eventually be overcome. Destroyed it. In one body, through the cross, by which He put to death their hostility.

If the cross has the power to destroy the barrier between Jew and Gentile — two groups separated by centuries of history, by dietary laws, by circumcision, by deeply held mutual contempt — it has the power to destroy the barrier between you and the person in your church who said the thing that wounded you. It has the power to destroy the barrier between the two elders whose unresolved argument is splitting the congregation. It has the power to destroy the barrier between friends who have not spoken in years because of a misunderstanding that neither of them was fully responsible for. This is not sentimentality. This is the specific claim of Ephesians 2 — and it is a claim that has been demonstrated in the most unlikely reconciliations in the history of the church.

Matthew 5:23–24 says something that should permanently change how every believer approaches worship: if you are offering your gift at the altar and remember that your brother or sister has something against you — leave your gift. Go first. Be reconciled first. Then come back and offer your gift. Jesus is saying that unresolved conflict between believers is not a private relational matter that can be compartmentalised away from your spiritual life. It reaches into your worship. It interrupts the offering. The God who receives worship will not receive it from a person who is holding an unresolved grievance against a brother or sister — not because He is petty, but because the reconciliation is itself the worship. The cross that forgave you has obligated you to forgive. Forgive as the Lord forgave you — freely, fully, specifically, without requiring the other person to earn it first.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I bring to You today every unresolved conflict, every unforgiven wound, every barrier that has been built between me and another person in the body of Christ — and I hold it up against the cross. You destroyed the dividing wall. You put to death the hostility. You reconciled enemies to Yourself in one body through the cross. What You did between Jew and Gentile You are able to do between me and the person whose name surfaces in me with a weight I have not been willing to address.

I receive Colossians 3:13 today as a direct command rather than an aspiration: forgive as the Lord forgave you. The standard is specific. Not forgive when it is easy. Not forgive when they have apologised. Forgive as You forgave — freely, fully, before they deserved it, at cost to yourself, without condition. Let me meet that standard today — not because I am capable of it by my own strength, but because the same Spirit who empowered the cross empowers the forgiveness that the cross makes necessary.

Show me if there is someone I need to go to today. Not to be right, not to relitigate the grievance, but to leave my gift at the altar and go first.

## Daily Practices

- Read Ephesians 2:11–22 in full — the complete theology of reconciliation through the cross. Note that the reconciliation between people and the reconciliation between people and God happen simultaneously, in one body, through the cross. You cannot fully receive one without the other becoming available. Ask: is there a person whose reconciliation I have been avoiding while still expecting full access to God?
- Read the parable of the unforgiving servant in Matthew 18:21–35 in full. Note the staggering disproportion: the servant who was forgiven ten thousand bags of gold refused to forgive a debt of a hundred silver coins. Ask honestly: is there a person in your life whose offence against you, measured against what God has forgiven you, looks like a hundred silver coins?
- Journal: "Who is the person I most need to forgive — or seek forgiveness from — right now? What is the specific grievance that is maintaining the barrier? What would it cost me to go first?" Write it honestly and then bring it to the cross specifically.
- Take one concrete step toward reconciliation today. Not the whole journey — one step. A message, a phone call, a prayer that is genuinely for their blessing rather than their conviction. Let the cross be applied specifically to the specific situation rather than held as a general theological belief that changes nothing in practice.
