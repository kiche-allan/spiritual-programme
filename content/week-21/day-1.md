## Revelation

The fear of the Lord is the most misunderstood phrase in the wisdom literature of the Old Testament — and possibly in the entire Bible. It has been domesticated by those who want to make it comfortable: it only means reverence, they say, not actual fear. And it has been distorted by those who want to weaponise it: it means terror, they say, the cowering of a slave before an unpredictable master. Both of these readings miss what the wisdom writers were actually describing — and in missing it, they have deprived the church of one of the most transformative and most practically significant postures available to the Christian life.

The fear of the Lord is not the terror of a slave before a tyrant. The person who is in Christ has not received a spirit of slavery leading back to fear, as Romans 8:15 says — they have received the spirit of adoption. The fear of the Lord is not incompatible with the love of God, with the intimacy of Abba, with the confidence of a child who knows they are welcome. What it is — what the wisdom literature consistently describes it as — is the reverential awe of a creature who has genuinely reckoned with who they are standing before. The awe of someone who has seen enough of God to know that He is not manageable, not domesticated, not a bigger and better version of a human authority figure who can be flattered or manipulated or reduced to something comfortable. The awe of someone who has encountered genuine holiness and discovered that genuine holiness produces in the encountering person both an overwhelming sense of their own smallness and an overwhelming sense of the privilege of being near.

Proverbs 9:10 says the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom — and the word beginning here is not the preliminary stage that you pass through on the way to the real thing. In Hebrew, reshit means the foundational starting point, the generative origin, the thing that everything else grows out of. The fear of the Lord is not what you have before you get to wisdom. It is the root from which wisdom grows, the soil in which every other virtue takes hold, the posture that makes every other posture possible. A person can be intelligent, educated, theologically literate, and experienced in ministry without the fear of the Lord — and what they will produce, for all their gifts, is something that falls short of wisdom. Because wisdom is not the accumulation of knowledge. It is the alignment of the whole person with the reality of who God is — and that alignment requires the awe that genuine encounter with His holiness produces.

Isaiah 11:2–3 gives us the most startling statement about the fear of the Lord in the entire prophetic literature: the messianic figure on whom the Spirit rests will delight in the fear of the Lord. Delight. Not endure, not practise as a discipline, not maintain as an obligation. Delight — the word used for the deepest, most genuine, most voluntary pleasure of a person in the thing they love most. The One on whom the Spirit of wisdom and understanding and counsel and might and knowledge rests — the One who is the fullest expression of what human life under the anointing of God looks like — delights in the fear of the Lord. Which means the fear of the Lord is not a beginner's posture to be grown out of. It is the permanent, deepening, increasingly joyful orientation of the most spiritually mature life available. The person who has gone furthest in God does not have less fear of the Lord. They have more — and it produces not dread but delight.

Acts 9:31 gives us the community expression of the same reality: the church throughout Judea, Galilee and Samaria was living in the fear of the Lord and was encouraged by the Holy Spirit, and it increased in numbers. The fear of the Lord and the encouragement of the Spirit are not presented as opposites — as though the Spirit's encouragement compensates for the grimness of the fear. They are presented as companions, as two dimensions of the same healthy spiritual atmosphere. The church that is living in genuine awe of God is the church that is simultaneously encouraged by the Spirit and growing. The fear of the Lord is not the enemy of vitality. It is its condition.

What does the fear of the Lord produce in the life that genuinely inhabits it? It produces the hatred of evil — Proverbs 8:13. The person who fears the Lord is not primarily motivated to avoid sin by the calculation of consequences. They hate what God hates because they love what God loves, and the love and the hate both flow from the same source — genuine closeness to the One whose character makes evil genuinely repulsive to anyone who has been near enough to see what it is. The fear of the Lord produces the humility that Proverbs 22:4 says brings wealth and honour and life. It produces the confidence that Proverbs 14:26 calls a secure fortress. It produces the lengthening of life that Proverbs 10:27 promises. Not as rewards dispensed from outside. As the natural consequences of a posture that orients the whole person correctly toward the whole of reality.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to You today not with the casual familiarity that has sometimes replaced the awe that genuine encounter with You requires — but with the reverential, joyful, deepening fear that Proverbs 9:10 calls the beginning of everything. Not the terror of a slave. The awe of a child who has seen enough of their Father to know that He is magnificent beyond all description and that being near Him is the greatest privilege available to a human being.

Let the fear of the Lord be the root from which everything else in my life grows this week. Let it produce in me the hatred of evil that genuine closeness to Your holiness generates, the humility that knows exactly who it is before, and the confidence that comes not from self-assurance but from knowing I am near the One before whom everything else is small.

And let me delight in it. The way Isaiah 11 says the Anointed One delights in it. Not as a burden but as a joy. The awe that is the beginning of everything.

## Daily Practices

- Read Proverbs 1:1–7 in full — the prologue to the entire book of Proverbs, which establishes the fear of the Lord as the foundation of everything that follows. Note that the fear of the Lord is described as the beginning of knowledge in verse 7 and the beginning of wisdom in 9:10 — wisdom and knowledge both rooted in the same soil. Ask: what is the practical difference between information and wisdom, and how does the fear of the Lord account for that difference?
- Read Isaiah 6:1–8 in full — the encounter with the enthroned God that produces in Isaiah both the devastating recognition of his own smallness and the cleansing that makes him usable. Note that the fear produced by the vision did not drive Isaiah away from God. After the cleansing, it drove him toward the mission. Ask: what would it look like for the fear of the Lord to drive me toward something rather than away from something?
- Journal: "Where have I allowed my familiarity with God to shade into a casualness that has reduced my awe? What has the loss of awe cost me practically — in the quality of my prayer, the seriousness of my obedience, the depth of my worship?" Write the honest answer.
- Read Psalm 112 — a portrait of the person whose life has been formed by the fear of the Lord. Note every quality listed: their children are mighty in the land, wealth and riches are in their house, light dawns in the darkness for them, they are gracious and compassionate and righteous. Ask: which of these qualities is most present in my life? Which is most absent? And what does its absence say about the depth of the root?
