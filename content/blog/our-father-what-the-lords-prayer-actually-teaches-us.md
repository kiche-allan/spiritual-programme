Jesus had been praying. The disciples watched Him — and when He finished, one of them said: Lord, teach us to pray, the way John taught his disciples. The request is significant. They did not ask Him to teach them to preach, or to heal, or to lead. They asked Him to teach them to pray. Something in the way He prayed was different from everything they had seen before — different in posture, different in intimacy, different in the quality of the relationship it seemed to assume. And His response to their request was not a lecture on prayer technique. It was a prayer itself. Short enough to be memorised in a single sitting. Deep enough to contain an entire theology.

Matthew 6:9 — this, then, is how you should pray. Not word for word, as a recitation to be performed. How — as a framework, a shape, a model for the kind of prayer Jesus had in mind when He imagined His followers addressing God. And the framework begins, before any request has been made, before any petition has been presented, with two words that have more theological weight than most sermons manage in forty minutes.

Our Father.

## The Revolution in Two Words

The religious world into which Jesus spoke these words did not address God as Father. The Old Testament uses the language of fatherhood for God occasionally — always in a collective, national, covenantal sense. Father of Israel. Father of the covenant people. The intimacy of a direct, personal, individual address was not the available framework. God was the Almighty, the Most High, the Holy One of Israel, the Lord of Hosts. These are magnificent titles. They are also titles that maintain a significant distance between the speaker and the One being addressed.

Jesus collapses the distance in two words.

Our Father. The Greek is Pater — the ordinary, domestic, intimate word for a father in a family. Not a title of majesty. A term of belonging. The same word a child in the marketplace would use to get their father's attention. And Paul confirms in Romans 8:15–16 that this is precisely the word the Spirit moves us to use: you have received the spirit of adoption by which we cry out Abba, Father — and Abba is the Aramaic word that goes even further than Pater, the word that is most closely translated Daddy, the most intimate address a child makes to a father who is near, who is safe, who is on their side.

The first thing the Lord's Prayer teaches us about who God is — before it teaches us anything about what to ask or how to ask it — is who we are to Him. Not subjects petitioning a sovereign. Not employees submitting a request to management. Not sinners approaching a judge who has agreed, under certain conditions, to hear their case. Children addressing a Father who already knows what they need, who already loves them completely, who has already made them welcome in His presence — and who gave them these words so that they would know how to come.

## Our — The Word That Creates a Community

Before the prayer has even named who it is addressed to, it has established who is praying. Not my Father. Our Father. The possessive is plural, and the plurality is not incidental. It is the first theological statement of the prayer — that the approach to God is never a solitary, private, individual transaction between one soul and its Creator. It is always a communal act. The person who prays the Lord's Prayer is praying it in the company of everyone else who has ever prayed it — across two thousand years, across every language the prayer has been translated into, across every circumstance and every culture in which the people of God have gathered to address the God who made them.

Our Father in heaven — the our reaches backward to Abraham and Moses and David and the disciples who first heard these words in the upper room. It reaches forward to every generation that will pray this prayer until the kingdom comes in its fullness. It reaches sideways to every believer currently alive in every place — to the church in Nairobi, to the church in Lagos, to the underground church in China, to the house church in Iran — all of them, right now, addressing the same Father, by the same word, in the same posture of children who have been welcomed into a household they did not build and cannot earn.

The our creates a solidarity before a single request has been made. It says: you are not alone in this address. The God you are approaching is not only yours. He is ours — which means the concerns you are about to bring are not carried only by you. They are carried by a family.

## Hallowed Be Your Name — Before the Requests Come the Recognition

The prayer does not move immediately from the address to the petition. It establishes an orientation first — a deliberate positioning of the self before God before any asking begins. Hallowed be your name. The word hallowed is the Greek hagiazo — to be set apart, to be treated as holy, to be regarded as sacred and worthy of the reverence that holiness demands. Hallowed be your name is the prayer of a person who has not yet brought a single request but has already declared that the One being approached is in a different category from everything else — set apart, above, worthy.

This is not merely liturgical decoration. It is the correction of the most common error in prayer — the error of approaching God with the list before the recognition, with the requests before the reverence, with the asking before the acknowledgment of who the One being asked actually is. Hallowed be your name is the discipline of taking a moment, before the urgency of the need pushes everything else aside, to remember that the God being addressed is not a vending machine, not a cosmic customer service agent, not a more powerful version of a human authority figure. He is the One whose name is holy — whose character, whose reputation, whose very identity is set apart from everything created.

The prayer that begins with hallowed be your name is the prayer of someone who has placed themselves correctly before they have placed their requests. The orientation precedes the petition. The reverence precedes the request. And the reverence, far from reducing the intimacy, deepens it — because the Father who is holy is the Father who is also Abba, and the combination of holiness and intimacy is not a contradiction but the most complete portrait of the God the prayer is teaching us to know.

## Your Kingdom Come, Your Will Be Done — The Submission That Makes Prayer Powerful

Two phrases that belong together and that most people pray too quickly. Your kingdom come. Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. The prayer begins with the address — Our Father. It establishes the orientation — hallowed be your name. And now, before a single personal request has been presented, it asks for two things that have nothing to do with the individual needs of the person praying. Or rather — they have everything to do with those needs, but they approach them from above rather than from below.

Your kingdom come is the prayer of a person who has understood that the resolution of every personal need exists within the larger context of God's reign being established on earth. The health that is needed, the relationship that is broken, the financial pressure that is real, the grief that is unresolved — all of these are personal. And all of them find their ultimate resolution not in the individual management of each specific crisis but in the coming of a kingdom in which every tear is wiped away, every disease is healed, every broken thing is made whole. The prayer for the kingdom is not a distraction from the personal. It is the largest possible context for the personal — the declaration that the God being addressed is not only the Father of the person praying, but the King of the universe, and that His reign coming is the answer to everything the prayer is about to request.

Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. This is the prayer's most profound act of submission — and it is not a resignation. In heaven, the will of God is done immediately, completely, joyfully, without resistance. Every angel, every living creature, every heavenly being orients itself around the will of the One on the throne with the completeness that earth has not yet reached. Your will be done on earth as it is in heaven is the prayer that asks for the quality of heaven's obedience to be present on earth — not only in the praying person's life but in the world they inhabit. And it is the prayer that submits the personal requests about to come — give us, forgive us, lead us, deliver us — to the wisdom of a God whose will is always better than our requests, whose way is always higher than our way, and whose answer is always the best possible answer, even when it does not look like the one we were hoping for.

## Give Us Today Our Daily Bread — Dependence Made Daily

Only now, after the address, the orientation, the submission to the kingdom and the will, does the prayer turn to personal need. And the first personal request is the simplest and the most revealing: give us today our daily bread. Not give us this year's security. Not give us the provision we need to stop worrying about provision. Give us today what we need for today.

The daily bread request is the prayer of radical, practiced, daily dependence. It is the prayer of a person who has not secured enough to stop needing God — who comes back to the same Father every morning with the same request because the bread for today was not designed to carry over into tomorrow's need. The Israelites in the wilderness received manna daily. When they tried to collect extra for the next day, it rotted. The provision was structured for daily dependence — not because God could not have given them a month's supply at once, but because the daily return was the practice of the relationship. Every morning, back to the wilderness floor. Every morning, the bread is there. Every morning, the practice of need is also the practice of trust.

There is something deeply countercultural in this request for the person who has achieved a degree of financial stability. The temptation of provision is the temptation of Deuteronomy 8 — the full stomach and the fine houses and the multiplied resources that quietly shift the felt sense of dependence away from God and onto the provision itself. Give us today our daily bread is the prayer that refuses that shift — the deliberate, daily, honest acknowledgment that every meal is still a gift, every resource is still a stewardship, and every day of provision is still a reason to come back to the Father who gave it.

## Forgive Us Our Debts — The Prayer That Requires Self-Examination

As we forgive our debtors. The two halves of the forgiveness petition cannot be separated, and Jesus emphasises this immediately after the prayer in Matthew 6:14–15: if you forgive other people when they sin against you, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. But if you do not forgive others their sins, your Father will not forgive your sins. The petition for personal forgiveness is structurally linked to the practice of extending forgiveness — not as a condition of God's love, but as the evidence that the forgiveness received has actually been received. The person who genuinely knows the weight of what they have been forgiven carries a different posture toward the offences of others. The comparison, as the parable of the unforgiving servant makes clear, is always disproportion — what has been forgiven us versus what we are being asked to forgive is never a fair comparison, and the awareness of that disproportion is what makes the extension of forgiveness possible.

The forgiveness petition is also the prayer's most searching self-examination. To pray forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors honestly requires a moment of genuine inventory — not the performed acknowledgment of a generic sinfulness, but the specific naming of what has not been right, what has been held against another person, what has been left unaddressed between you and God or between you and another person. The Lord's Prayer does not allow this to be rushed past. It places the petition at the centre of the prayer — after the kingdom and the bread, before the deliverance — as if to say: the forgiveness that flows in both directions is not a peripheral concern. It is the hinge on which the whole prayer turns.

## Lead Us Not Into Temptation, Deliver Us From Evil — The Honest Acknowledgment of Vulnerability

The prayer closes its petitions not with an aspiration toward personal greatness or a request for expanded blessing. It closes with the acknowledgment of vulnerability — lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one. This is the prayer of a person who knows themselves well enough to know that they need guidance rather than autonomy, protection rather than independence, deliverance rather than self-sufficiency. The request is not that temptation would never come — it is that when it comes, the Father who is leading would lead in the direction that avoids its worst expressions, and that whatever evil is actively working would find in the deliverer a power greater than its own.

The evil one is named — not as a superstition or a medieval holdover but as the specific, personal, intelligent, active adversary that Scripture consistently describes. The prayer does not ask us to pretend he does not exist. It asks us to bring his existence before the Father who is greater — to bring the awareness of the threat to the One who can deliver from it in a way that no amount of personal spiritual effort can accomplish alone.

## For Yours Is the Kingdom, the Power and the Glory

The doxology that closes the prayer in most traditions — for yours is the kingdom, the power and the glory, forever and ever, Amen — brings the prayer full circle. It began with the address of a Father. It ends with the declaration of a King. It began with the intimacy of Abba. It ends with the glory of the One who reigns forever. These are not two different postures toward two different Gods. They are the full portrait of the One God whose relationship with His children is both the most intimate thing available to a human being and the most magnificent thing that exists in the universe.

The kingdom is His — not a human construction, not a political programme, not a religious institution, but the reign of the Father who is also King, coming on earth as it already is in heaven. The power is His — the same power that raised Jesus from the dead, that split the Red Sea, that spoke the universe into existence and holds it together by the word of His power. The glory is His — the manifest presence of everything He is, the radiance that Isaiah saw filling the temple, the light that has no darkness in it, the beauty before which every created thing eventually bows.

And it is His forever. Not for a season. Not until a better claim is established. Forever — the duration that exceeds every human timeline, that outlasts every earthly power, that continues when everything that felt permanent has passed away. The Amen at the end is not a verbal full stop. It is the agreement of the person praying with everything the prayer has declared — the alignment of their amen with the Amen who is Jesus Christ Himself, in whom all the promises of God are yes and Amen.

## What Two Words Changed

Our Father. The prayer begins there and everything else flows from there. The holiness and the intimacy. The submission and the request. The daily dependence and the forgiveness and the deliverance and the glory. All of it is possible because the one who is praying has been given permission, by the Son of God, to address the Creator of the universe as Father. Not as the God of our fathers — though He is. Not as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob — though He is. As our Father. Mine and yours and the whole company of heaven and earth who have been adopted into the household of the One who, in the most extraordinary act in the history of creation, sent His Son to make the adoption possible.

This is why the disciples asked Jesus to teach them to pray. Not because they did not know how to speak to God. Because they had seen someone who knew God as Father — who prayed to Him the way a child speaks to a parent who is completely safe and completely present and completely on their side — and they wanted what He had.

He gave it to them. He gave it to us. Two words. Everything else follows.

## A Prayer

Our Father in heaven — hallowed be Your name. Let it be set apart in my life the way it is set apart in heaven, treated as holy in my ordinary days, the way it is treated as holy in the courts of eternity.

Your kingdom come. Your will be done — on earth, in my specific life, in the situations I am navigating right now — as it is in heaven. Not my kingdom. Not my will. Yours, which is always better.

Give us today our daily bread. Let me come back tomorrow for tomorrow's. Let the daily return be the daily practice of the dependence that keeps me close to You.

Forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors. Search me for what needs to be forgiven. And find in me the grace to extend to others what I have received from You — freely, specifically, as the overflow of a person who knows the weight of what they themselves have been forgiven.

Lead us not into temptation but deliver us from the evil one. I cannot navigate this without Your leading. I cannot withstand this without Your deliverance. I am not self-sufficient and I do not want to pretend otherwise in my prayers.

For Yours is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory — forever. Not mine to grasp. Yours to give. Yours to hold. Yours to fill with the presence that makes the whole prayer possible.

Amen — and Amen.
