## Revelation

Hagar does not appear in the genealogies. She is not listed among the heroes of faith in Hebrews 11. She does not have a book named after her or a psalm attributed to her or a letter written to the churches that carry her testimony. She is a supporting character in someone else's story — an Egyptian slave woman who was given to Abraham by Sarai as a surrogate, who became pregnant, who was treated harshly when the pregnancy made the power dynamic uncomfortable, and who ran away into the wilderness with nothing except the child in her womb and the desperate instinct of someone who had no other option. She is the most overlooked person in the Abraham narrative. She may be the most overlooked person in the entire book of Genesis.

And she is the only person in the entire Bible who gives God a new name.

Genesis 16:13 — she gave this name to the Lord who spoke to her: You are the God who sees me. El Roi. The name had never been spoken before. No patriarch had named Him this. No prophet had declared it. Abraham, who had walked with God for decades, who had received the covenant promises, who had hosted the divine visitors — Abraham had not thought to call Him this. It took a slave woman alone in the wilderness, at the absolute end of everything she had and everything she was, to see the dimension of God's character that no one else had yet perceived — not because it was new, but because it takes a specific kind of desperation to recognise it.

The angel of the Lord found her by a spring in the desert. The verb found implies searching — the active, purposeful, directional movement of someone who knows where they are going because they know who they are looking for. Hagar had not sent for God. She had not prayed for rescue. She had simply run — in the way that people run when there is nothing left to do but flee. And God found her. By a spring. In the desert. Before she had done anything to qualify for His attention. He found her the way a shepherd finds the one sheep that has gone into the wilderness — not because the sheep called out, but because the shepherd went looking.

He called her by name. He knew she was Hagar, the servant of Sarai — which means He knew her situation in its specific social and relational complexity, not merely as a generic human being in distress. He asked where she had come from and where she was going — not because He did not know, but because the question was the gift of being genuinely addressed, of having someone care enough about your specific situation to ask about it. And then He spoke her future. He told her about the son she was carrying, gave the child a name, described what he would be. He gave her a future she had not asked for in the middle of a desperation she had not anticipated.

And Hagar — the slave woman, the overlooked, the one who had been used and discarded — gave God the name He had never been given before. You are El Roi. The God who sees me. And she added the wonder of the secondary discovery: I have now seen the One who sees me. The seeing went in both directions. She saw, and she was seen. And the double seeing — the moment when the invisible person discovers that the God she can barely see has been looking at her all along — is the moment that changes everything.

Psalm 139 unpacks what this seeing actually means: you have searched me, Lord, and you know me. You know when I sit and when I rise. You perceive my thoughts from afar. You discern my going out and my lying down. You are familiar with all my ways. Before a word is on my tongue, You know it completely. This is not surveillance. This is intimacy — the complete, exhaustive, sustained attention of a God who has not looked away from you for a single moment of your existence, who knows the thoughts before they are formed, who is familiar with all your ways because He has been present for all of them. You have never been invisible to Him. Not in the wilderness. Not in the hard season. Not in the moment when every human eye had looked elsewhere.

1 Samuel 16:7 adds the most comforting dimension of the seeing: the Lord does not look at what people look at. People look at the outward appearance. The Lord looks at the heart. What God sees when He looks at you is not the version you have managed for public consumption — not the composed face, the theological vocabulary, the spiritual performance. He looks at the heart. The real one. The one that is afraid and hopeful and wounded and longing and more complicated than any single description can capture. He looks at all of it. And He does not look away.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the name that Hagar gave You in the desert — El Roi, the God who sees me. Not the God who sees crowds. Not the God who sees the impressive and the prominent and the people whose lives appear to be working. The God who found a slave woman by a spring in the wilderness before she had done anything to qualify for His attention. That God is the same God who is looking at me today — in whatever wilderness I am currently in, in whatever season of invisibility or overlooking or being the supporting character in someone else's story.

Let me know today that I am seen. Not performing for an audience that may or may not be paying attention. Seen — completely, specifically, tenderly — by the One who looked at the heart of a forgotten woman in the desert and found something worth speaking a future into. You are El Roi. I have now seen the One who sees me.

## Daily Practices

- Read Genesis 16:1–13 in full — the complete Hagar narrative. Note every specific detail: the harshness of Sarai's treatment, Hagar's flight, the spring in the desert, the angel's questions, the future spoken over her unborn son, the name she gave God. Ask: what does the specificity of God's attention to Hagar say about the specificity of His attention to you?
- Read Psalm 139 in full — slowly, as a personal address rather than a theological study. Let each verse be received as something spoken directly to you. Note especially verse 16: your eyes saw my unformed body; all the days ordained for me were written in your book before one of them came to be. Ask: what does it mean that God saw you before you existed?
- Journal: "In what specific season or circumstance of my life have I felt most invisible — most like the supporting character in someone else's story, most overlooked and least seen? What does El Roi say directly to that specific season?" Write the honest answer and let it be a prayer.
- Read Genesis 21:9–19 — the second wilderness encounter between Hagar and God, when she and Ishmael have been sent away and she can no longer bear to watch her son die. Note verse 17: God heard the boy crying. God heard. God saw. God spoke. God acted. Ask: is there a current situation in which you need to hear that God has heard the crying you have been doing in secret?
