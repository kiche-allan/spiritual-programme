## Revelation

Every major figure in Scripture spent time in the wilderness before they stepped into their calling. Moses spent forty years in the desert of Midian tending sheep after fleeing Egypt — forty years of apparent obscurity between the burning ambition of his youth and the burning bush that commissioned him to lead the Exodus. David spent approximately fifteen years between his anointing by Samuel and his coronation as king — years of hiding in caves, of Saul's spears, of the Philistine territories and the wilderness of Judah. Elijah spent forty days in the wilderness after the greatest spiritual victory of his life, when the depression and exhaustion of Carmel had reduced him to wanting to die under a broom tree. Paul spent three years in Arabia after his Damascus road conversion — three years of almost complete silence in the biblical record, before he emerged as the apostle to the Gentiles. And Jesus — the Son of God Himself, freshly baptised, the Spirit having descended on Him like a dove, the Father having just declared this is my beloved Son in whom I am well pleased — was immediately led by that same Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil for forty days.

The pattern is too consistent to be incidental. The wilderness is not the detour from the destiny. It is the preparation for it. What God does in the wilderness is specifically what He cannot do in the comfortable season, in the season of visible fruitfulness, in the season when the identity is confirmed by the results. He does it in the wilderness precisely because the wilderness strips away every other source of confirmation and leaves the person with nothing but God — no platform, no results, no audience, no visible evidence that anything is happening — and in that stripping discovers, or more accurately forms, what the person is made of at the level that will determine everything about how they carry what is coming.

Deuteronomy 8:2–3 gives us God's own explanation of what He was doing in Israel's forty years of wilderness: He led you in the wilderness to humble you and to test you in order to know what was in your heart. The testing was not punitive. It was diagnostic and formative — God learning, through the test, what was genuinely in the heart, and the wilderness being the specific environment in which what was genuinely in the heart was revealed rather than concealed. The hunger was deliberate. The manna was deliberate. The lesson that man does not live on bread alone but on every word that comes from the mouth of God could not be learned in a well-provisioned land. It could only be learned in the place where bread was absent and the word was all that remained. Jesus quotes this exact verse in Matthew 4:4 when He is tempted by the devil to turn stones into bread after forty days of hunger. He had learned, in the wilderness, what Israel had been taught in their wilderness — that the Word is the sustenance that bread is only a shadow of.

Hosea 2:14 gives us the most surprising description of the wilderness available in Scripture: therefore I am now going to allure her; I will lead her into the wilderness and speak tenderly to her. The wilderness as the place of tender speech. The wilderness as the destination of the divine alluring — not the punishment of an angry God but the wooing of a pursuing lover who wants to get the beloved alone, away from the noise and the distractions and the other voices, in order to speak what can only be heard in the quiet. The wilderness is the place of intimacy — the desert that Israel experienced as punishment and that Hosea reframes as the specific environment in which God's voice is most clearly heard, precisely because every other voice has been removed.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the wilderness I am in — or the wilderness I have been through, or the wilderness I sense is coming — not as evidence that something has gone wrong but as the specific, deliberate, Spirit-led environment in which You do what You cannot do in any other season. You led Jesus into the wilderness. You allured Israel there to speak tenderly. You kept Moses in the desert for forty years until the burning bush. The wilderness is Yours. The in-between is not abandoned — it is inhabited, by You, doing what only the wilderness makes possible.

What is in my heart — let the wilderness reveal it and form it. What I cannot learn in the well-provisioned season — let the hunger teach it. What I can only hear when every other voice has been removed — let the desert silence make space for it. I do not need to rush through this. I need to learn what You are teaching in it.

Lead me in the wilderness and speak tenderly to me there.

## Daily Practices

- Read Matthew 4:1–11 in full — Jesus's complete wilderness encounter with the devil. Note the three temptations and what each one was specifically targeting: provision, protection, and power. Note that Jesus answers every temptation with the Word of God — specifically with Deuteronomy, the book about Israel's wilderness. Ask: what does it mean that Jesus overcame the wilderness temptations with the lessons Israel was supposed to have learned in their wilderness?
- Read 1 Kings 19:1–18 — Elijah's wilderness after Carmel. Note that God's response to his exhaustion and depression is not a spiritual lecture. It is bread, water, and sleep — twice. The angel touches him and says the journey is too much for you. Then, after the physical needs are met, the still small voice. Ask: what does God's practical, physical care for Elijah in the wilderness say about how He meets us in our lowest moments?
- Journal: "What is the specific wilderness I am currently in — the in-between season, the apparent obscurity, the place where the results are not visible and the waiting has gone on longer than expected? What might God be specifically doing in this season that He could not do in a more comfortable one?" Write the honest exploration.
- Read Genesis 16:7–14 — Hagar in the wilderness, El Roi finding her by the spring. Read Genesis 21:14–19 — Hagar in the wilderness a second time, God hearing the boy crying. Note that God found Hagar in the wilderness twice. Ask: is it possible that the wilderness you are in is the specific address at which God is most actively present — not despite the difficulty but because of it?
