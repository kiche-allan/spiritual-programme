## Revelation

The parable of Luke 15 has been named after the wrong character for two thousand years. It is called the Parable of the Prodigal Son — and the younger son is genuinely prodigal, genuinely lost, genuinely in need of the father's running and the robe and the ring and the feast. But the most unsettling character in the story is not the one who left. It is the one who stayed. The elder brother. The one who never squandered his inheritance, never fed pigs in a foreign country, never came to himself in the ash heap of his own choices. The one who was always there. The one who worked every day, kept every rule, fulfilled every obligation — and who, at the moment of the feast, was standing in the field refusing to go in.

He heard the music first. The text says that as he came near the house, he heard music and dancing — and he called one of the servants to ask what was happening. He did not go in to find out. He stood outside and asked someone else. And when he learned that his brother had returned and the father had killed the fattened calf, he became angry. The Greek word is orgizo — the settled, sustained orge that we discussed earlier, the anger that does not merely flare but takes up residence. He became angry and refused to go in.

His complaint to the father, when the father came out to plead with him, is one of the most revealing speeches in the entire Gospel narrative: look, these many years I have been serving you and I have never disobeyed your orders. Yet you never gave me even a young goat so I could celebrate with my friends. But when this son of yours who has squandered your property with prostitutes comes home, you kill the fattened calf for him. Three specific failures of perception in this speech. He called his father's house your property rather than our home — the elder brother had been living in the father's house without ever fully receiving it as home. He described his years of service as slavery — I have been serving you like a slave — rather than as the privilege of a son. And he called his brother this son of yours — refusing the relationship, the family, the belonging that had always been available to him. He had been in the father's house for years without receiving any of what the house contained.

The father's response is the most tender and the most searching sentence in the entire parable: son, you are always with me, and everything I have is yours. Always with me. Everything I have is yours. The elder brother had had access to everything the father possessed for the entire length of his elder-brother life — and had never claimed it, never entered it, never received it. He had been present without arriving. He had been in the house without being home.

Revelation 2:4 gives us the theological category for what the elder brother represents in the life of the church: you have forsaken the love you had at first. The church at Ephesus — doctrinally correct, enduring, hard-working, testing false apostles, persevering — had lost the first love. Not the doctrine. Not the service. Not the endurance. The love. The joy of the first encounter with the Father. The running posture of the person who has been received. The feast that belongs to the person who knows they have come home. They had stayed. They had maintained. They had served. And something essential had been lost in the staying.

Matthew 23:23 gives us the specific content of what gets lost: the more important matters — justice, mercy, and faithfulness. The elder brother had the tithing of the spices. He had the technical compliance with every requirement. He had never disobeyed an order. And he had lost mercy — he could not celebrate the return of the one who had been lost. He had lost justice — he could only see the inequity of the calf being killed for the returning prodigal, not the justice of the father's love for both sons. And he had lost faithfulness — not the faithfulness of rule-keeping, but the faithfulness of a son who trusts that the father's generosity toward the returning brother does not diminish the inheritance of the one who stayed.

The week ends here — with the question that the elder brother's refusal forces: are you in the house or at home? Are you present in the community or genuinely belonging to it? Are you serving the father or receiving the father? The feast is still happening. The music is still playing. The father has come out to plead with you. Will you go in?

## Morning Prayer

Father, I close this week with the elder brother's question — not his answer, but his question. Am I in the house but not home? Have I been serving without receiving, staying without arriving, keeping the rules without inhabiting the relationship? Have I been so long at the outside of the feast that I have forgotten what the feast is for?

You have come out to plead with me the way the father came out to plead with the elder son. Son, you are always with me. Everything I have is yours. Let me receive that today — not as a theological position but as the lived, joyful, feast-entering reality of a person who has finally understood that the house was always home and the inheritance was always available and the music was always meant for me too.

I am going in. The feast is still happening. And I will not stand in the field while the music plays.

## Daily Practices

- Read Luke 15:11–32 in full — the complete parable, both brothers. Read it this time specifically from the elder brother's perspective — his work, his staying, his anger, his speech, his refusal, and the father's response to the refusal. Ask: which dimension of the elder brother's experience most closely describes your own current relationship with the father's house?
- Read Revelation 2:1–7 — the complete letter to the church at Ephesus. Note what Jesus commends: the deeds, the hard work, the perseverance, the testing of false apostles, the endurance. And note what He holds against them: the forsaking of the first love. Ask: what is the specific first love I have forsaken — the joy, the intimacy, the running posture of the early encounter with God — that the years of service and doctrinal correctness have gradually displaced?
- Review the entire week — the fear of the Lord as a way of life, the sovereignty over the specific story, the lament of the overlooked, the adoption from Lo-debar, the table that is always ready, the ministry of intercession, and the elder brother who refused to go in. Write one sentence for each day capturing what it produced in you. Seven sentences. This is your testimony of the week.
- Go in. Whatever the specific posture of the elder brother has been in your life this week — the anger at the feast, the itemising of the service, the refusal to celebrate what the father is celebrating — bring it to the father who has come out to plead with you. Receive the son, you are always with me. And go in to the feast that has been waiting for you.
