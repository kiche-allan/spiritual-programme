## Revelation

Moses was tending sheep. Not praying. Not fasting. Not in the temple or at the altar or in any of the locations that the religious imagination associates with divine encounter. He was doing the most ordinary thing — the work he had been doing for forty years in the wilderness of Midian, the same route, the same flock, the same desert landscape that had become the unremarkable background of his daily existence. And the bush burned.

The location of the burning bush is the first thing worth noting: the far side of the wilderness, near Horeb, the mountain of God. The far side of the wilderness. Not the accessible side, not the populated side, not the side near the cities and the courts and the centres of religious and political activity. The far side. The place where Moses had gone because the flock went there, not because he expected anything significant to happen. The burning bush was not in a location that invited divine encounter. It was in the last place Moses would have looked for one.

This is the pattern of divine encounter throughout Scripture. The angel appeared to the shepherds — not to the priests in the temple, not to the scribes in the study halls, but to the shepherds out in the field keeping watch at night. The Spirit fell on the disciples gathered in a house — not in the specific location that the religious establishment would have chosen for the inaugural event of the new covenant community. God consistently shows up in the ordinary places, in the working hours, in the midst of the unremarkable activity of people who are simply present to their lives rather than waiting for significance to arrive.

What made the burning bush into an encounter was Moses's response: I will turn aside to see this great sight. The bush may have been burning for some time before Moses noticed it. The fire was unusual but not immediately overwhelming — it required the specific attention of someone who chose to leave the main path and investigate. When the Lord saw that he turned aside to see, God called to him out of the bush. The encounter waited for the turning aside. The divine call waited for the human attention.

This is the most practically significant detail in the entire burning bush account — because it means that divine encounters are not always the overwhelming, unmissable manifestations that bypass human agency and attention. Sometimes they are the quiet, persistent, unusual thing at the edge of the ordinary day that requires the decision to turn aside, to leave the main path, to investigate what the distracted person would walk past. The bush burned. Moses turned aside. Then God spoke.

Luke 2:8–9 gives us the same pattern at the nativity: the shepherds were in the field, doing their night work, when the glory of the Lord appeared. Acts 2:2 gives it again: the disciples were sitting in the house when the sound like a rushing wind filled it. In every case the people who encountered God were present to their ordinary location, doing their ordinary work, living their ordinary lives — and the encounter came to them there rather than requiring them to be somewhere else first.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to the ordinary place today — the working hours, the unremarkable location, the daily routine that has become so familiar that I have stopped expecting You to show up in it. And I receive the burning bush today as the pattern of how You often come: not in the dramatic departure from the ordinary, but in the unusual thing at the edge of the ordinary that requires me to turn aside.

Give me the Moses attention today — the willingness to notice the bush that is burning differently, to leave the main path and investigate, to turn aside from the efficiency of the route I was planning in order to see the great sight that is happening at the edge of it. When I turn aside, speak. I am turning.

## Daily Practices

- Read Exodus 3:1–12 in full. Note the specific sequence: the appearance, the noticing, the turning aside, and then — only then — the divine call. Ask: what is the burning bush at the edge of my current ordinary life — the unusual, persistent thing that I have been too busy to turn aside and investigate? What would happen if I turned aside today?
- Read 1 Kings 19:9–13 — Elijah at Horeb, the same mountain as the burning bush. Note that God was not in the wind, not in the earthquake, not in the fire — but in the still small voice that came after. Ask: have I been looking for God in the dramatic while He has been speaking in the quiet thing I keep moving past?
- Journal: "Where has God been showing up in my ordinary — in the work, the routine, the unremarkable daily pattern — that I have been too distracted to notice? What specific bush has been burning at the edge of my path that I have not yet turned aside to see?" Write the honest exploration.
- Practise the turning aside today. Identify one moment in your ordinary day — a meal, a commute, a quiet moment between tasks — and treat it as the potential burning bush. Stop. Pay attention. Notice what is unusual or persistent or quietly insistent in the environment of your ordinary life. Let the turning aside be the prayer.
