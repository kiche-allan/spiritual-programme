## Revelation

Luke 19:10 is one of the shortest and most complete mission statements in the entire Gospel — seven words that summarise why the Son of God left the throne of heaven and entered the specific, limited, costly form of a human life: the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost. The word lost does not primarily mean morally corrupt, though that is included. It means misplaced — the sheep that has wandered from where it was supposed to be, the coin that has fallen somewhere in the house and cannot be found, the son who has gone to a far country and spent everything. Lost is the condition of a person who was made for something and is not currently in it — who has potential and purpose and destiny that have not yet been located and claimed.

The context of Luke 19:10 is Zacchaeus — and Zacchaeus is one of the most important case studies in the Gospels for understanding what the sentence actually means. He is the chief tax collector of Jericho, which means he is the most despised man in a city that already despised tax collectors. He has become wealthy through the systematic exploitation of his own people. He is short in stature and has to climb a tree to see over the crowd — a detail that is both literal and metaphorical, describing a man who is always on the outside looking in, who can never quite get to the front of any crowd because no crowd has ever wanted him there. He is the person the religious establishment had permanently written off — too compromised, too corrupt, too far gone to be worth addressing.

And Jesus looks up into the tree and says: Zacchaeus, come down immediately. I must stay at your house today. The must is significant — the same word used in Luke 4:43 when Jesus says I must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God. It is the language of divine necessity, of the compulsion that comes from mission. Jesus did not pass by Zacchaeus as a courtesy. He stopped because the mission required it. Zacchaeus was not incidental to the journey through Jericho. He was the reason for it.

You are a possibility. This sentence contains everything that Luke 19:10 is trying to say to the person who has concluded that their situation is too compromised, their history too complicated, their failures too significant, their position too marginal for God to have something to do with them. The Son of Man came to seek the lost — and seeking implies active effort toward something that does not know it is being looked for. He came for you before you came for Him. He climbed to where you are — the tree, the tax collector's booth, the far country, the ICU, the prison, the failed marriage, the wasted years — and He called you by name. And when He did, the crowd grumbled that He had gone to be the guest of a sinner. And salvation came to that house anyway.

Jeremiah 29:11 and Ephesians 2:10 add the full picture of what the possibility actually contains: plans to prosper and not to harm, a hope and a future. Good works prepared in advance. You are not a blank page that God is trying to fill with something improvised. You are God's handiwork — a poem, a crafted thing, something made with intention and artistry — and the good works prepared in advance for you to do are not waiting for you to become someone else first. They are waiting for the person you already are, found by the One who came seeking, to walk into what was prepared before you were ready for it.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the declaration that Luke 19:10 makes about every person who reads it: the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost. He came for the Zacchaeus situations — the ones the crowd had written off, the ones too compromised and too marginal for polite religious company. He came for those, specifically, necessarily, with the must of divine mission.

Let me receive today that I am a possibility — not despite my history, not after my situation has improved, but now, as I am, in the specific circumstances I am in. The good works prepared in advance for me are not waiting for a better version of me to show up. They are waiting for the me who is here today, found by the God who came looking.

And let me look up from whatever tree I have climbed to see over the crowd — and hear my name being called from below.

## Daily Practices

- Read Luke 19:1–10 in full. Note every specific detail — the sycamore tree, the crowd, Jesus looking up, the immediate response of Zacchaeus, the crowd's grumbling, the declaration of restitution, and the final sentence. Ask: which detail in this story speaks most directly to your own sense of where you currently are?
- Read the three parables of lostness in Luke 15 in full — the lost sheep, the lost coin, the lost son. Note what all three have in common: the seeker does not wait for the lost thing to find its way back. The seeker goes. Ask: where do you see God's seeking in your own history — the moments when He moved toward you before you moved toward Him?
- Journal: "What has made me feel like a less likely candidate for God's purposes — my past, my failures, my current situation, my sense of inadequacy? What does Luke 19:10 say directly to that specific conclusion?" Write the honest answer and then write the honest response of Luke 19:10 to it.
- Identify one person in your life who has been written off by the crowd — by circumstance, by reputation, by the weight of their own history — and pray for them specifically today. Pray that the Son of Man who came to seek and save would seek them with the same necessity He sought Zacchaeus.
