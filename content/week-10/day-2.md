## Revelation

Abraham waited twenty-five years between the promise and the child. Joseph spent thirteen years between the dream and the throne — two of them in a prison for a crime he did not commit. The disciples spent three days between the crucifixion and the resurrection, not knowing that Sunday was coming. Every one of these people lived in the gap — the space between what God had said and what had not yet arrived — and every one of them was required to make a decision about what to do with that gap. The decision was always the same: would they anchor their identity to the promise or to the circumstances?

Hebrews 11:1 gives us the biblical definition of faith, and it is almost entirely about this gap. Faith is confidence in what we hope for and assurance about what we do not see. It is not confidence in what has already arrived — that is not faith, that is sight. Faith is the posture you hold in the gap — the settled conviction that what God has spoken is more real than what your circumstances are currently saying, and that the silence of the not-yet does not mean the absence of the God who promised. This is the hardest thing. Not the dramatic crisis of faith where everything is on the line and the stakes are obvious. The slow, grinding, ordinary, undramatic challenge of continuing to trust a God you cannot currently see or feel through the long weeks and months where nothing appears to be happening.

Romans 5:3–5 gives us the most important insight into what the gap is actually doing. Suffering — the experience of the not-yet — produces perseverance. Perseverance produces character. Character produces hope. This is not a platitude. It is a description of a process. The hope that does not put us to shame — the unshakeable, proven, tested hope — is not available at the beginning of the wait. It is produced by the wait. The hope that Abraham had at the end of twenty-five years was qualitatively different from the hope he had at the beginning. It had been tested. It had survived years of silence and apparent contradiction. It had become something the circumstances could no longer touch. That is the hope God is building in you through the very gap you are currently impatient to escape.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I bring You today the gap — the space between what You have spoken and what has not yet arrived. The promise I am still holding. The prayer I am still waiting to see answered. The situation that has not yet resolved. The season that has gone on longer than I thought it would.

I choose today to anchor myself to what You have said rather than to what I can currently see. Not because the waiting is easy — it is not. Not because I have arrived at some elevated spiritual state that is above the frustration of the not-yet — I have not. But because You are trustworthy. Because every person who ever waited on You found that You were worth waiting for. Because the hope that comes through the wait is something the circumstances can never produce by themselves.

Renew my strength in the waiting. Not the strength to grit my teeth and endure, but the strength of someone whose roots have gone down deep enough to sustain them through the drought. Let me walk and not faint, run and not grow weary, soar — even here, even in this — on wings like eagles.

## Daily Practices

- Read Hebrews 11 in full — the great hall of faith. Note that almost every person listed died without receiving what was promised. They trusted a God whose faithfulness they would not fully see in their lifetimes. Let their testimony reframe your own timeline expectations.
- Journal: "What is the specific promise or prayer I am currently in the gap on? How long have I been waiting? What has the waiting produced in me so far — bitterness, perseverance, drift, or deeper roots?" Write honestly.
- Read Genesis 15:1–6 — the moment Abraham believed God and it was credited to him as righteousness. Note that he believed in circumstances that appeared to completely contradict the promise. Ask: what would it look like for me to believe God today, specifically, in my own contradicting circumstances?
- Read Lamentations 3:19–33 slowly. The writer is in genuine suffering. And from inside that suffering he says: the Lord is good to those whose hope is in him, to the one who seeks him; it is good to wait quietly for the salvation of the Lord. Let those words be your declaration today — not a denial of the difficulty, but a choice about where to anchor while the difficulty continues.
