## Revelation

The God of the Bible is not the God who waits to be reached. He is not the God of the philosophers — the unmoved mover, the first cause, the distant principle who set the universe in motion and withdrew to a safe theological distance from the consequences. He is the God who comes down. Every major act of God in the biblical narrative is a descent — a movement from above to below, from heaven to earth, from the infinite to the finite, from the untouched to the deeply, personally, costly involved.

Exodus 3:7–8 gives us the pattern in its clearest Old Testament form. God speaks to Moses from the burning bush and says three things in sequence: I have seen the affliction of my people. I have heard their cry. I have come down to deliver them. The seeing, the hearing, and the coming down are not three separate divine activities. They are the single movement of a God whose attention and whose presence are inseparable — who does not observe suffering from a distance and decide to send assistance, but who comes Himself, in person, to the place where the suffering is happening.

This coming down is the pattern that runs through the entire biblical narrative. God came down into the garden in the cool of the day to walk with Adam and Eve. He came down to Abraham at the oak of Mamre. He came down in the pillar of cloud and fire to lead Israel through the wilderness. He came down into the tabernacle so completely that Moses could not enter because the glory filled it. He came down in fire on the altar of Elijah at Carmel. And then — in the fullness of time, in the event that all the previous descents were pointing toward — He came down in the most complete and most costly form available: the Word became flesh and dwelt among us.

John 1:14 uses the Greek word eskēnōsen — He tabernacled among us, He pitched His tent among us. The word is deliberately chosen to connect the incarnation to the tabernacle of the wilderness — the portable dwelling place where God came down to be present in the middle of His people's journey. The incarnation is the ultimate tabernacling — God not merely visiting, not merely sending a representative, but personally, physically, irrevocably taking on the form of the very creatures He had made, entering the specific limitations of human existence, becoming subject to the hunger and thirst and weariness and grief that He had previously only observed from outside.

Philippians 2:7–8 gives us the internal logic of the descent: He emptied Himself. He humbled Himself. He became obedient. The descent of God is a voluntary descent — not the forced reduction of a power that has been overwhelmed, but the chosen kenosis of a love that is willing to go as far as the object of the love needs to be reached. He emptied Himself of the privileges of deity — not of the nature of deity, which cannot be emptied, but of the prerogatives, the comfort, the immunity from suffering that the divine nature in its unincarnated form possesses. And He descended all the way to the bottom: death on a cross. The descent that began in eternity ended on Calvary.

What does the theology of divine descent mean for the person who is currently in the place that needs the coming down? It means that the God you are praying to is not a God who receives your prayer from a safe distance and dispatches assistance while remaining comfortably above the situation. He is the God who came down. Who has been down. Who knows from the inside what it costs to be human in a fallen world — what the hunger feels like, what the grief feels like, what the forsakenness feels like. The descent of God is the guarantee that when you call out from your Egypt, your burning bush, your Calvary — He is not hearing you from far away. He has been there. He came down.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the most extraordinary truth about who You are — that You are the God who comes down. Not the distant sovereign who observes from above and sends instructions. The God who descended — into the garden, into the bush, into the womb, into the tomb. Who came all the way to the bottom so that the bottom would never again be a place where You are absent.

Let me live today from the awareness of a God who has come down — who is not far from any one of us, who does not need to be persuaded to be present, who has already made the descent and who therefore meets me in the lowest places of my life not as a visitor but as One who knows the terrain from the inside.

You came down. You are here. That is enough.

## Daily Practices

- Read Exodus 3:1–12 in full — the burning bush account. Note that God's coming down is announced before any instruction is given. He comes to Moses first as the God who has seen, heard, and descended — and only after establishing that does He commission. Ask: what does it mean that the God who calls you has already come down into the situation He is calling you toward?
- Read John 1:1–18 in full — the prologue of John's Gospel. Note the movement from the beginning through the Word, through the light, through the incarnation, to the tabernacling. Ask: at which point in this prologue does the descent feel most real to you — and why?
- Journal: "In what specific area of my life do I most need to receive the truth that God has come down — that the situation I am in is not one He is observing from a distance but one He has entered? What changes when I receive His presence there as the coming-down God rather than the watching-from-above God?" Write the honest exploration.
- Read Hebrews 4:14–16 — the high priest who has passed through the heavens, who has been tempted in every way as we are. Note that the invitation to come boldly to the throne of grace is grounded in the fact that the One on the throne has been where you are. The descent is the basis of the access. Ask: how does knowing that Jesus has been where I am change the quality of my prayer today?
