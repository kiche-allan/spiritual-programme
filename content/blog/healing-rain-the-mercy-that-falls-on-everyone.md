There are songs that arrive at the right moment and say the thing that has been impossible to say. Healing Rain, written and recorded by Michael W. Smith in 2004, is that kind of song. It is not a song about healing already received. It is a song about healing coming — approaching, drawing nearer, not yet fully arrived but moving with a certainty that changes the posture of everyone who believes it is on its way. It is the song for the ICU waiting room. For the diagnosis that has not yet resolved. For the dry heart that has been in the desert long enough that it has stopped expecting rain.

## It Is Coming Nearer

The opening line does something specific and important: *healing rain is coming down — it's coming nearer to this old town.* The verb is present progressive — coming, not arrived, not promised for some distant future, but actively in motion, actively drawing closer to the specific place where the need is. This old town. Not a theological abstraction about healing in general but a movement toward a specific location, a specific situation, a specific person whose heart has been waiting.

The image of rain as healing is ancient in Scripture. James 5:7 uses the farmer waiting for the autumn and spring rains as the image of patient, expectant faith. Isaiah 44:3 speaks of God pouring water on the thirsty land and streams on the dry ground, and in the same breath promises to pour His Spirit on the people. Joel 2:23 promises the autumn rain and the spring rain — restoration after the locust years, abundance after the season of loss. Rain in the Hebrew imagination was not merely meteorological. It was the visible expression of God's favour returning to a land and a people who had experienced its absence.

Healing Rain picks up this ancient imagery and places it in motion. It is coming. It is getting closer. The distance between the need and the provision is actively shrinking. This is not optimism dressed as theology. It is the specific posture of faith that Hebrews 11:1 describes — the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not yet seen. The rain has not yet fallen on every situation. But it is coming. And the coming itself changes everything about how you wait.

## Rich and Poor, Weak and Strong

The second line of the song is where the theology becomes most surprising and most important: *rich and poor, weak and strong — it's bringing mercy, it won't be long.* The healing rain does not discriminate. It does not fall on those who have earned it through sufficient faith, or on those whose theology is most correct, or on those whose lives are most composed. It falls on rich and poor alike — which means it falls regardless of what you have or do not have. It falls on weak and strong alike — which means it falls regardless of what your capacity or your faith currently feels like.

This is the indiscriminate character of mercy, and it is the most countercultural thing the gospel ever says. Every human system of distribution is discriminatory in some way — mercy and resources flow toward those who deserve them, those who have connections, those who are strong enough to access them. God's rain falls on the just and the unjust, as Jesus says in Matthew 5:45. Not because the distinction does not matter, but because mercy, by definition, is not a response to deserving. It is the response of a God who gives because of who He is, not because of who the recipient has managed to become.

If you are reading this from a place of weakness right now — if your faith feels insufficient, if your strength has been depleted by the length of the wait, if you have arrived at the place of needing healing precisely because you do not have the resources to produce it yourself — the song has specific words for you: *weak and strong.* You are in the list. The rain is coming for you too.

## Tears of Joy and Tears of Shame

The second verse gives us the full emotional range that healing encompasses: *tears of joy and tears of shame are washed forever in Jesus' name.* This pairing is worth sitting with. The song does not promise that only tears of joy will be washed. It promises that tears of shame — the tears that come from the places you are most reluctant to bring into the presence of God, the tears that represent your deepest failures and your most hidden wounds — are included in what the rain covers.

This connects directly to what John 4 shows us at the well. The Samaritan woman Jesus encountered had every reason to approach with tears of shame — five husbands, the sixth man not her husband, an encounter at the well alone in the heat of the day because the presence of other women had become too costly. And Jesus asked her for water. He initiated the conversation. He moved toward the shame before she had composed herself or prepared a presentation. The healing available to her was not contingent on the shame being dealt with first. The healing came to where the shame was, and the washing happened there.

Tears of joy and tears of shame are washed forever. The word forever here is doing significant work. Not temporarily soothed. Not covered until the next failure. Washed — completely, in an act that does not need to be repeated, in Jesus' name, whose name carries the authority to accomplish what the act declares.

## Healing Rain It Comes With Fire

The chorus introduces an image that seems to contradict itself at first: *healing rain, it comes with fire.* Rain and fire are opposing elements. Rain extinguishes; fire consumes. And yet the song holds them together without apology, because the biblical picture of genuine healing always involves both.

The rain comes with fire because real healing is not merely the restoration of what was broken to its previous state. It is a purification — a burning away of what was not supposed to be there in the first place. Malachi 3:2 asks who can endure the day of His coming, describing the Lord as a refiner's fire and a launderer's soap. The metaphors are both about removing what does not belong. The fire removes the impurities from the metal. The soap removes the stain from the cloth. Neither process is comfortable. Both produce something that was not possible before the process.

Isaiah 4:4 describes the Lord washing away the filth with a spirit of judgment and a spirit of fire — the fire and the washing occurring simultaneously, not sequentially. The rain of healing and the fire of purification are not two separate events. They are the same event experienced from two different angles. What feels like the fire of an impossible season — the illness, the crisis, the waiting that has gone on too long — may be simultaneously the rain of the very healing that feels most distant. Not because suffering is good in itself, but because the God who heals does not always heal in the way that feels most like healing from the inside of the process.

*So let it fall and take us higher.* The invitation is surrender — not to the suffering, but to the process of a God whose methods of healing are not always the most comfortable or the most direct. Let it fall. Do not resist what is coming because it is arriving in a form you did not expect. Let it take us higher — which implies that the destination the rain is carrying us toward is not a return to where we were before, but an elevation to somewhere the suffering has been making possible all along.

## I Am Not Afraid

*Healing rain, I'm not afraid to be washed in Heaven's rain.* This line is one of the most honest confessions in the song, precisely because of what it implies: that the person singing has been afraid, or has some reason to be afraid, of what being washed might involve. Fear of healing is a real thing, and it deserves to be named rather than glossed over.

There are things about healing that can feel threatening. The healing of a relationship requires the exposure of what has been hidden. The healing of a wound requires the wound to be opened, cleaned, and dressed before it can close properly. The healing of a season of spiritual dryness often requires first the honest acknowledgment of how dry the ground has actually become — which means sitting in the awareness of the absence before the rain can be received. Being washed implies that there is something to be washed away — and identifying what that is can require a vulnerability that feels more dangerous than the unhealed state.

The declaration I am not afraid is therefore not the absence of fear. It is the choice — the deliberate, willed decision — to open toward the healing despite what the opening might cost. To be washed in Heaven's rain rather than remaining dry because the dryness is at least familiar and predictable. It is the posture of Naaman in 2 Kings 5, who nearly walked away from the very healing he had travelled for because the method was not what he expected. His servants persuaded him: if the prophet had told you to do something great, you would have done it. How much more should you do it when the instruction is simply — go and wash.

## The Mercy Seat

The bridge brings the image to its most personal and most theologically concentrated moment: *lift your heads, let us return to the mercy seat where time began. And in your eyes I see the pain — come soak this dry heart with healing rain.*

The mercy seat in the Old Testament was the lid of the ark of the covenant — the place where the blood of atonement was sprinkled on the Day of Atonement, the specific location where God said He would meet with Moses, the physical address of divine mercy in the tabernacle. Hebrews 4:16 transforms this image for the New Testament believer: let us therefore come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need. The mercy seat is now accessible — not once a year by the high priest alone, but always, by every believer, boldly.

*And in your eyes I see the pain.* This line inverts the expected direction of the gaze. The person singing is not describing their own pain to God. They are describing what they see when they look into God's eyes. The pain in God's eyes. This is not a metaphor for divine empathy in the abstract — it is the specific claim that the God who is being asked to heal is not observing the suffering from a position of comfort and distance. He is pained by it. He is not indifferent to what the waiting has cost, to what the illness has taken, to what the season of dryness has done to the heart that is asking to be soaked.

*Come soak this dry heart with healing rain.* The prayer at the centre of the song is not for an immediate removal of the circumstances causing the dryness. It is for a soaking — a thorough, saturating, extended contact with the mercy that the circumstances have not been able to produce. A soaked heart is not a heart whose circumstances have improved. It is a heart that has been so thoroughly in contact with the presence of God that the dryness no longer defines it.

## The Leper Who Could Stand

*And only you, the Son of Man, can take a leper and let him stand.* The leper in the Gospels — most vividly in Matthew 8:1–4 — was not merely physically ill. He was, by the law and by social reality, untouchable. His disease placed him outside the community, outside the temple, outside the possibility of normal human contact. He was required to announce his own uncleanness to anyone who approached so they could avoid him. He was the walking embodiment of what it feels like to be beyond the reach of normal care.

And Jesus touched him. The text is specific: *He reached out his hand and touched the man.* This is not incidental. The healing could have happened at a word, from a distance, without contact — as it did in other accounts. But Jesus touched the untouchable man before the healing, at the point of maximum uncleanness, before anything had changed. And in the touching, the healing came: *immediately his leprosy was cleansed.*

The song's use of this image is pointed: only you, the Son of Man, can take a leper and let him stand. The standing is significant. The leper who has been healed is not merely recovered. He is standing — upright, dignified, restored to the posture of a person who belongs in the company of others. The healing rain does not merely remove the illness. It restores the standing.

## So Lift Your Hands

*So lift your hands, they can be held by someone greater, the Great I Am.* The song ends where the leper's story ends — not with the healed person managing their recovery alone, but with the healed hands being held by the One who healed them.

The hands that were sick, the hands that were too weak to lift, the hands that have been at the side for so long that lifting them feels like too much to ask — these are specifically the hands the song addresses. Lift them. They can be held. Not by someone equal to what you are facing. By someone greater. The Great I Am — the name God gave Himself in Exodus 3:14, the name that is not past tense or future tense but present tense: I am. Not I was. Not I will be. I am, here, now, in this moment, in this situation, in this specific place of need where the rain has not yet fully fallen.

Lift your hands. They can be held.

## I Am Not Afraid

The final repetition of the song's central declaration — *healing rain is falling down, I'm not afraid* — moves from confession to declaration. What began as a decision (*I am not afraid to be washed*) becomes, through the repetition that constitutes the song's ending, a settled confidence. I am not afraid. I am not afraid. Again and again.

This is how declarations work in the spiritual life. You do not usually arrive at settled confidence through a single act of will. You arrive at it through the repeated, deliberate, sometimes desperate choice to declare what you believe above what you feel, until the declaration and the reality have aligned. The song repeats its declaration not because the singer does not believe it but because the repetition is itself the practice of belief — the thing done in the dark, in the waiting, in the season when the rain has not yet fully fallen, until the falling becomes evident.

The healing rain is falling down. It has been falling on this situation longer than it has been visible. It is falling on you right now, in the specific place of your specific need, regardless of whether you can currently feel it. And the appropriate response to rain that is falling — even rain you cannot yet see — is to lift your head, open your hands, and let yourself be washed.

*I'm not afraid.*

## A Prayer

Father, I come to You with this dry heart — the places where the waiting has gone on long enough that I have stopped expecting rain, the wounds that have been open long enough that I have started to mistake them for permanent features of who I am, the fears that have been present long enough that they feel more real than the healing You have promised.

I bring to You the specific people and situations where healing is most needed right now — physical bodies in crisis, relationships that have fractured, faith that is running dry, hearts that have been in the desert longer than they had resources for. Let the rain fall on all of it. On the rich and the poor, the weak and the strong, the lost and the found. Let the tears of shame be washed with the same thoroughness as the tears of joy. And let the fire that comes with the rain burn away what has no business remaining, so that what emerges on the other side is not merely recovered but elevated.

Only You, the Son of Man, can take a leper and let him stand. Only You can soak what has become completely dry. Only You are the Great I Am, present in this moment, in this specific need, holding the hands that are finally willing to be lifted.

Let the healing rain fall. We are not afraid.

In Jesus' name, Amen.
