## Revelation

Matthew 7:12 is known as the Golden Rule — and like most things that become well known, it has become familiar enough to be heard without being felt. Do unto others as you would have them do unto you. Most people have known this sentence since childhood. Most people agree with it in principle. Very few people have genuinely reckoned with what it would mean to apply it consistently, across every relationship, in every circumstance, including the ones where the other person is not applying it toward them.

The sentence Jesus gives is deceptively simple and structurally demanding. It does not say do unto others as they have done unto you — which would be the natural human instinct, the law of reciprocity that governs most social interaction. It does not say do unto others what you feel they deserve. It says do unto others what you would have them do unto you — which requires you to move the imagination in a specific direction. Not how have they treated me? But how would I want to be treated in their position? The standard is not their behaviour toward you. It is the behaviour you would want directed toward yourself, projected outward into your behaviour toward them.

This requires, in practice, a specific act of empathy that most people perform inconsistently and selectively. It is easy to apply the golden rule to people who are applying it toward you — to be kind to the kind, generous to the generous, patient with the patient. The genuine challenge of Matthew 7:12 is in the opposite direction: to treat the difficult colleague the way you would want to be treated if you were in their position; to treat the family member who has hurt you the way you would want to be treated if you were the one who had made a mistake of that magnitude; to treat the stranger the way you would want to be treated if you had arrived in their circumstances.

Jesus adds a phrase that elevates the instruction from a social ethic to a theological summary: for this sums up the Law and the Prophets. The entire moral content of the Old Testament — all the commands, all the case laws, all the prophetic calls to justice and mercy — is being summarised in this one sentence. This is not an oversimplification. It is a distillation. If a person genuinely, consistently, imaginatively applied this principle to every relationship and every circumstance, they would be fulfilling everything the law ever asked for — because every specific command in the law is a specific application of this general principle. Romans 13:10 confirms it from the other direction: love does no harm to a neighbour; therefore love is the fulfilment of the law. The golden rule is love in its most practical, most applicable, most daily form.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the instruction that is both the simplest and the most demanding in the Sermon on the Mount: do unto others what I would have them do unto me. I confess that I have applied this selectively — generously toward the people I find easy to love, and inconsistently toward the people I find difficult. I have used the standard when it benefited me and set it aside when applying it cost me something.

Give me the specific empathy that this instruction requires — the ability to genuinely ask, in each relationship and each circumstance today: how would I want to be treated if I were in their position? And then to act from the answer rather than from my own hurt, my own convenience, or my own instinct of reciprocity.

Let the golden rule be not a sentence I know but a posture I embody today — in the difficult conversation, in the relationship that has not been reciprocal, in the circumstance where the other person has not applied this standard toward me. Let love be the fulfilment of the law in my ordinary, daily interactions.

## Daily Practices

- Read Matthew 7:1–12 in full — the golden rule in its full context. Note that it comes after the teaching on judging others, on asking and receiving in prayer, and on the narrow and wide gates. Ask: how does the context shape how you understand the golden rule? What does it mean that it appears here, as the culmination of these particular teachings?
- Identify the three relationships in your life where the golden rule is currently hardest to apply. For each one, ask specifically: how would I want to be treated if I were in their position? Write the honest answer for each. Then ask: am I currently treating them that way?
- Read Luke 10:25–37 — the parable of the Good Samaritan, which Jesus told in response to the question of who is my neighbour. Note that the Samaritan applied the golden rule across every social and religious barrier that would normally have prevented it. Ask: who is the person in my life I am most tempted to exclude from the application of this rule?
- Journal: "In what specific situation today can I apply the golden rule in a way that will cost me something — that will require me to give what I would want to receive rather than what the other person's behaviour has earned?" Identify it and commit to doing it.
