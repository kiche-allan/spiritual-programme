## Revelation

The Great Commission is the most misunderstood mandate in the New Testament. It has been professionalised — turned into the special calling of missionaries, evangelists, and full-time ministers — in a way that has quietly absolved the ordinary believer of the responsibility it actually places on them. Jesus did not give this command to a select group of spiritually qualified professionals. He gave it to eleven men standing on a hillside in Galilee — men who had recently been hiding behind locked doors, who had doubted in His presence, who had no institutional infrastructure, no financial support, no strategy. He gave it to them, and through them, to every person who has ever received what they received.

The grammatical structure of Matthew 28:19–20 is important: the main verb is not go. It is make disciples. Going is a participle — as you go, as you move through your ordinary life — make disciples. The command is not primarily about geographical movement. It is about intentional, ongoing, relational investment in the people God has already placed in your path. You do not need to cross an ocean to obey the Great Commission. You need to cross the room.

The Samaritan woman at the well is the New Testament's most striking portrait of spontaneous, ordinary, untrained witness. She had one conversation with Jesus. She did not receive theological training before she went. She did not wait until she had all her questions answered. She left her water jar — the everyday, practical, completely ordinary object that represented everything she had come to do — and she went back to her town and said: come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. Could this be the Messiah? She shared what she knew from what she had encountered. The whole town came. And Jesus stayed two more days because of the harvest that her witness produced. She did not preach a sermon. She told a story. And it was enough.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive the Great Commission today not as the calling of a special category of Christians but as my personal mandate. I was not saved into a private relationship with You that is mine to enjoy in isolation. I was saved into a story that is moving toward the redemption of all things — and I have been placed in this specific moment of that story, in this specific city, in these specific relationships, as a sent person.

Forgive me for the ways I have treated evangelism as someone else's responsibility — the professional's job, the extrovert's gift, the evangelist's calling. You have given me a story. You have given me encounters. You have given me the words: come and see. Let me say them.

Give me the Samaritan woman's instinct — to leave my water jar without waiting for the perfect moment, and to tell the people I already know about the One who told me everything I ever did.

## Daily Practices

- Read John 4:1–42 in full — the complete account of the Samaritan woman. Note the progression: she came to draw water, she had an encounter with Jesus, she left her water jar, she told her town, they came to see. The whole sequence took one afternoon. Ask: what is the equivalent of my water jar — the everyday thing I am carrying that I need to set down in order to go and tell?
- Journal: "Who are the specific people in my life who do not yet know Jesus? Name them — do not keep this abstract. For each one, ask: what is my current posture toward their faith? Am I praying for them? Am I intentional with them? Have I ever told them my story?"
- Read Acts 1:8 and note the progression: Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, ends of the earth. Your witness has a geography. It starts where you are. Who is in your Jerusalem? Name the people, the places, the relationships that constitute your immediate mission field.
- Pray specifically for one person by name who does not yet know Jesus. Not a general prayer for the lost — a specific, detailed, informed prayer for a specific person. Commit to praying for them by name every day this week.
