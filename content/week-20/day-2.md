## Revelation

Romans 8:28 is one of the most quoted verses in the New Testament and one of the most poorly received. It is quoted most frequently at the worst possible moments — in the immediate aftermath of loss, in the middle of grief that has not yet had time to breathe, as a theological corrective to emotions that are entirely appropriate and do not need correcting. When it is received as a quick comfort designed to resolve the discomfort of the person offering it rather than the person receiving it, it produces not faith but the peculiar additional burden of feeling that you are not allowed to be as devastated as you actually are.

But received in its proper depth, on its own terms, with the full weight of the word all allowed to land — Romans 8:28 is one of the most radical and most sustaining truths available to a human being. Not some things. Not the things that made sense at the time. Not the things that were pleasant or the things that were clearly God's will or the things that produced visible good quickly enough to make the connection obvious. All things. Including the betrayal. Including the diagnosis. Including the years that felt wasted. Including the door that closed when you were certain God had opened it. Including the prayer that went unanswered for so long that you stopped praying it. All things — God works in all of them, for the good of those who love Him, who have been called according to His purpose.

Joseph is the defining Old Testament case study for this. Thirteen years — from the pit his brothers threw him into at seventeen to the throne room of Pharaoh at thirty. Thirteen years in which every visible indicator suggested that the dreams God had given him were cruel jokes, that the promises were empty, that the favour he thought he had was either imaginary or had been revoked. Sold by his brothers. Falsely accused by Potiphar's wife. Imprisoned. Forgotten by the man he helped. And then — with the abruptness that characterises God's interventions when the preparation is finally complete — standing before the most powerful man in the known world, interpreting dreams that would save millions of lives.

Genesis 50:20 gives us Joseph's theological summary of the thirteen years: you intended to harm me, but God intended it for good to accomplish what is now being done, the saving of many lives. The you is his brothers — the specific human agents whose jealousy and cruelty had set the whole chain of events in motion. Their intention was harm. It was real harm. The pit was a real pit. The slavery was real slavery. The imprisonment was real imprisonment. Joseph does not minimise any of it or pretend it was secretly pleasant. He names it accurately: you intended to harm me. And simultaneously — not instead, not instead of, but simultaneously — God intended it for good. Two intentions, operating on the same set of events, producing radically different outcomes from the same raw material.

Job's case is the most extreme version of the same testimony. Job lost everything — his children, his wealth, his health, the support of his wife, the theological framework of his friends — and received, from God Himself, no explanation for why. The book of Job is forty-two chapters long and God never explains the reason for the suffering. What He does instead, in the whirlwind speeches of chapters 38–41, is reveal Himself — display the vastness and the wisdom and the power and the beauty of who He is in a way that makes explanation unnecessary. And Job's response in 42:5 is the testimony of a man who has received something more valuable than an explanation: my ears had heard of you, but now my eyes have seen you. The suffering produced a seeing that prosperity could not have produced. The loss produced an encounter with God that the abundance had not provided. Nothing was wasted — not a single day of the suffering, not a single tear of the grief. All of it produced the seeing.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I bring to You today the things that feel wasted — the years, the efforts, the prayers, the relationships, the opportunities that seemed to close before they opened, the suffering that has not yet produced a visible good. I bring them to Romans 8:28 honestly — not as a quick comfort but as a genuine, sustained trust that You are working in all of them, for my good, according to a purpose that exceeds what I can currently see.

Let me have the faith of Joseph — who held the dream through thirteen years of evidence against it and arrived at the throne room with the character that only the pit and the prison could have produced. And let me receive the gift that Job received — not necessarily an explanation, but a seeing. The encounter with who You are that makes the explanation unnecessary.

Nothing is wasted in Your hands. Not one day. Not one tear. I trust You with all of it.

## Daily Practices

- Read Genesis 37 and 39–45 in a single sitting — Joseph's complete journey from pit to palace. Note every transition and ask: what specific quality was each stage of the journey forming in him that the next stage required? What did the pit produce that the comfortable life at home could not have produced?
- Read Job 38:1–7 — the opening of God's response to Job from the whirlwind. Note that God answers Job's sustained lament not with an explanation but with questions — where were you when I laid the earth's foundations? Ask: what does it mean that the answer to Job's suffering was not information but encounter? Is there a place in your own suffering where encounter might be more valuable than explanation?
- Journal: "What specific thing in my life have I been interpreting as wasted — a season, a relationship, a prayer, a period of suffering that has not yet produced visible fruit? What would it look like to trust Romans 8:28 specifically over that thing — not generally, but naming it and declaring that God is working in it for good?" Write it and let it be an act of faith.
- Read 2 Corinthians 4:16–17 — the outward wasting and the inward renewal, the light and momentary troubles achieving an eternal weight of glory. Ask: what eternal weight of glory might the current trouble be achieving in me that I cannot yet see?
