## Revelation

The goodness of God is the most tested doctrine in the Christian life. It is the one that the circumstances most persistently argue against — the diagnosis, the unanswered prayer, the loss that makes no theological sense, the suffering that continues past the point where any obvious purpose can be identified. Every believer who has lived long enough and honestly enough has arrived, at some point, at the question that the circumstances seem to force: if God is good, why is this happening? And the quality of the answer they find — or fail to find — determines whether their faith deepens through the difficulty or erodes under it.

The word that Psalm 34:8 uses is not believe or declare or affirm. It is taste. Taste and see that the Lord is good. Tasting is the most immediate, most personal, most embodied form of knowing available. You cannot taste by proxy. You cannot taste at a distance. You cannot taste by reading a description of what something tastes like or by trusting the report of someone who has tasted it before you. Tasting requires your own direct contact with the thing being tasted — your own mouth, your own experience, your own encounter with the actual reality rather than the theological description of it. Psalm 34:8 is not an invitation to believe a doctrine. It is an invitation to a direct, personal, experiential encounter with the goodness of God — to find out for yourself, in the actual circumstances of your actual life, that He is what He says He is.

The context of Psalm 34 is important. David wrote this psalm when he was pretending to be insane before Abimelech in order to escape — one of the most undignified and desperate moments in his entire story. He was not writing from a position of theological security and comfortable circumstances. He was writing from a place of fear and flight and the kind of desperation that produces behaviour you are not proud of. And from that place — not from a retreat centre, not from a season of spiritual abundance, but from the middle of the most embarrassing chapter of his life — he wrote: taste and see that the Lord is good.

The goodness of God is not a doctrine that requires pleasant circumstances to be true. It is a foundation that is most genuinely discovered when the circumstances are least pleasant — when everything that might otherwise be providing a sense of security has been removed and what remains is the question of whether God, in Himself and by His own character, is actually good. Romans 8:32 gives the most theologically decisive argument for the goodness of God available in the New Testament: He who did not spare His own Son, but gave Him up for us all — how will He not also, along with Him, graciously give us all things? The argument moves from the greater to the lesser. If God has already given the most costly thing He possessed — the life of His own Son — what possible reason could there be for withholding anything genuinely necessary? The cross is the proof of God's goodness that no circumstance can disprove. Because the cross happened in the worst possible circumstance — the death of the Son of God — and it was the most extravagant expression of goodness the universe has ever witnessed.

Nahum 1:7 adds the dimension that makes the goodness practically relevant in the difficult season: the Lord is good, a refuge in times of trouble. He cares for those who trust in Him. The goodness is not distant, abstract, or future. It is available now, in the time of trouble, as a refuge — a specific place to go when everything outside is dangerous, a shelter that holds because it is made of what God is rather than what the circumstances are. The person who has taken refuge in the goodness of God during the worst season of their life knows something that cannot be learned from a book — the specific, experiential, tasted knowledge that the doctrine is true, not because the circumstances have become good, but because God is good in the middle of circumstances that are not.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I taste Your goodness today — not as an intellectual exercise, but as the deliberate, personal, direct encounter that Psalm 34:8 invites. I choose to find out for myself, in my actual circumstances, that You are what You say You are. Not because the season is pleasant enough to make it easy, but because the cross has already proved what the season is currently testing. He who did not spare His own Son — how will He not also graciously give us all things?

You are good. Not good in the version that means comfortable or convenient or immediately explainable. Good in the version that held through Calvary and came out the other side as resurrection. Let that goodness be the foundation I stand on today — the thing that holds when the building above it is shaking.

I take refuge in You. And You are good.

## Daily Practices

- Read Psalm 34 in full. Note that it is an acrostic psalm — each verse beginning with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet — which means it was crafted with extraordinary care and intentionality. Every line is a chosen declaration. Ask: which line of Psalm 34 most directly addresses where I am today?
- Read Lamentations 3:19–33 — the passage that moves from the bitterness of affliction to the declaration of God's goodness through the discipline of calling something specific to mind. Note verse 21: yet this I call to mind and therefore I have hope. The goodness is not felt first. It is called to mind deliberately, against the grain of the feeling, and the hope follows the calling. Ask: what do I need to deliberately call to mind today about the goodness of God?
- Journal: "What is the specific circumstance that is currently making it hardest for me to believe that God is good? What does Romans 8:32 say directly to that circumstance — the argument from the greater gift to the lesser?" Write the argument out fully.
- Practise the tasting today — not as metaphor but as practice. Identify one specific evidence of God's goodness in your current season — however small, however easy to overlook — and taste it deliberately. Name it, thank God for it specifically, and let the tasting of one thing begin to open the palate for more.
