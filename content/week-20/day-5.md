## Revelation

The parable has been misnamed for two thousand years. It is called the Parable of the Prodigal Son — and the son is prodigal, in the original sense of the word: recklessly wasteful, extravagantly squandering what he was given. But the son is not the most remarkable character in the story. The most remarkable character in the story is the father. And the father is also prodigal — extravagantly, recklessly, wastefully generous with a love that bears no proportion to what the returning son has done to deserve it. If the parable has to be named after the one whose behaviour is most theologically significant, it should be called the Parable of the Prodigal Father.

The son had taken his portion of the estate — which in first-century Jewish culture was the equivalent of telling his father he wished he were dead — and gone to a far country and spent everything. He had fed pigs, which for a Jewish young man was the deepest possible defilement. He had eaten the food of pigs. He had reached the absolute bottom of everything a person in his cultural context could reach. And he came to himself — the text's description of the moment of sanity that arrived when the desperation was complete — and rehearsed the speech he would give. He would return not as a son but as a servant. He had forfeited the son relationship. He would take whatever the servant relationship offered.

He was still a long way off. The father saw him. This detail contains an entire theology. The father was looking. Not waiting indoors, hoping the son might eventually return, willing to receive him if he came. Looking — in the direction the son had gone, scanning the road, watching for the silhouette that would eventually resolve into the face he had been missing. The watching implies the daily expectation of return. It implies the father had been doing this since the son left. Every day, looking toward the far country, watching the road for the figure that would mean the waiting was over.

He ran. In first-century Palestinian culture, a man of property and honour did not run in public. Running required the hitching up of robes — an undignified exposure that was beneath the social standing of a patriarch. The father ran anyway. The dignity was less important than the son. The social convention was less important than the reunion. He covered the distance between them before the son had arrived — which means the son never had to walk the last stretch of the journey alone. The father was running toward him while he was still rehearsing the speech. The speech was never finished. The robe was already on his shoulders before he could complete the apology.

The robe, the ring, the sandals, the feast — each of these is a restoration of a status the son had forfeited. The best robe covered the pig smell and the tattered clothing of the far country. The ring was the signet ring — the seal of the family's authority, the mark of belonging. The sandals distinguished the son from the servants, who went barefoot. And the fattened calf was not a routine meal. It was the feast that had been prepared for a specific occasion and kept in readiness for when it was needed. The father had not given up on the occasion. He had kept the calf ready. The celebration was not improvised in the relief of the moment. It had been prepared in the faith that the moment would come.

This is the God Jesus was describing. Not a God who waits to be persuaded of your sincerity before restoring your status. Not a God who requires a completed apology before He will move toward you. A God who has been watching the road since you left, who runs toward you while you are still rehearsing the speech, who covers the distance before you arrive, who puts the robe on your shoulders before the words are out, who has kept the feast in readiness because He never stopped believing the occasion would come. Romans 5:8 confirms that this is not parable but theology: while we were still sinners — while we were still in the far country, still smelling of the pig pen, still rehearsing the inadequate speech — Christ died for us. The running happened at the cross. Before we arrived. Before we turned back. Before we came to ourselves.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the portrait of You that Jesus drew in Luke 15 — the running father, the watching father, the father who covered the distance before the son arrived. I receive it not as a nice story but as the most accurate description of Your posture toward me that has ever been given. You have been watching the road. You have been looking in the direction I went. And You ran before I was ready to be received — at the cross, while I was still in the far country, before I had composed the speech.

Let me receive the robe today without finishing the apology first. Let me receive the ring without feeling I need to earn back the status first. Let me receive the feast without feeling I need to do something first to deserve being celebrated. This is what it means to receive grace — not the diminished standing of the servant who comes back ashamed, but the restored standing of the son who is welcomed home by a father who ran.

I am home. And the feast is already prepared.

## Daily Practices

- Read Luke 15:11–32 in full — slowly and in its entirety. Pay attention to the older son in verses 25–32, who is angry about the celebration. Note the father's response to him: son, you are always with me, and everything I have is yours. Ask: are you more like the returning prodigal or the resentful older brother in your current relationship with the Father?
- Read Luke 15:1–10 — the lost sheep and the lost coin that precede the prodigal son parable. Note that all three parables are told in response to the same complaint: the Pharisees and teachers of the law were muttering that Jesus welcomes sinners and eats with them. Ask: is there anyone in your life whom you have been less willing to welcome than the Father has been?
- Journal: "What is the speech I have been rehearsing — the apology, the explanation, the conditions I have been putting on my own return to God? What would it look like to stop rehearsing the speech and simply let the Father run?" Write the speech and then cross it out. Let the crossing out be the act of receiving grace.
- Read the parable one more time and this time read it for the feast. The fattened calf had been kept in readiness. The celebration had been prepared in faith. Ask: what has God been keeping in readiness for your return that you have not yet arrived to receive?
