## Revelation

The Christian hope is not the immortality of the soul. This is one of the most important distinctions in the entire theological vocabulary — and it is one of the most consistently collapsed in the popular Christian imagination. The Greek philosophical hope was the immortality of the soul — the liberation of the immaterial self from the prison of the body, the escape of the real person from the physical container they had been confined to during earthly life, the final freedom of pure spiritual existence from the corrupting influence of matter. This is not the Christian hope. It is almost the opposite of it.

The Christian hope is the resurrection of the body. Not the survival of the soul, not the continuation of a spiritual essence, not the persistence of consciousness after physical death. The resurrection — the same word used for what happened to Jesus on the third day — of the body. The specific, physical, material human person, transformed and glorified but genuinely continuous with the body that died, raised to an imperishable and glorious and powerful existence that the current body is the seed of rather than the final form of.

1 Corinthians 15 is the most sustained theological argument in the New Testament — forty-nine verses of Paul's most careful reasoning — and it is entirely about the resurrection of the body. The argument begins with the resurrection of Christ as the foundational fact: if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile. The resurrection of Christ is not a spiritual metaphor for the continuing influence of His teachings. It is a physical event — He appeared to Peter, to the Twelve, to five hundred brothers and sisters at once, to James, to all the apostles, and last of all to Paul himself. These are eyewitness testimonies of a physical appearance of a physically raised person. And from this foundation, Paul argues to the resurrection of those who belong to Christ: Christ has indeed been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep.

Verses 42–44 give us the most specific description of the resurrection body available in Scripture — and it is organised as four contrasts. Sown perishable, raised imperishable. The current body is subject to decay — the slow or sudden deterioration that every physical body experiences and that no medical intervention can ultimately prevent. The resurrection body will not decay. It will be imperishable — permanently, constitutively beyond the reach of the entropy that governs the current creation. Sown in dishonour, raised in glory. The current body, however well maintained, is subject to the dishonour of illness and weakness and death — the indignity that the fall introduced into the physical human experience. The resurrection body will be raised in glory — the doxa of God, the radiance of a body that has been transformed by the same power that fills the throne room of heaven. Sown in weakness, raised in power. The current body is limited — in strength, in endurance, in capacity, in the sheer amount of life it can hold. The resurrection body will be raised in power — the specific power of the One who brings everything under His control, applied to the transformation of the lowly body into the likeness of His glorious body. And sown a natural body, raised a spiritual body.

John 20:27 gives us the most important single piece of evidence about what the resurrection body actually is: Jesus invites Thomas to touch His hands and His side. The resurrection body has hands. It has a side. The wounds are present. The body is touchable, physical, real — not a ghost, not a vision, not a spiritual apparition. It is continuous with the body that was crucified — bearing the specific marks of the specific death it endured — and it is simultaneously transformed, glorified, capable of appearing behind locked doors, no longer subject to the limitations of the pre-resurrection physical existence. The wounds in the resurrection body of Jesus are the most important single testimony to the continuity between what is sown and what is raised — the marks on the glorified body telling the story of the seed from which the glory grew.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the hope that 1 Corinthians 15 places at the centre of the Christian life — not the vague spiritual survival of some immaterial self, but the specific, physical, glorious resurrection of the body that I currently inhabit in its perishable, weak, and dishonoured form. This body is the seed. The resurrection body is what the seed becomes. And the One who will transform my lowly body to be like His glorious body is the same One who raised Jesus from the dead — which means the power available for the transformation is the most decisive power that has ever operated in the physical universe.

Let this hope change how I treat my current body today — with the respect due to the seed of something glorious, with the care due to the temple of the Spirit, with the patience of a person who knows that what is sown in weakness will be raised in power. And let it change how I endure the specific weaknesses and limitations of this body — with the perspective of someone who can see the seed and the harvest simultaneously, because the same God who planted the seed has already revealed what the harvest will be.

Imperishable. Glorious. Powerful. Like His. That is what is coming.

## Daily Practices

- Read 1 Corinthians 15:1–58 in full — the complete resurrection chapter. Read it as a sustained argument rather than a collection of isolated verses — note how each section builds on the previous one, from the resurrection of Christ to the resurrection of believers to the nature of the resurrection body to the final defeat of death. Ask: what does this argument, taken as a whole, say about the place of the physical body in God's redemptive purpose?
- Read John 20:19–29 in full — the two resurrection appearances to the disciples, including Thomas. Note the specific physical details of both appearances: the wounds shown to the disciples, the breathing on them, the invitation to Thomas to touch. Ask: why does the risen Jesus still have wounds? What does the continuity of the wounds say about the continuity between the current body and the resurrection body?
- Journal: "How does the hope of the resurrection body change how I think about my current physical experience — illness, weakness, limitation, aging, the specific physical difficulties I am carrying right now? What difference does it make to know that this body is the seed of an imperishable, glorious, powerful body that will be like His glorious body?" Write the honest exploration.
- Read Romans 8:11 — and if the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead is living in you, he who raised Christ from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies because of his Spirit who lives in you. Ask: the same Spirit who raised Jesus is living in me right now. What does it mean for how I pray about physical healing, physical weakness, and the hope of the resurrection that the resurrection power is already resident in this body?
