## Revelation

Bartimaeus appears in Mark 10 in a single paragraph — twelve verses — and disappears from the biblical record as quickly as he arrived. He is given no backstory, no family history, no explanation of how long he had been blind or what had caused it. He is simply a blind beggar sitting by the road outside Jericho when Jesus passed by. And in twelve verses, he demonstrates a quality of faith that most believers who have walked with God for decades have not yet fully inhabited.

The first lesson is the lesson of persistence that will not be silenced. He began to shout — and the crowd rebuked him and told him to be quiet. The crowd's logic was probably reasonable: Jesus is passing through, He is surrounded by people, there is no reason to expect that a blind beggar by the road would receive His personal attention. The crowd was attempting to manage Bartimaeus's expectations. And he shouts all the more. The opposition of the crowd did not quiet his cry. It intensified it. The rebuking became the signal to increase the volume rather than reduce it. Many of the prayers that should have changed things have been abandoned not because God was not listening but because the crowd — the voice of discouragement, of reasonable expectation management, of don't get your hopes up — told them to be quiet and they were.

The second lesson is the lesson of the specific prayer. When Jesus called him and asked what he wanted, Bartimaeus did not offer a vague spiritual request. He did not say bless me, or help me, or whatever You think is best. He said: I want to see. Specific. Clear. Unambiguous. The prayer that knows exactly what it is asking and asks it directly. Jesus's question — what do you want me to do for you — is the same question He asked James and John two verses earlier. They gave an ambitious but vague answer about greatness. Bartimaeus gave a specific, personal, immediate answer about his deepest need. The specificity of the prayer is the evidence of the genuineness of the faith.

The third lesson is the lesson of the courageous response. When Jesus called him, the text says he threw aside his cloak and jumped to his feet and came to Jesus. The cloak is not an incidental detail. For a blind beggar in first-century Palestine, the cloak was both the equipment of his trade — spread on the ground to collect the coins people threw — and his most valuable possession. It represented his livelihood, his security, his only significant asset. He threw it aside. He did not carefully fold it and set it where he could find it again in case the healing did not come through. He threw it. The response to Jesus's call was immediate, whole-hearted, and costly — the abandonment of the one thing he had been depending on.

The fourth lesson is the lesson of the following that results from genuine healing. He received his sight and followed Jesus along the road. He did not return to his corner by the city gate, now as a sighted man able to pursue a different kind of livelihood. He followed Jesus on the road. The healing that Jesus gives is never merely the restoration of what was lost. It produces the following — the person who has been genuinely healed by an encounter with Jesus does not simply return to a better version of their former life. They follow Him.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to You today with the faith of Bartimaeus — the faith that will not be quieted by the reasonable expectations of the crowd, that knows specifically what it is asking and asks it directly, that throws aside the cloak in courageous response to Your call, and that follows You when the healing comes rather than returning to a better version of the former life.

Where I have been allowing the voice of discouragement — the crowd telling me to be quiet, to manage my expectations, to not get my hopes up — to silence the cry that You were actually ready to hear, restore my voice. Let me shout all the more in exactly the moment the opposition intensifies.

Give me the specificity of Bartimaeus's prayer. Not vague spiritual requests but the honest, direct, clear naming of what I actually need. I want to see. Let me say that clearly, in whatever form my specific blindness takes, and trust that the same Jesus who stopped for Bartimaeus is stopping for me.

## Daily Practices

- Read Mark 10:46–52 in full. Read it slowly and notice every specific detail — the road, the crowd, the cloak, the jumping up, the question, the answer, the following. Ask: which of the four lessons speaks most directly to where you are in your own faith right now?
- The crowd told Bartimaeus to be quiet. Ask: what are the voices in my life — internal or external — that are telling me to quiet my faith, manage my expectations, stop asking for what I am asking for? Name them specifically and decide today whether you will listen to them or shout all the more.
- Journal: "What is my cloak — the thing I am holding on to as security that I have not yet been willing to throw aside in courageous response to what Jesus is asking of me?" Name it honestly.
- Practise the specific prayer today. Instead of a general request for blessing or help, identify the one specific thing you most need Jesus to do and ask for it directly, in plain language, the way Bartimaeus did: I want to see. What is your equivalent of that sentence?
