## Revelation

The theology of the second chance runs through the entire biblical narrative like a thread that refuses to be cut. It is not a minor theme or a marginal comfort offered to the especially repentant. It is a core attribute of the God of Scripture — the God who specifically and repeatedly takes what was broken, wasted, lost, or destroyed and brings it back in a form that is often more fruitful than the original.

Joel 2:25 is the most direct statement of this attribute in the entire Old Testament: I will repay you for the years the locusts have eaten. The locust in the ancient world was the image of total devastation — the swarm that arrived and left nothing. Fields that had been years in the cultivating, stripped to the ground in hours. The work of seasons, gone. And God's word to the people who had experienced this devastation was not merely comfort — it was a specific, economic promise of repayment. Not that the lost years would be ignored. Not that the damage would be minimised. But that what was lost would be repaid.

The repayment of God does not work on the human accounting system. It does not give you back exactly what was taken and stop there. The pattern throughout Scripture is that the restoration exceeds the loss — that the Joseph who comes out of the prison is more prepared for the palace than the Joseph who was thrown into the pit, that the Peter who is restored after the denial becomes the preacher of Pentecost in a way that the Peter who had never denied might not have been, that the John Mark who deserted Paul on the first missionary journey becomes the writer of the Gospel that bears his name.

John 21:15–17 is perhaps the most intimate restoration scene in the entire New Testament — and it is intimate precisely because of what it costs both parties. Three times Peter denied. Three times Jesus asks: do you love me? The parallel is not accidental. Jesus is not rubbing salt into the wound — He is healing it at exactly the depth it was inflicted. The three-fold question undoes the three-fold denial not by ignoring it but by replacing it with a three-fold recommission. Feed my lambs. Tend my sheep. Feed my sheep. The restoration is not back to where Peter was before the denial. It is forward into the specific calling that the restored Peter is now more qualified to carry than the unbroken Peter would have been.

Acts 15:37–39 gives us John Mark — the deserter whom Paul refused to take on the second missionary journey, whose failure was significant enough to cause a sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas. And yet the same John Mark who caused that disagreement is the John Mark whom Paul, at the end of his life, asks Timothy to bring to him: he is helpful to me in my ministry. The deserter became the helper. The failure became the Gospel writer. The second chance produced something the first chance could not have.

The theology of the second chance is not the theology of consequences without consequences — it is the theology of a God who takes the consequence-bearing person and redeems them further than the consequence would have allowed them to go on their own. The locust years are real. The denial is real. The desertion is real. And God repays, restores, and recommissions from exactly the place of the worst decision.

If you are living in the aftermath of your worst decision — if the locusts have eaten what you spent years building, if you are on the other side of a denial or a desertion — Joel 2:25 is your word today. The God who repays years is the God of your years. The restoration is not behind you. It is ahead. And it will exceed what was lost.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I close this week with the word that is most needed in the aftermath of the worst decision — the word that says You repay the locust years, that You restore the deserter, that You recommission the denier. I bring You what the locusts have eaten — the years, the opportunities, the relationships, the purity, the time — and I receive Your promise not as comfort but as covenant: I will repay.

Let me be Peter this morning — not the Peter before the denial, but the Peter after the restoration. The one who has been asked three times and answered three times and received the commission that the unbroken version of himself could not yet carry. Take what was lost and repay. Take what was broken and restore. Take what was wasted and redeem it into something that no eye has yet seen.

The second chance is not behind me. It is ahead. I receive it today.

## Daily Practices

- Read Joel 2:18–27 in full — the complete restoration passage. Note that the repayment is specific and economic — God names the creatures that devastated and promises to repay what each one ate. Ask: what specifically has been eaten from your life that you need to bring before God with the specific faith that He repays specifically?
- Read John 21:1–19 in full — the complete restoration of Peter. Note that it happens over breakfast — in the ordinary, around a fire, with fish. Note the three questions. Note the three commissions. Ask: where in my own story has Jesus been asking me the same question three times — and what is the commission on the other side of the honest answer?
- Read Acts 15:36–41 and then 2 Timothy 4:11 together — the desertion and the restoration of John Mark. The gap between the two passages is years. Ask: what is the gap in my own story between the desertion and the restoration — and am I willing to stay in the process long enough for the restoration to be complete?
- Review the whole week — Feel Him or Not, The Best Is Yet to Come, A Promise from the Word, The Only Solid Ground, Choosing Joy, The Armour of God, When God Restores. Write one sentence for each day capturing what it produced in you. Seven sentences. This is your testimony of the week and the evidence that the God who restores has been at work in you through seven days of His word.
