## Revelation

The Bible is not a collection of loosely related religious teachings assembled over centuries by various authors with various agendas. It is one story — a single, unified, coherent narrative with a beginning, a catastrophe, a long and costly rescue operation, and an ending that is also a beginning. And the most important structural fact about that story is that it begins and ends in a garden. Genesis 2 gives us the garden of Eden — the place of the original, unbroken, unashamed communion between God and the people He made. Revelation 22 gives us the garden-city of the new Jerusalem — the place of the restored, perfected, permanent communion between God and the people He redeemed. Everything between Genesis 3 and Revelation 21 is the story of how the God who lost the garden in the catastrophe of the fall has been recovering it ever since.

Genesis 3:8 gives us the most heartbreaking verse in the entire Bible — and the most revealing. Then the man and his wife heard the sound of the Lord God as he was walking in the garden in the cool of the day. The walking in the garden in the cool of the day was not a one-time visit. The verb form implies a regular, habitual, ongoing practice — God walked in the garden in the cool of the day as a pattern, as a rhythm, as the daily expression of a relationship that was the point of the whole creation project. He made the garden because He wanted someone to walk with in it. He made the man and the woman because the garden was where He wanted to be with them. And the catastrophe of Genesis 3 is not primarily a moral catastrophe — though it is that. It is a relational catastrophe. The people who were made to walk with God in the garden are now hiding from Him among the trees. The communion has been broken. The garden has been lost.

And from that moment — from the first question God asks in the aftermath of the fall, where are you, which is not a request for geographical information but the anguished cry of the One who made this for communion and has found the communion broken — the entire Bible is the story of the recovery. The covenants with Noah, Abraham, Moses, and David are all stages of the recovery operation. The tabernacle and the temple are portable and permanent versions of the garden — the place where God dwells with His people. The incarnation is the ultimate expression of the recovery — God Himself entering the broken world, walking among the people who are no longer walking with Him in the garden, being Emmanuel, God-with-us, in the most personal and most costly way available. And the cross is the decisive act of the recovery — the moment when the barrier between God and humanity erected by the sin of Genesis 3 is removed by the death of the One who had no sin of His own.

Revelation 22:1–2 gives us the completion. The river of the water of life, flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb, down the middle of the great street of the city. On each side of the river, the tree of life — the tree that was blocked by the cherubim and the flaming sword in Genesis 3:24, the tree that humanity has been excluded from since the fall — bearing twelve crops of fruit, yielding its fruit every month, its leaves for the healing of the nations. The tree of life is accessible again. The exclusion is over. What was lost in the garden of Eden has been recovered in the garden-city of the new Jerusalem — not merely restored to its original state but elevated, expanded, perfected. The new Jerusalem is not plan B. It is the completion of plan A — the original intention of the God who made the garden because He wanted to walk with the people He loved, now fully, permanently, irreversibly accomplished.

Romans 8:20–21 gives us the cosmic scope of the recovery: the creation itself will be liberated from its bondage to decay and brought into the freedom and glory of the children of God. Not just human beings. The whole creation — the groaning world, the cursed ground, the frustrated creation that has been subjected to decay since Genesis 3 — is included in the recovery operation. The new Jerusalem is not the rescue of souls from a condemned physical world. It is the transformation of the physical world itself into the garden-city that God always intended it to become.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the story I am living inside — the one story of the Bible, the recovery operation that You have been running since the moment the garden was lost and You called out where are you in the cool of the evening. I am not an isolated individual navigating a random life. I am a person living inside the most important story ever told — the story of a God who made a garden for communion, lost it in catastrophe, and has been recovering it ever since at infinite cost.

Let me live today with the perspective of the full story — from the garden of Genesis to the garden-city of Revelation. Let the new Jerusalem be the fixed point on the horizon that gives meaning to everything between Eden and the end. And let the tree of life that the leaves of the nations will be healed by be the destination that orients every step I take in the direction of the One who planted the first garden because He wanted to walk with me in it.

You are still walking. I am no longer hiding.

## Daily Practices

- Read Genesis 2:4–3:24 in full — the complete garden narrative, from the planting of the garden through the fall and the expulsion. Read it slowly, as the opening act of the one story. Note every specific detail of what was lost — the unashamed nakedness, the walking with God, the access to the tree of life — and let the specificity of the loss inform the specificity of the hope.
- Read Revelation 21:1–22:5 in full — the complete new Jerusalem passage. Note every specific detail of what is recovered and what is new — no more sea, no more death, no more night, the tree of life accessible, the face of God seen directly, His name on their foreheads. Ask: how does the specificity of the recovery correspond to the specificity of the loss in Genesis 3?
- Journal: "Where in my own life do I most feel the effects of the Genesis 3 catastrophe — the broken communion, the frustrated creation, the hiding that the fall produced? And what does the Revelation 22 recovery say specifically to that place?" Write the honest connection.
- Read John 20:11–17 — Mary Magdalene at the tomb, mistaking Jesus for the gardener. Note that she is in a garden. Note that the risen Jesus is mistaken for the one who tends the garden. Ask: is the mistake accidental? What is John communicating by placing the resurrection encounter in a garden, with the risen Christ mistaken for a gardener?
