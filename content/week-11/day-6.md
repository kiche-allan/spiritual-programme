## Revelation

Acts 1:8 gives the Great Commission a geography: Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, ends of the earth. The order is deliberate and instructive. Jesus does not say start with the ends of the earth and work your way back. He says start in Jerusalem — the place you are already standing, the city you already know, the community already familiar with your face and your story. The global mandate begins with the local one. And the believer who is not a witness in their Jerusalem will not become one by moving to a different geography.

Your Jerusalem is wherever you already are. It is your household — the people who live under the same roof and see you before you have arranged yourself for public presentation. It is your workplace — the colleagues who know your ordinary, unperformed self better than most of your church community does. It is your neighbourhood — the people whose names you may or may not know but whose lives intersect with yours in the daily, unremarkable routines that constitute most of your existence. These are not the waiting room before the real mission. These are the mission. The person who is a faithful witness in their Jerusalem is already doing the most important evangelistic work available to them.

1 Peter 3:15–16 gives us the posture of the local witness: always be prepared to give an answer to everyone who asks you to give the reason for the hope that you have — but do this with gentleness and respect. Two things to notice. First, the witness begins with a question. Someone asks about the hope that is visible in your life. This means that the prerequisite for effective local witness is a life that is visibly, inexplicably different — a life that generates questions. If no one is asking about your hope, the question is not primarily about your evangelistic technique. It is about whether your life is displaying anything that requires an explanation. Second, the answer is given with gentleness and respect — not argument, not pressure, not the anxious urgency of someone whose job it is to close a sale. The witness is a person who has a reason, offers it honestly, and trusts the Spirit to do what only the Spirit can do.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I begin today in my Jerusalem. Not at the ends of the earth — there. In my household. At my workplace. In my neighbourhood. Among the people who already know my name and who are watching my life whether I have invited them to or not.

Let my life generate questions. Not through performance or spiritual showmanship, but through the genuine, visible, inexplicable difference that comes from actually living in the reality of everything we have studied this week — the community, the armour, the victory, the Commission. Let the people in my Jerusalem see something in me that they cannot explain apart from God. And when they ask — let me be ready. Not with a prepared speech, but with the honest, gentle, respectful telling of my own story.

Give me the courage to be a witness where I actually am. That is where it starts. That is where it stays until You send me further.

## Daily Practices

- Draw your Jerusalem. Literally — take a piece of paper and write your name in the centre. Around it, write the names of every person in your immediate relational world who does not yet know Jesus: family members, colleagues, neighbours, friends. This is your mission field. Look at it. Pray over it.
- Read Acts 2:46–47 — the early church met in the temple courts and in homes, and the Lord added to their number daily. The witness was both public and domestic. Ask: is my home a place of welcome and gospel hospitality? What would it look like to open it more deliberately to the people in my Jerusalem?
- Journal: "What is the hope in my life that is currently visible enough to generate questions? And if I am honest — is there enough visible difference in how I live that someone who does not know Jesus would have reason to ask about it?" Let this be a diagnostic rather than a condemnation.
- Practise your testimony today. Write it in three minutes: what your life was like before Christ, what happened when you encountered Him, and what is different now. Not a theological treatise — a story. Three minutes. Write it, read it aloud, trim it. This is your answer when someone asks about the hope you have.
