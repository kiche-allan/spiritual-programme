## Revelation

There is a second crisis that often runs alongside the first one — not the external circumstance itself, but the internal erosion that happens when the wait for resolution has gone on long enough that faith itself begins to crack. This is rarely discussed in church settings with the honesty it deserves, because it can feel like an admission of spiritual failure to say: I do not know if I still believe this is going to change. But Scripture is remarkably honest about this exact experience, and it is worth sitting with that honesty rather than rushing past it toward easy resolution.

Habakkuk opens his entire book with a complaint, not a worship song: how long, Lord, must I call for help, but you do not listen? This is a prophet — someone with a direct, established relationship with God, someone whose job was to speak God's words to others — and his opening posture toward God is frustration bordering on accusation. The Bible did not edit this out. It is preserved as the first chapter of a book that bears his name, which tells us something important: the honest cry of how long is not a disqualifying failure of faith. It is itself a form of faith — because Habakkuk is still speaking to God even in his frustration. He has not walked away. He is arguing with the One he still, underneath the frustration, believes is listening.

Psalm 13 gives us David's version of the same crisis, compressed into four devastating questions in two verses: how long will you forget me, how long will you hide your face, how long must I wrestle, how long must I have sorrow. This is not a psalm of confident trust. It is a psalm of someone who has been waiting long enough that the waiting itself has started to feel like abandonment. And yet — this is included in the inspired songbook of Israel. It was sung. It was prayed by generations of believers who needed exactly this language for exactly this experience. Your honest exhaustion with waiting has biblical precedent and biblical permission.

Mark 9:24 gives us perhaps the most relatable line in the entire Gospels — a father, desperate for his son's healing, says to Jesus: I do believe; help me overcome my unbelief. He does not pretend to have unshakeable faith. He names both realities simultaneously — I do believe, and I need help with the unbelief that is also genuinely present. Jesus does not rebuke him for this contradiction. He heals the boy. Faith that is honest about its own fragility is not lesser faith. It is often the most realistic kind available to someone who has been waiting a long time.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to You today with an honesty I have sometimes been afraid to bring into prayer — the honesty of how long. I have been waiting longer than I expected to, and the waiting itself has started to wear down something in me that used to feel more certain. I do not want to pretend my faith is stronger right now than it actually is.

Like Habakkuk, I bring my complaint rather than walking away. Like David, I ask the questions that feel almost too raw for prayer. And like the father in Mark 9, I say both things at once: I do believe, and I need help with the part of me that does not. Receive this honesty as the faith that it actually is — not its absence.

Help me overcome my unbelief today. Not by pretending it is not there, but by bringing it directly to You, the way every honest believer in Scripture eventually had to.

## Daily Practices

- Read Habakkuk 1 and 2 in full. Note the progression — complaint, God's response, a second complaint, and then Habakkuk's resolve in 2:1 to stand at his watch and wait to see how God will respond. The honesty did not disqualify him from eventually arriving at trust. It was the path toward it.
- Read Psalm 13 in full — note that it is only six verses, and the final two verses pivot from despair to trust: but I trust in your unfailing love; my heart rejoices in your salvation. Ask: is it possible to hold both the how long of the first four verses and the trust of the final two simultaneously, rather than waiting for the first to fully resolve before allowing the second?
- Journal: "What is the specific thing I have been waiting for that has gone on long enough to wear down my faith? What does my honest current state of belief actually look like — not the version I present, but the version that is true?" Write it without shame.
- Pray the Mark 9:24 prayer today as your own: I do believe; help me overcome my unbelief. Say it exactly as it is, even if it feels contradictory. Let the contradiction be the prayer rather than something to resolve before you pray.
