## Revelation

The New Testament has no category for the solitary Christian. This is one of the most countercultural claims of the biblical witness in an age that celebrates individual spirituality, personal faith journeys, and the private management of one's relationship with God without the inconvenience of other people. The person who says I am spiritual but not religious, I follow Jesus but not the church, I have my own relationship with God that does not require the community — that person has constructed a version of Christian faith that the New Testament does not recognise. Not because the New Testament is institutionalist or because the church as an organisation is the necessary mediator of salvation. But because the Christian life as the New Testament describes it is inherently, structurally, irreducibly communal — it can only be fully lived in the company of others who are also living it.

The reason is not primarily pragmatic — not merely that accountability and support and encouragement are helpful, though they are. The reason is theological. The church is the body of Christ. Not a collection of individuals who happen to share beliefs and gather occasionally. A body — an organism in which each part is essential to the functioning of the whole, in which the health of any member affects the health of every other member, in which the growth of the body requires the contribution of each part. Ephesians 4:15–16 describes this with the most vivid organic imagery available: the whole body, joined and held together by every supporting ligament, grows and builds itself up in love as each part does its work. The growth is the growth of the whole body — not the individual spiritual development of each member in parallel — and it requires the work of each part. The part that withdraws from the body does not flourish in isolation. It atrophies. And the body from which it has withdrawn is diminished by the absence of the contribution only that part could make.

Hebrews 10:24–25 connects the gathering with the approaching Day — not giving up meeting together, and all the more as you see the Day approaching. The logic is not immediately obvious: why should the approaching return of Christ be an argument for more community rather than for the private preparation of the individual soul? Because the church that is gathered — spurring one another on toward love and good deeds, encouraging one another, holding one another in the accountability of genuine relationship — is the church that will be ready. The individual who has been preparing privately, without the friction and the formation and the challenge and the gift that only genuine community provides, will be less ready. The Day is coming. Meet together all the more.

Acts 2:42 gives us the simplest and most complete description of what the gathering actually looks like when it is functioning as designed: they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching, to fellowship, to the breaking of bread, and to prayer. Four things. The word — the sustained, consistent, communal engagement with the Scriptures that forms a shared theological framework. Fellowship — the koinonia, the genuine mutual sharing of life, resources, and experience that goes far beyond the friendly greeting at the Sunday service. The breaking of bread — the Lord's Supper, the regular communal participation in the reminder of what the community is gathered around and at what cost. And prayer — the communal addressing of God together, the shared acknowledgment of dependence and the shared expression of trust.

The church that the New Testament envisions is not the programme-heavy organisation that many contemporary churches have become — where people attend services, consume spiritual content, and participate in organised activities without ever being genuinely known by the people sitting beside them. It is the community of Acts 2 — where everything was held in common, where the breaking of bread happened in homes, where the learning and the fellowship and the prayer were woven into the fabric of daily life rather than scheduled into the Sunday programme. This is the church that the world cannot explain, that the watching nations are drawn to, that produces the growth that Acts 2:47 describes: the Lord added to their number daily those who were being saved.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the irreducibly communal nature of the Christian life You have called me to. Not the private, individualised, self-managed spirituality that my culture celebrates, but the body life of the gathered community — the spurring on and the encouraging and the devoted fellowship and the breaking of bread and the prayer that can only happen in the company of others who are also genuinely following You.

Where I have been withdrawing from community — because it is inconvenient, because it is imperfect, because the friction of genuine relationship is costly and the distance is easier — bring me back. And where I have been attending without belonging, occupying a seat without occupying a place in the body, consuming the service without contributing the part that only I can contribute — challenge me to the fuller participation that the body requires and that my own formation needs.

The Day is approaching. Let me meet together all the more. And let the meeting be genuine — the apostles' teaching and fellowship and the breaking of bread and prayer, not the managed, surface-level contact that looks like community from the outside and produces nothing from the inside.

## Daily Practices

- Read Acts 2:42–47 in full — the portrait of the early church in its most vital expression. Note every specific practice listed and every specific outcome described: the awe, the wonders and signs, the holding of everything in common, the daily meeting in the temple courts, the breaking of bread in homes, the glad and sincere hearts, the praising of God, and the daily addition to their number. Ask: which of these practices is most present in my current experience of church? Which is most absent?
- Read 1 Corinthians 12:12–27 — the body metaphor in full. Note especially verse 22: the parts that seem weaker are indispensable. Ask: what does it mean that the apparently weak parts of the body are indispensable? Which parts of the body of Christ I am part of am I most tempted to treat as dispensable — and which part of the body am I most tempted to treat myself as?
- Journal: "What is the honest state of my relationship with the specific community of believers I am part of? Am I genuinely known by any of them — known in the full, uncomfortable, accountable sense that genuine community requires? Or am I attending without belonging, present without being genuinely part of?" Write the honest audit.
- Reach out to one specific person in your church community today — not a general connection, but a specific, intentional, genuine investment in a relationship within the body. A phone call, a visit, a meal, a specific act of service or encouragement that costs you something. Let it be the beginning of the genuine belonging that the body requires.
