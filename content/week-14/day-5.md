## Revelation

Scripture consistently connects faithful waiting with reward — not as a transactional formula where enough patience guarantees a specific outcome, but as the natural, organic unfolding of character, breakthrough, and intimacy that genuinely cannot be produced any other way. The reward is not arbitrary compensation for suffering through the wait. It is the fruit that the wait itself, properly engaged, was always going to produce.

Abraham is the paradigm case in Hebrews 6:13–15. He waited decades for the promised son — long enough that by any natural measure the promise should have expired with his and Sarah's fertility. And the text says simply: after waiting patiently, Abraham received what was promised. The emphasis is on the patience as the bridge between the promise and the receiving. Not because the patience earned the promise — Abraham did nothing to deserve Isaac. But because the patience was the space in which Abraham's faith was tested, refined, and ultimately demonstrated to be the kind of faith Romans 4 says was credited to him as righteousness. The wait was not wasted time before the real story began. The wait was where the real story was happening.

Joseph's narrative across thirteen years — from the pit to slavery to false imprisonment to forgotten obscurity to, finally, the second-highest position in Egypt — shows the same pattern with even more dramatic stakes. Genesis 41:46 tells us Joseph was thirty years old when he stood before Pharaoh; he had been seventeen when his brothers sold him. Thirteen years. And the man who emerged from those thirteen years was not merely older. He was equipped — with the administrative wisdom, the emotional depth, and the theological maturity that would let him say to his brothers years later, you intended to harm me, but God intended it for good. That sentence could not have been spoken by the seventeen-year-old who was thrown into the pit. It could only be spoken by the thirty-year-old who had been formed by the thirteen years between.

Hannah's years of barrenness, met with ridicule and grief, produced not only Samuel but a depth of prayer and praise — recorded in 1 Samuel 2 — that became one of the templates for Mary's Magnificat centuries later. Simeon and Anna, who appear in Luke 2 as elderly figures who had waited their entire lives to see the Messiah, were given the specific privilege of recognising and blessing the infant Jesus in the temple — a privilege available only to people who had waited decades without giving up. James 5:7–8 gives us the image that ties all of these together: the farmer who waits for the land to yield its valuable crop, patiently waiting for the rains. The farmer does not wait passively — he waits through the necessary process, knowing that the crop cannot be rushed without being ruined. The reward is not separate from the waiting. It grows out of it, in its own proper time.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the truth that the wait I am in is not wasted time before the real story begins. It is where the real story is happening. Abraham's faith was not merely tested by decades of waiting — it was formed by them, into the kind of faith that became the pattern for all who would believe after him. Joseph's character was not merely preserved through thirteen hard years — it was built by them, into the wisdom that would save a nation.

I do not know exactly what You are forming in me through this season of waiting. But I trust, like the farmer who cannot rush the harvest without ruining it, that the timing of this wait is not arbitrary. Let me not become weary in doing good, trusting that at the proper time — Your proper time, not mine — the harvest will come.

Let this season produce in me what only this season could produce. I do not want to rush past the forming in my hurry to reach the resolved.

## Daily Practices

- Read Genesis 37 and 39–45 in a single sitting if possible — Joseph's complete journey. Note every transition point and ask: what specific quality or capacity did each stage of the wait seem to be building in him, that the next stage required?
- Read Luke 2:25–38 — Simeon and Anna. Note that both are described as elderly, having waited their entire lives. Ask: what does their example teach about the relationship between the length of a wait and the significance of what is eventually received?
- Journal: "What is the wait I am currently in? If I trust that it is forming something in me rather than merely delaying something I am owed, what might that forming look like? What capacity, character, or depth might this season be building that a shorter or different path could not have built?"
- Read Galatians 6:9 aloud as a declaration over your current circumstances: let us not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up. Identify one specific way you are tempted to give up right now, and choose, today, not to.
