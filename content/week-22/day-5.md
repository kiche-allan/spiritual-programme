## Revelation

Proverbs 18:21 is one of the most sober statements in the wisdom literature — and one of the most consistently underestimated in the daily practice of the Christian life. The tongue has the power of life and death. Not the power of pleasantness or unpleasantness. Not the power of helpfulness or unhelpfulness. Life and death — the most ultimate categories available in human experience, applied to the instrument that most people treat as the least consequential part of their existence. Words are not just sounds. They are not merely the expression of thoughts that would exist and do their work regardless of whether they were spoken. They have power — the power to build and to destroy, to heal and to wound, to create the conditions for flourishing and to create the conditions for devastation — and the person who has not reckoned with that power is someone who is wielding a weapon they do not know they are carrying.

The theology of speech begins in Genesis 1 — where God creates by speaking. He said, and it was. The creative power of the universe is exercised through the word. Human beings, made in the image of God, carry a derivative version of this creative capacity. Not the omnipotent, ex nihilo creation of Genesis 1 — but the genuine, significant, consequential power of the word spoken into the relational and spiritual environment of the person's life. What you say about yourself, about your situation, about other people, and about God has weight that most people have not accounted for. The words spoken over a child in their formative years — whether of affirmation or of shame, of possibility or of limitation — shape the person that child becomes in ways that decades of subsequent experience struggle to fully undo. The words spoken in the heat of an argument create wounds that the subsequent apology acknowledges but cannot always heal. The words spoken in prayer — the specific, faith-grounded declarations of what God is and what He has promised — have a different quality from the words spoken in anxiety and complaint, and the difference is not merely psychological.

Matthew 12:36–37 introduces the most sobering dimension of the theology of speech: everyone will have to give account on the day of judgment for every empty word they have spoken. The Greek for empty word is argos logos — literally idle word, the word that has done no work, that has been spoken without purpose or care or love. And the accounting is complete: by your words you will be acquitted and by your words you will be condemned. Jesus places the words in the category of the things that will be examined at the end — alongside the deeds, alongside the secret things, alongside the thoughts of the heart. The words are the evidence. They reveal what is actually inside the person who spoke them — because the mouth speaks from the overflow of the heart, as Jesus says in the same passage.

James 3:9–10 gives us the most specific and most practically urgent expression of the tongue's inconsistency: with the tongue we praise our Lord and Father, and with it we curse human beings who have been made in God's likeness. Out of the same mouth come praise and cursing. My brothers and sisters, this should not be. The incongruity that James identifies is not merely ethical. It is theological. To curse a human being — to speak words designed to diminish, to wound, to destroy the worth of someone made in the image of God — is to do something categorically inconsistent with the worship of the God whose image they bear. You cannot simultaneously honour the original and desecrate the image. The person you are cursing is the image of the One you are praising. Out of the same mouth come praise and cursing. This should not be.

What the tongue is most consistently used for reveals the state of the heart most accurately. The person whose default speech about themselves is the speech of defeat and unworthiness has a heart that has not yet inhabited the new creation identity of 2 Corinthians 5:17. The person whose default speech about others is critical and diminishing has a heart that has not yet been fully formed by the love that 1 Corinthians 13 describes. The person whose speech about their circumstances consistently contradicts what they declare in worship has a heart that has not yet integrated faith and ordinary life. And the person whose speech about God — in the private conversation of daily life, not only in the formal context of worship — is full of the specific, confident, grateful declaration of who He is and what He has done and what He has promised — that person's heart has been shaped by a theology of the tongue that has made the most of the power that Proverbs 18:21 describes.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I bring to You today the instrument I most consistently underestimate — my tongue. The words I speak about myself, about my situation, about the people around me, about You. I receive today the weight that Proverbs 18:21 assigns to every word — the life and the death that are in the power of this small instrument.

Set a guard over my mouth today — not a lock, but a guard. The filter of love that considers what a word will produce before it is spoken. The wisdom that knows when to speak and when to be silent. The faith that speaks what You have said rather than what the circumstances are insisting. And the grace that uses the tongue primarily for what it was made for — the praise of the One whose image every person I encounter today bears.

Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in Your sight. Today. In every conversation. In every moment where the tongue has the power of life and I choose to use it for life.

## Daily Practices

- Read James 3:1–12 in full — the complete theology of the tongue. Note the three images James uses: the bit in the horse's mouth, the rudder of a ship, and the small spark that sets a great forest on fire. Ask: which of these images most accurately describes the role the tongue plays in the specific area of your life where it does the most damage?
- Read Ephesians 4:29–32 together — the instruction about unwholesome talk and the instruction about not grieving the Holy Spirit in the same breath. Note the positive standard Paul gives: only what is helpful for building others up according to their needs, that it may benefit those who listen. Ask: if I applied this standard to every word I spoke today, what would change?
- Journal: "What are the default patterns of my speech — about myself, about my circumstances, about the people closest to me, about God? Which patterns are producing life and which are producing death? Where is the gap between what I declare in worship and what I say in the ordinary conversation of daily life?" Write the honest audit.
- Practise the life-giving tongue today in three specific ways: speak one word of genuine, specific affirmation to someone who needs to hear it; speak one declaration of faith about your situation that aligns with what God has said rather than what the circumstances are insisting; and catch one word that was about to be spoken carelessly and choose not to speak it. Let each of these be a deliberate, conscious act rather than an accident.
