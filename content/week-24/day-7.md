## Revelation

Matthew 11:28 is the most generous invitation in the Gospels — and the most structurally simple. Come to me. Two words. No conditions attached to the coming. No list of prerequisites to be met before the invitation is valid. No specification of the spiritual state required for the coming to be received. All you who are weary and burdened — which is not a narrowing of the invitation but a broadening of it, a description capacious enough to include every human being who has been carrying the weight of being alive in a fallen world. The invitation is to the weary. To the burdened. To the people who are not managing well, who have not got it together, who are not arriving at the destination in the condition that the destination seemed to require. Come. To me.

The week has covered a great deal of territory. The confidence of goodness in the land of the living — and the waiting that the confidence requires. The ministry of presence — and the cost of staying when the staying is harder than the leaving. The Lord's Prayer as a daily framework — and the sustained engagement that praying through rather than reciting requires. The contentment that is learned in prison — and the school that the learning requires. The armor of light — and the wakefulness to the hour that the eschatological urgency demands. The wounds of God — and the healing that the permanent wounds make available. Each theme has made its demands. Each has invited the kind of engagement that takes something from the person who brings it. And the closing day is the invitation that receives everything that the week has required and gives something that the week has not yet fully offered.

I will give you rest. The rest of Matthew 11:28 is not the rest of sleep — the cessation of activity. It is the rest of anapausis in Greek — the refreshment, the recovery, the specific restoration of a depleted person to a state from which they can continue. The weary who come are not simply told to stop. They are given rest — the active provision of what the weariness has depleted. And then in verse 29: take my yoke upon you and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. The yoke is not the removal of all burden. It is the exchange of one burden for another — the heavy, ill-fitting burden of carrying life under the conditions of a fallen world in one's own strength, exchanged for the easy and light burden of carrying it in the company of the One whose shoulder is under the other side of the yoke.

Isaiah 55:1 gives us the Old Testament echo of the same invitation — and it is addressed specifically to the people who have nothing to bring. Come, all you who are thirsty. Come to the waters. You who have no money — come, buy and eat. The economy of the invitation is the economy of grace — the thing that is being offered is available to those who cannot pay for it, which means it is available to everyone who needs it, which means it is available to everyone. The thirsty. The hungry. The ones who have spent their money on what is not bread and their labour on what does not satisfy. Come. The waters are here. The wine and the milk are here. Without money and without cost.

Revelation 22:17 gives us the final invitation of the entire Bible — the last word before the closing benediction of the entire canonical Scripture. The Spirit and the bride say: come. And let the one who hears say: come. Let the one who is thirsty come; and let the one who wishes take the free gift of the water of life. The invitation has not expired. After sixty-six books and thousands of years of history and the full sweep of the story from the first garden to the last — the invitation is still open. The Spirit is still saying it. The bride is still saying it. And the appropriate response of everyone who has heard it is to say it to everyone who has not yet heard: come.

The week ends here — not at the summit of a theological achievement but at the foot of the invitation. Not at the arrival point of the disciplines practised and the contentment learned and the armor put on. At the beginning. At the come. Because the come is not only the beginning of the Christian life. It is its permanent posture — the ongoing, repeated, daily return to the One who gave rest the last time and who gives it again now. Come to me. All you who are weary and burdened. And I will give you rest.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I close this week at the invitation — the simplest and the most complete word available to me. Come. All I am and all I carry: the weight of the goodness I am still waiting to see, the cost of the presence I have been offering, the sustained engagement of the prayer framework, the school of contentment I am still in, the wakefulness to the hour that the darkness makes difficult, and the wounds I am still carrying. All of it comes with me.

And You say: come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest. I come. Not with the impressive portfolio of spiritual achievement. With the weariness and the burden and the genuine need of a person who has been in the week and is still carrying what they were carrying when it began. And I receive what You promise: rest. Not the end of the journey. The refreshment of a person who has been in the yoke with You, whose burden has been easy and light precisely because Your shoulder has been under the other side.

I come. Again. As always. Because this is the posture — the ongoing, repeated, daily return to the One who gives rest every time I come and has never once turned away the weary and the burdened who arrived. Come. I am coming. Here I am.

## Amen

In Jesus' name — and with the gratitude of a week received and a rest given — Amen.

## Daily Practices

- Read Matthew 11:25–30 in full — the complete passage including the hidden-from-the-wise and revealed-to-little-children section that precedes the invitation. Note the connection: the things that are hidden from the wise and learned are revealed to little children — and the invitation that follows is the invitation to the childlike posture of coming with nothing except the need. Ask: what would it look like to come to Jesus today with the simplicity and the empty-handedness of a little child rather than with the theological sophistication of someone who has been a Christian for a long time?
- Review the entire week — the goodness of God in the land of the living, the ministry of presence, the Lord's Prayer as a daily framework, contentment, the armor of light, the wounds of God, and the come of Matthew 11:28 — and write one sentence for each day that begins with come: "Come — and bring the specific weight of what each day has addressed." Seven come sentences. Let them be the prayer that closes the week.
- Journal: "What am I carrying as I arrive at the end of this week — what weight, what weariness, what specific burden that has not yet been resolved by anything the week has offered? What does it mean to bring specifically that — the unresolved, the still-heavy, the not-yet-seen-goodness — to the invitation of Matthew 11:28? What does the come look like for me today, specifically?" Write the honest bringing.
- Sit with Matthew 11:28–30 for ten minutes — not studying it, not analysing it, but receiving it. Read it once slowly. Then close your eyes and let the come be the posture of the next ten minutes. Let the weariness be present without managing it. Let the burden be acknowledged without fixing it. And receive the rest that is promised to the ones who come — not the resolution of the circumstances, but the refreshment of the Person who gives rest every time the weary arrive.
