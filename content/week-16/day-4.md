## Revelation

The Lord's Supper happened on the most important night in the history of the world — the night before the crucifixion, in an upper room in Jerusalem, with twelve men who did not yet understand what the next eighteen hours would bring. And what Jesus chose to do with that night — the last night He would spend with His disciples before everything changed — was not a final lecture, not a systematic theology, not a comprehensive set of instructions for the church He was about to leave behind. He chose a meal. He broke bread. He poured wine. He washed feet. He sat with His friends at a table and gave them something to do every time they gathered in His name — something physical, something shared, something that would carry the full weight of what He was about to do every time it was repeated until He came back.

The first lesson of the Lord's Supper is the lesson of the body broken and the blood poured out — grace that is not abstract but specific, not theoretical but physical, not general but given for you. Luke 22:19 records the exact words: this is my body given for you. The you is personal. Not given for humanity in general, in a way that your individual need is lost in the crowd. Given for you — for the specific person holding the bread, in the specific moment of their specific need, with the specific weight of their specific sin and failure and struggle already absorbed into the body that is being broken.

The second lesson is the lesson of remembrance — do this in remembrance of me. The Lord's Supper is the practice of a deliberate, repeated, corporate memory. In a world that forgets — that forgets what it has received, forgets what it cost, forgets the One who paid the cost — the table is the recurring, embodied, undeniable act of not forgetting. Every time the bread is broken and the cup is taken, the church is saying: we have not forgotten. We remember what You did. We remember the body. We remember the blood. We remember the night when everything changed and the morning when everything was made new.

The third lesson is the lesson of the foot washing — which preceded the supper and cannot be separated from it. John 13 records Jesus rising from the table, removing His outer clothing, wrapping a towel around His waist, and washing the feet of twelve men — including the man He knew would betray Him within hours, and the man He knew would deny Him before the night was over. The towel and basin in the hands of the Son of God is the most dramatic portrait of servant humility in all of Scripture. And His instruction is direct: I have set you an example that you should do as I have done for you. The Lord's Supper is not only received at the table. It is expressed in the towel. Every act of genuine servant love toward another person is a participation in what Jesus modelled in that upper room.

The fourth lesson is the lesson of the new covenant — this cup is the new covenant in my blood. The old covenant was sealed with the blood of animals on an altar. The new covenant is sealed with the blood of the Son of God on a cross. Everything the old covenant promised but could not fully deliver — the forgiveness of sins, the law written on the heart, the direct and unmediated knowledge of God — the new covenant accomplishes. The cup is the seal of a covenant that cannot be broken because the One who signed it with His own blood is the same One who rose from the dead and lives to enforce it.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to the table today — not physically but spiritually — and I receive what Jesus gave when He broke the bread and poured the cup. His body, given for me specifically. His blood, poured out for my sins specifically. The new covenant sealed in the most costly way available, so that I would never have to wonder whether it would hold.

Let the remembrance that the table requires produce in me what remembrance was always meant to produce: gratitude that does not fade, humility that does not perform, and love that expresses itself in the towel rather than reserving itself for the impressive moments. Let me carry the spirit of the upper room into today — the spirit of a servant who has been served by Someone greater, and who now serves others from the overflow of having been served.

Do this in remembrance of me. I remember today. I will not forget.

## Daily Practices

- Read Luke 22:14–23 and John 13:1–17 together — the foot washing and the institution of the Supper as one continuous event. Note the flow: Jesus washes feet, then breaks bread, then pours the cup. The humility and the sacrifice are inseparable. Ask: what does it mean that the table is preceded by the towel?
- Read 1 Corinthians 11:23–32 — Paul's full teaching on the Lord's Supper. Note verse 28 — let a person examine himself before eating. Ask: when you last took communion, was there genuine self-examination? What would that examination honestly reveal today?
- Journal: "What does the Lord's Supper mean to me personally — not what I know it means theologically, but what it actually means when I participate? Is it a routine, or is it still the weight of bread and cup carrying the full freight of what they represent?"
- Identify one person in your life you could serve in a practical, unglamorous, towel-and-basin way this week — the kind of service that no one will applaud, that requires stooping rather than standing. Do it as an act of participation in what Jesus modelled in the upper room.
