## Revelation

Dallas Willard made a distinction that has the quality of something that has always been true but has rarely been articulated so clearly — the distinction between trying and training. The person who wants to run a marathon does not achieve it by trying very hard on the day of the race. They achieve it by training — by running distances that are shorter than the marathon, consistently, over months, building the endurance and the capacity that the marathon requires. The trying on the day of the race is the expression of the training that preceded it. Without the training, the trying produces only exhaustion and failure. With the training, the trying is made possible by a capacity that the training built.

The spiritual disciplines are the training. They are the specific, sustained, intentional practices — prayer, Scripture reading, fasting, silence, solitude, worship, service, community, confession — that form the soul into the kind of soul that does naturally and spontaneously what the untrained soul cannot do even with great effort. The person who wants to love their enemy cannot typically achieve it by trying very hard in the moment of encounter with the enemy. But the person who has been practising the disciplines that form a loving heart — who has been praying for enemies regularly, who has been fasting the claim of their own preferences, who has been sitting in silence long enough to hear the voice that produces love — that person has been trained toward the capacity for love that makes the moment of the encounter different.

1 Timothy 4:7–8 gives us the most direct instruction about the disciplines in the Pauline corpus: train yourself to be godly. The Greek word for train is gymnazo — from which our word gymnasium derives. It is the word for the rigorous, sustained, physically demanding training of an athlete. Train yourself to be godly — not aspire to godliness, not intend to be godly, not occasionally make efforts toward godliness when circumstances are favourable. Train — with the consistency and the rigour and the commitment to a process that the word gymnazo implies. And the reason is given in the comparison: physical training is of some value, but godliness has value for all things, holding promise for both the present life and the life to come. The training that forms the soul for godliness has a higher return on investment than any other training available to a human being — not because physical training is worthless but because godliness benefits both the current life and the eternal one.

Hebrews 12:11 gives us the honest acknowledgment of what training costs: no discipline seems pleasant at the time, but painful. The spiritual disciplines are not comfortable. Silence is uncomfortable for the person whose inner life is noisy. Fasting is uncomfortable for the person whose comfort is food. Solitude is uncomfortable for the person who has been hiding from themselves in company. Confession is uncomfortable for the person who has been managing their image. The discomfort is not a sign that the discipline is wrong. It is a sign that the discipline is working — that it is applying the specific pressure that produces the specific formation that the easy path cannot produce. Later on, however, it produces a harvest of righteousness and peace for those who have been trained by it. The harvest is the payoff of the training — the spontaneous, natural, effortless expression of a character that has been formed by the sustained, effortful, sometimes painful practice of the disciplines.

Luke 5:16 gives us the most practically instructive piece of information about the disciplines available in the Gospels: but Jesus often withdrew to lonely places and prayed. Jesus — the Son of God, in whom the fullness of the Godhead dwelt bodily, who had the Spirit without measure — often withdrew. The word often implies regularity, habit, the consistent practice of someone for whom the withdrawal was not an occasional retreat from the demands of ministry but the sustaining rhythm from which the ministry flowed. If the Son of God maintained a disciplined practice of withdrawal and prayer as the foundation of His public ministry, the follower of Christ who is too busy for the disciplines has misunderstood something fundamental about the relationship between the private formation and the public expression.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the invitation to train — not to try harder in the moment of the test, but to build, through consistent, sustained practice, the kind of soul that does naturally in the moment what the untrained soul cannot do even with great effort. Train me in the disciplines that form the soul for godliness. Let the discomfort of the training be received not as the evidence that something is wrong but as the evidence that something is being formed.

Where I have been trying without training — attempting the performance without doing the work that makes the performance possible — redirect me. Where I have been hoping for the harvest without tending the crop — expecting the fruit of disciplines I have not practised — correct the expectation by correcting the practice. And where I have allowed the busyness of the public life to crowd out the private formation that the public life requires, restore the rhythm of the withdrawal and the prayer that Jesus modelled as the necessary foundation of everything He did.

Train me. It will be painful. And the harvest will be worth it.

## Daily Practices

- Read Luke 5:12–16 — the healing of the leper and the withdrawal to pray, in immediate sequence. Note that Jesus healed the leper and then withdrew to pray — the public ministry and the private discipline are directly connected. The withdrawal follows the healing, not the other way around. Ask: what does it say about the sustainability of public ministry that even after a miracle, Jesus immediately withdrew to pray?
- Read Psalm 1 in full — the portrait of the person who meditates on God's law day and night, who is like a tree planted by streams of water. Note that the fruitfulness is the natural result of the location — the tree does not strain to produce fruit. It is planted in the right place, and the fruit is the inevitable consequence of the planting. Ask: what is the discipline equivalent of being planted by streams of water — the specific practice that positions me to receive what produces the fruit?
- Journal: "Which spiritual discipline is most absent from my current practice — and what is the specific fruit that its absence is preventing? What would it look like to begin the training in that discipline today — not the marathon level immediately, but the first consistent, sustained step toward the capacity that the discipline would build?" Write the specific plan.
- Choose one discipline to practise deliberately today — not as a general aspiration but as a specific act at a specific time. If it is prayer, set a specific time and a specific duration and do not negotiate with yourself when the time arrives. If it is silence, find fifteen minutes of genuine, device-free quiet. If it is fasting, identify the meal and the purpose. Do the training today, even if it is small. The consistency is more important than the duration.
