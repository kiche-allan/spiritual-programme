Think about the last twenty-four hours. What was the first thing you reached for when you woke up, and what was the last thing you looked at before you slept?

For most of us, the answer is the same device in both cases. We do not need a sermon to tell us that. What we need is to understand what that habit is doing to us, and why it matters to God.

That is why a talk by Arthur Brooks stayed with me. Brooks is a social scientist who teaches the science of happiness at Harvard and Vanderbilt. Speaking at the ARC 2026 conference in London, he argued from the start that Western civilisation faces something deeper than a political or social crisis. It is a crisis of meaning. I want to take his argument seriously, and then ask what Scripture says about it. I think it says a great deal.

## Two questions that expose us

In his talk, Brooks describes a quiz he gives his students. It has two questions. Why are you alive? And for what would you give your life? You pass if you have answers. You fail if you say, "I have never thought about that," or "those questions have no answers."

Then he makes his observation. For years his students could answer. Then they started to fail. He points to data showing a sharp rise, from around 2008 and 2009, in young adults who say their lives feel meaningless, and links it to the arrival of the smartphone.

We should be careful here. Correlation is not proof, and he is describing studies from his own context. But look at what he is really pointing to. People can be fed, employed, connected and entertained, and still not know why they exist. A church member in Nairobi can attend three services a week, belong to four WhatsApp groups and still be unable to answer those two questions. If that is true of us, we have a problem no data plan can fix.

## Complicated and complex

Here is the heart of his argument. Brooks uses the idea that the two halves of the brain do different work. The left, he says, handles complicated problems: things, information, the "how" and "what" of life. A complicated problem can be solved. If you do not know how your car works, you can watch a video or find a mechanic.

The right side, he says, deals with complex problems, which are easy to understand and impossible to solve: love, meaning, mystery and faith. His example is his marriage. After thirty-five years it is still not "solved", and he says that is exactly why he loves it. You do not solve a marriage. You live in it.

A word of honesty. Many neuroscientists would say the popular "left brain, right brain" picture is far too simple, and you do not need to accept it to follow the argument. Treat it as a picture, not a scan. The real point stands without it: life contains problems you can solve and mysteries you can only enter, and a healthy person needs both.

Brooks then says that when we check our phones (he cites about 205 times a day for the average American) we push our attention toward information and things and away from mystery and meaning. He calls the result a "simulation": a copy of friendship, love and purpose that leaves us starving for the real thing.

## The Bible already named this

Now put that beside Scripture. Through the prophet Jeremiah, God says: "My people have committed two evils: they have forsaken me, the fountain of living waters, and hewed out cisterns for themselves, broken cisterns that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13).

That is the simulation, three thousand years early. The problem was not that Israel stopped drinking. They kept drinking. They simply drank from cisterns they had made themselves, which looked like water and could not hold it. Isaiah asks the same question: "Why do you spend your money for that which is not bread, and your labour for that which does not satisfy?" (Isaiah 55:2). A feed that never ends, and never fills us, is a broken cistern in our hands.

Then there is a warning in the Psalms about idols: "Those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them" (Psalm 115:8). We shape our tools, and then our tools shape us. A person who gives their first and last attention to a screen slowly begins to think in scrolls, to love in likes and to rest in noise.

Notice also that the Bible's answer to emptiness is never "do more". Jesus found Martha busy with many tasks, the complicated side of life, and said, "Mary has chosen the good portion, which will not be taken away from her" (Luke 10:41-42). Martha was not wicked. She was distracted. And that is the word for our age.

## The counterfeit formula

Brooks names two ways to order a life. The counterfeit one is: love things, use people, worship yourself. The right one is: use things, love people, worship God alone.

Read that again as a believer. It is almost a commentary on the Bible. "Do not love the world or the things in the world" (1 John 2:15). "You cannot serve God and money" (Matthew 6:24). Paul describes people who "exchanged the truth about God for a lie and worshipped and served the creature rather than the Creator" (Romans 1:25). And yet Scripture does not tell us to despise things. "Everything created by God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving" (1 Timothy 4:4). Use things, and thank God for them. Love people, because they bear God's image. Worship God alone, because only He can bear the weight of worship.

A phone, then, is not evil. It carries the Bible, sermons, prayer requests and family far away. The danger begins when a tool moves into the place that belongs to God.

## Why questions are holy

One more idea from the talk struck me. Brooks says that what makes us human is not answering questions but asking them, and especially the kind you cannot search for online.

Look at how God deals with us. His first words to Adam after the fall are a question: "Where are you?" (Genesis 3:9). He answers Job with questions. Jesus asks, "Who do you say that I am?" (Matthew 16:15). David looks at the night sky and asks, "What is man that you are mindful of him?" (Psalm 8:4). Faith in the Bible is a long conversation of deep questions. If a screen is never silent, the questions never get asked.

## Taking back the first hour and the last

Brooks's practical advice is to change our relationship with technology: no phone in the first hour of the day, at mealtimes, and in the last hour of the day. You do not need to throw it away. You need to put it in its place.

For a Christian this is not a productivity tip. It is worship. David wrote, "O God, you are my God; earnestly I seek you" (Psalm 63:1), and "in the morning you hear my voice" (Psalm 5:3). Jesus rose "very early in the morning, while it was still dark" to pray (Mark 1:35). Moses told Israel to speak of God's words "when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise" (Deuteronomy 6:7). That is mealtimes, bedtime and morning, exactly the places a phone has taken.

So try this for one week. Charge your phone outside the bedroom and use a cheap alarm clock. Before you look at anything, pray and read a few verses. At the table, put every phone face down. At night, read a passage and thank God for the day before you sleep. And each week, ask yourself the two questions again, in writing: Why am I alive? What would I give my life for?

Brooks tells of his son, who put the screen aside and went looking for answers. He came back able to say that he is alive because God made him to serve others. That is the kind of answer a believer can give, and it is never found by scrolling.

If you fail tomorrow, begin again. God's mercies "are new every morning" (Lamentations 3:23). Tomorrow, before you pick up the phone, choose the good portion. The notifications will still be there in ten minutes.

Note: this post draws on a talk by Arthur Brooks at the ARC 2026 conference. The left brain and right brain model is his way of explaining the argument, and the scientific picture is more complicated.
