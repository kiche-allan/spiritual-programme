## Revelation

The benediction is the most undervalued moment in the worship service. It is often received as the signal that the meeting is over — the dismissal that releases the congregation back into the ordinary week. People check their phones. The conversations begin before the last word has been spoken. The benediction is rushed through because everyone knows it is the end and the end is when you leave. And in treating the benediction as a dismissal, the church misses the most important thing the benediction is trying to do.

The benediction is not a dismissal. It is a commissioning.

Numbers 6:24–26 is the oldest benediction in the history of the people of God — given by God Himself to Aaron and his sons as the specific words they were to speak over Israel. The Lord bless you and keep you. The Lord make His face shine on you and be gracious to you. The Lord turn His face toward you and give you peace. Six lines. Three parallel couplets. Each one beginning with the Lord — the covenant name of God, the name that carries the full weight of His faithfulness and His personal commitment to His people. The blessing is not a wish or a hope or a prayer that things will go well. It is the declaration of what the Lord — the One who keeps every covenant, who never breaks His word — is actually doing for the people who bear His name.

The Lord bless you — the comprehensive, multi-dimensional flourishing of a person who is in right relationship with God. The Lord keep you — the active, vigilant, military-grade protection of someone who is being watched over by a guardian who neither slumbers nor sleeps. The Lord make His face shine on you — the specific image of a face turned toward you with warmth and delight, the face of a Father who is pleased to see you, the opposite of the hidden face of Psalm 13. The Lord be gracious to you — the specific word chen, which means the unmerited favour that flows from the character of the one giving it rather than the merit of the one receiving it. The Lord turn His face toward you — the repeated emphasis on the turning of the divine face, the insistence that the One who made you is facing you rather than facing away. And give you peace — shalom, the comprehensive wellbeing of a person who lacks nothing that matters.

This is what the people of God carry out of the worship gathering and into the ordinary week. Not the memory of a pleasant service. Not the residual warmth of a good sermon. The face of God turned toward them. The keeping of the One who neither slumbers nor sleeps. The grace that flows from character rather than merit. The shalom that no external circumstance can produce and no external circumstance can remove.

Ephesians 3:20–21 gives us the scale of what is being carried into the week: now to Him who is able to do immeasurably more than all we ask or imagine, according to His power that is at work within us. The phrase immeasurably more translates the Greek huperekperissou — beyond all measure, beyond all excess, a superlative of a superlative that exhausts the language trying to describe the magnitude of what God is able to do. And the power that accomplishes it is not somewhere outside the believer, requiring a special dispensation to be accessed. It is at work within us — the same power that raised Jesus from the dead, already resident, already active, already working in the person who walks out of the worship gathering and into the Monday morning.

Jude 24–25 closes with the most magnificent doxology in the New Testament — and it begins with the one promise that makes every other promise sustainable: to Him who is able to keep you from stumbling. He is able to keep you. Not you keeping yourself. Not the effort of your own vigilance and discipline and moral consistency. The One who is able — the same One who kept Hagar in the wilderness, who worked all things together for good in Joseph's thirteen years, whose goodness held through Calvary, who runs while you are still a long way off, who heard the surrender of Gethsemane and sent an angel to strengthen the One who prayed it — He is able to keep you from stumbling, and to present you before His glorious presence without fault and with great joy.

Without fault. The person who stands before the glorious presence at the end of the story is not presented tentatively, not presented as someone who barely made it, not presented with an asterisk of qualification. Without fault. And with great joy — the joy of the Father who has been watching the road, who ran when He saw the figure still a long way off, who has the best robe ready and the feast prepared and the ring that restores what was lost. You are going to arrive home. You are going to be presented without fault. And the joy will be great.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the benediction that has been spoken over Your people since the days of Aaron in the wilderness. The Lord bless me and keep me. The Lord make His face shine on me and be gracious to me. The Lord turn His face toward me and give me peace. I receive this not as a pleasant religious phrase but as the specific declaration of what the Lord — the One who keeps every covenant — is actually doing for me as I walk out of this moment of worship and into the ordinary week.

I carry Your face into Monday. Not the memory of a feeling. Your face — turned toward me, shining on me, giving me the shalom that no external circumstance can remove because it comes from You rather than from the circumstances.

And I trust the One who is able to keep me from stumbling to do exactly that — all the way to the glorious presence, where I will be presented without fault and with great joy. To the only God our Saviour be glory, majesty, power and authority, through Jesus Christ our Lord, before all ages, now and forevermore.

Amen.

## Daily Practices

- Read Numbers 6:22–27 in full. Note verse 27: so they will put my name on the Israelites, and I will bless them. The benediction is the placing of God's name on the people — the declaration of whose they are as they go into the world. Ask: what does it mean to carry the name of God into the ordinary week? How does knowing whose name is on you change how you inhabit Monday?
- Read Ephesians 3:14–21 in full — Paul's complete prayer that builds to the benediction of verse 20–21. Note what he prays for: to be rooted and established in love, to grasp the dimensions of Christ's love, to be filled to the measure of all the fullness of God. Ask: what dimension of this week's journey has most contributed to being rooted and established in love?
- Review the entire week — El Roi, Nothing Wasted, the Goodness of God, the Names of God, the Prodigal Father, Surrender, and the Benediction — and write one sentence for each day capturing what it produced in you. Seven sentences. This is your testimony of the week. Read it back to God as the closing offering.
- Read Jude 24–25 aloud as a declaration over yourself, over your family, over everyone you love. Let the confidence of to Him who is able to keep you from stumbling be the final word of the week — the assurance that the God who has been present in every theme of this week is the same God who is able to complete what He has begun, all the way to the glorious presence, without fault, with great joy.
