There is a particular kind of worship song that arrives at peace too quickly. The storm is named in the first verse, the chorus announces that everything is fine, and by the bridge, the trouble feels like a distant memory rather than a present reality. These songs are not dishonest — they are simply incomplete. They describe one end of the journey without sitting long enough in the middle of it for the people still there to feel seen.

Prince of Peace, written by Joel Houston, Matt Crocker and Dylan Thomas and recorded by Hillsong United, does something different. It stays in the storm longer than comfort would prefer. And in staying there, it says something about the nature of God's peace that the songs which resolve too quickly cannot say.

## My Heart a Storm

The song begins not with an announcement but with an admission: *my heart a storm, clouds raging deep within.* The opening image is interior — not the external circumstances producing difficulty, but the internal state of a person whose heart has become the storm itself. This is an important distinction, because the external circumstances can sometimes be managed, explained, or waited out. But when the storm has moved inside — when the anxiety, the grief, the fear, the doubt have taken up residence in the place where peace is supposed to live — there is nowhere to run from it. You carry it with you into every room.

If you have ever lain awake at three in the morning with thoughts that would not stop, or sat in a waiting room with a fear that no amount of distraction could quiet, or watched the life you planned come apart in ways you could not have prevented — you know what the song is describing in its opening line. The storm that is most difficult to survive is the one that has moved from outside to inside. My heart a storm.

And then: the Prince of Peace came bursting through the wind. Not tiptoeing in. Not arriving once the conditions were calm. Bursting — through the very thing that was causing the distress. The grammar of the song at this point is past tense — came — which suggests that the singer is not anticipating the peace, they are testifying to it. They have been in the storm. They are describing what happened inside it.

## The Violent Sky Held Its Breath

The first verse closes with one of the most arresting images in the entire song: *the violent sky held its breath.* This phrase is doing something theologically important. It is not saying the storm disappeared. It is saying the storm paused — held its breath, acknowledged the presence of something greater than itself. The peace that the Prince of Peace brings does not always manifest as the immediate removal of what was causing the distress. Sometimes it manifests as the authority of His presence being recognised by the very things that were raging.

This mirrors exactly what happened on the Sea of Galilee in Mark 4:39 — Jesus did not debate the storm or negotiate with it or explain to the disciples why it was permitted to be happening. He rebuked it. *Peace, be still.* And there was a great calm. The violent sky held its breath. The prince who commanded the weather on that particular evening is the same prince the song is addressing — and the peace He brought then was not the gentle fading of conditions that were already improving. It was the authority of a Person who stands above what is raging and commands it to recognise who He is.

## My Eyes Found Yours

The chorus gives us the central image the entire song is built around: *tearing through the night, riding on the storm, staring down the fight — my eyes found Yours.* This sequence matters. The tearing through the night. The riding on the storm. The staring down the fight. These are not descriptions of circumstances that have resolved. They are descriptions of circumstances that are still fully present and fully active — and in the middle of all of them, the turning point is not a change in the external situation. It is a change in where the eyes are directed.

My eyes found Yours.

This is the core of what Prince of Peace is saying, and it is the core of what the entire biblical theology of peace is saying. The peace that surpasses understanding, that Paul describes in Philippians 4:7, does not arrive because the anxiety-producing circumstances have been removed. It arrives — it guards the heart and mind — in the act of turning toward God rather than continuing to stare at the storm. Peter walked on water precisely as long as his eyes remained on Jesus. The moment his eyes found the wind instead of the One who commanded it, he began to sink. My eyes found Yours is not a description of the storm's ending. It is the description of the moment that changed everything even before the storm ended.

## The Violent Cross — The Empty Grave

Verse two expands the theology significantly. The same Prince of Peace who came bursting through the wind of the personal storm is the One who engaged the greatest storm in history — the cross. *The violent cross. The empty grave.* The word violent here is jarring in a worship song, but it is precisely right. The cross was not gentle. It was the full weight of human sin, divine wrath, and cosmic darkness concentrated into one event. The storm that broke over Calvary was not a personal storm — it was the storm that threatened to consume everything.

And the Prince of Peace rode it. He entered the violent cross and on the other side of it — the empty grave — He demonstrated that there is no storm He cannot pass through and emerge from the other side. The empty grave is the proof of concept for every personal storm the song has been describing. If He passed through death itself and the grave could not hold Him, then the storm in your heart, the violent sky of your circumstances, the night you cannot find your way through — none of these are beyond the jurisdiction of the One who came out of the grave on the other side of the worst thing that has ever happened in history.

*And in Your light I found grace.* Not just rest, as the first verse found. Grace. Rest is what the storm gives way to when peace arrives. Grace is what the cross makes available when you understand who absorbed the storm so you would not have to.

## When Night Screams Terror

The bridge of Prince of Peace is where the song reaches its fullest and most honest theological expression. It does not describe the absence of night or the removal of terror. It describes the presence of God inside both.

*When night screams terror — there Your voice will roar.* Not when night is silent. When it is screaming. *Come death or shadow — God I know Your light will meet me there.* Not when death has passed. When death is present. *When fear comes knocking — there You'll be my guard.* Not after fear has left. When it is at the door. *When day breeds trouble — there You'll hold my heart.* Not when the day is finally peaceful. When it is breeding the very trouble you are trying to survive.

The bridge is a sustained theological argument against the version of faith that requires circumstances to improve before it can function. It is saying: the peace of the Prince of Peace is not a peace that arrives after the night ends. It is a peace that meets you when the night is at its loudest, when the terror is screaming, when the fear has come knocking, when the day has produced trouble rather than respite. *God I know Your peace will meet me there.* Not somewhere else. There. In the exact place where everything is currently most difficult.

This is what makes the song worth singing at three in the morning, in the ICU waiting room, in the season when faith itself feels like it is being tested to its limit. It does not ask the night to become day first. It says the Prince of Peace will meet you in the night, as it is, where you are.

## Be Still and Know

The post-bridge gives us one of Scripture's most well-known phrases reframed in the context of everything the song has built toward: *Oh be still my heart, and know that You are God.* The original Psalm 46:10 — be still and know that I am God — is often quoted as though it is a gentle invitation to quietness. In its original context it is anything but. Psalm 46 is written against the backdrop of the earth giving way, mountains falling into the heart of the sea, nations in uproar. The stillness commanded is not the stillness of peaceful surroundings. It is the stillness commanded to a heart that is in the middle of chaos — the deliberate, willed quieting of a heart that would otherwise be in the same uproar as the nations and the mountains around it.

*Fear no evil, for I know You are here.* Not fear no evil because there is no evil. Because I know You are here. The presence of God does not remove the evil that produces fear. It removes the power of the fear to determine the outcome. The one who knows God is here can be still even when the world around them is anything but.

## What This Song Is Actually About

Prince of Peace is not a song about circumstances improving. It is a song about the discovery that the Prince of Peace meets you before they do — in the storm, not after it; in the night, not at dawn; in the terror, not once it has quieted; in the trouble, not when the day finally relents. The meeting precedes the resolution. The peace precedes the calm.

This is the most important thing the song has to say, and it is the most important thing the gospel has to say about peace: *My peace I give you. I do not give to you as the world gives.* The world's peace is the absence of trouble. The peace of the Prince of Peace is a Person who is present in the trouble before the trouble ends — and whose presence in it is itself the peace, regardless of what the circumstances are still doing.

If you are in the storm right now — the one inside your heart, the one producing the violent sky in your circumstances, the one that seems to have no end in sight — the testimony of this song is that you are not waiting for the Prince of Peace to arrive. He is already in the storm with you. Your prayer has been heard. Your eyes can find His, even now, even here, even in the middle of this.

*Come storm or battle — God I know Your peace will meet me there.*

And again and again.

## A Prayer

Prince of Peace, I bring You the storm I am in right now — the one inside me more than the one around me, the violent sky that has not yet held its breath, the night that is still screaming rather than quieting. I do not ask You to remove it before You arrive. I ask You to arrive as You always have — bursting through the wind, riding on the storm, meeting me there rather than waiting until conditions are more suitable for Your presence.

Let my eyes find Yours today. Not after the storm ends. Now, in the middle of it, where the song says You have always been. Be still, my heart. Fear no evil. He is here.

In the name of the Prince of Peace, Amen.
