## Revelation

Three stories. Three tables set where there should be no table. Three feasts produced where there was nothing to produce a feast with. And one God, present in all three, doing what only He can do — feeding the hungry from the insufficient, multiplying the nothing into the more than enough, setting a table in the wilderness and in the middle of the crowd and at the end of a road where the only guests are the people from the hedges.

The first table is in Zarephath. 1 Kings 17 — the widow gathering sticks, preparing what she has described with devastating precision as the last meal. Not the second-to-last. The last. A handful of flour in a jar and a little oil in a jug — and then we will eat it and die. She is past hope in the way that the person who has calculated the last of everything is past hope — not the dramatic despair of someone who has just encountered the crisis, but the quiet, settled, arithmetic despair of someone who has counted what is left and arrived at the number that means the end. And Elijah asks her to feed him first. Before the son. Before herself. Make a small loaf for me first. And then make something for yourself and your son. For this is what the Lord says — the jar of flour will not be used up and the jug of oil will not run dry.

The provision did not come in advance. It came in the daily — the flour that was there each morning in the amount needed for that morning's bread, the oil that had not run dry by the time the bread required it. The feast of Zarephath was not a banquet. It was the daily sufficiency of a God who provided exactly enough for today and who could be trusted to provide exactly enough for tomorrow. The widow did not receive a year's supply in one miraculous provision. She received one day's supply every day for the length of the famine — which required the daily return to the jar to discover whether the trust of the previous day had been honoured. It always had been.

The second table is on the hillside outside Bethsaida. John 6 — five thousand men, more with the women and children, and the disciples' assessment of the situation: eight months' wages would not buy enough bread for each one to have a bite. Philip's arithmetic is correct. The resources available are genuinely insufficient for the need. Andrew finds the boy with five loaves and two fish and asks the question that honest insufficiency always asks: but how far will they go among so many? Jesus takes what is insufficient, gives thanks over it, and distributes it — and the insufficiency becomes the more than enough. Twelve baskets of leftovers. The feast that began with nothing to feed a crowd with ended with more than it began with. The multiplication happened in the giving — the loaves increased not in the hands that held them back but in the hands that broke and distributed them.

The third table is in the parable of Luke 14. The great banquet — everything prepared, the fatted calf killed, the table set, the feast ready. And the invited guests begin to make their excuses. One has bought a field. One has bought five yoke of oxen. One has married a wife. Reasonable, normal, entirely understandable human preoccupations — and none of them adequate as reasons to miss the feast that is already prepared. The master's response is not to cancel the banquet. It is to expand the invitation. Go out quickly into the streets and alleys and bring in the poor, the crippled, the blind, and the lame. And when there is still room: go out to the roads and country lanes and compel them to come in, so that my house will be full.

The feast does not shrink because the originally invited have declined. It opens wider. The table that was set for the comfortable and the established becomes the table of the people from the hedges — the ones who had no expectation of being invited, who came not because they had earned a seat but because the master sent for them and compelled them to come. Come, for everything is now ready. The readiness is not contingent on the worthiness of the guests. The table was set before the guests arrived. Everything is ready.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the table that is already set — the provision that does not require my sufficiency to function, the feast that does not depend on the adequacy of what I am bringing. I bring You the widow's arithmetic — the handful of flour, the little oil, the calculation that ends at nothing. And I receive the word that preceded the provision: the jar will not be used up, the jug will not run dry. Not because I have enough. Because You are the Lord who provides from the insufficient.

Let me be the person who comes from the hedges — who arrives at the table not because I was the obvious guest but because the master sent for me and compelled me to come. Everything is ready. Let me come.

## Daily Practices

- Read 1 Kings 17:7–16 in full — the complete widow of Zarephath account. Note the specific sequence: the request comes before the provision, the obedience precedes the miracle, the daily sufficiency requires the daily return. Ask: where in my life is God asking me to make the small loaf for Him first — to act in trust before the provision has been confirmed — and what is my response to that request?
- Read John 6:1–15 in full — the feeding of the five thousand. Note that Jesus asked Philip where they could buy bread — and John adds that He already knew what He was going to do. The question was a test. Ask: where is God asking me the question He already knows the answer to — testing whether I will bring my insufficiency to Him honestly or manage it independently?
- Journal: "What is my widow's arithmetic right now — the specific calculation of insufficient resources that ends at nothing? What would it look like to bring specifically that — the handful of flour, the little oil — to the God who told the widow the jar would not run out? What does the daily sufficiency of Zarephath say about how God tends to provide?" Write the honest exploration.
- Read Luke 14:15–24 in full — the parable of the great banquet. Note the specific excuses offered and the specific people invited in their place. Ask: what is the excuse I am most tempted to offer for not coming fully to the table that is already prepared — and what would it look like to accept the compulsion of the master's invitation and simply come?
