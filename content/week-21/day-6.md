## Revelation

Romans 8:17 contains a phrase that most Christians read quickly and prefer not to linger over: if indeed we share in his sufferings in order that we may also share in his glory. The if indeed is not a condition of doubt — it is a condition of genuine participation. The sharing in the glory is inseparable from the sharing in the sufferings. Not sequential — suffer first, then glory later as the reward for endurance. Inseparable — the same participation that connects the believer to the sufferings of Christ connects the believer to the glory of Christ, because the sufferings and the glory are not two separate events in Christ's story. They are two dimensions of the same event. The cross and the resurrection are not opposites. They are one continuous act of God, the suffering that becomes the glory, the death that becomes the life, the descent that becomes the ascent.

This matters enormously for how the believer understands the suffering they are currently in. The popular version of the Christian life suggests that suffering is an interruption — a season to be survived before the real life of blessing and fruitfulness resumes. The New Testament version is radically different: suffering is not an interruption of the life of participation in Christ. It is a form of that participation. To share in His sufferings is to be in the most intimate possible alignment with the One who went to the cross — the alignment that produces not merely endurance but the specific kind of character, depth, and capacity for glory that can only be formed through the sharing.

1 Peter 4:13 goes further and introduces the word that seems most out of place in this context: rejoice. But rejoice inasmuch as you participate in the sufferings of Christ. The rejoicing is not despite the suffering. It is in the participating — because the participation itself is the evidence of the connection to Christ that the suffering is expressing. The person who is sharing in the sufferings of Christ is not someone whose faith has failed. They are someone whose faith has been confirmed at the deepest possible level — the level of genuine participation in the life of the One who suffered most completely and was glorified most completely as a result.

2 Corinthians 4:10 gives us the most personal and most visceral description of how the suffering-glory connection actually operates in the body of the believer: we always carry around in our body the death of Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may also be revealed in our body. The death and the life are simultaneously present. The dying is the condition for the revealing. And the both are carried in the same body — the same physical, earthly, suffering body that is also the location of the revealed life of Jesus. This is not a metaphor about spiritual transformation. It is a statement about what happens in the actual body of the person who is genuinely participating in the death and life of Christ. The dying is real. The revealing is real. And they happen together in the same location.

The practical implication of the inseparability of suffering and glory is the recovery of a specific kind of steadiness in the difficult season — not the steadiness of someone who has found a way to avoid the cost, but the steadiness of someone who has understood that the cost is the pathway. Not to the glory instead of the suffering. Through the suffering, as the very mechanism by which the sharing in the glory becomes possible. The cross was not an obstacle to the resurrection. It was the only route to it. And the specific kind of glory that Christ carries — the glory of the One who was wounded for our transgressions, who bears the marks of the nails even in His resurrection body — is a glory that can only be shared by those who have genuinely shared in what the marks represent.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the most uncomfortable and most liberating truth in Romans 8:17 — that the suffering and the glory are inseparable, that to share in His sufferings is to be in the most intimate possible alignment with the One who also shares His glory, and that the difficulty I am currently in is not an interruption of my participation in Christ but a form of it. The most specific, the most confirming, the most deeply connecting form.

Let me not resent what I am carrying today as though it were a contradiction of the life You have promised me. Let me receive it as participation — in the death of Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may also be revealed in my body. The death is real. The revealing is real. And they happen together, in the same location, in the same season, in me.

I rejoice today in the participating. Not because the suffering is pleasant. Because the participating means I am connected to the One who suffered most completely and was glorified most completely — and that connection is the most valuable thing available to a human being.

## Daily Practices

- Read Romans 8:14–25 in full — the full context of the suffering-glory passage. Note that the suffering of the believer is placed within the larger context of the groaning of all creation — we groan, the Spirit groans, creation groans. The suffering is not isolated. It is the participation of the believer in a cosmic longing for what is coming. Ask: how does placing my specific suffering within the context of creation's groaning change how I understand it?
- Read Hebrews 12:1–3 — the cloud of witnesses, the race, the fixing of eyes on Jesus who endured the cross for the joy set before him. Note that Jesus endured — the word implies the sustained, deliberate, costly holding of a difficult position over time. And the joy was set before him — not the joy of the cross, but the joy that the cross was the pathway to. Ask: what is the joy set before me that the current difficulty is the pathway to?
- Journal: "What is the specific suffering I am currently carrying? What glory might it be the pathway to — not the glory of having survived it, but the specific character, depth, and capacity for God's life to be revealed that can only be formed through this particular difficulty?" Write the honest exploration of the connection.
- Read Philip Yancey's book Where Is God When It Hurts if you have access to it. If not, read 2 Corinthians 1:3–7 and sit with the specific argument Paul makes: the God of all comfort comforts us in all our troubles so that we can comfort those in any trouble with the comfort we ourselves receive from God. The suffering is not only forming you. It is equipping you to be the specific comfort that someone else in a similar situation will one day need.
