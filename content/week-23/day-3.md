## Revelation

The first and second commandments address idolatry — and the prophets of Israel spend more sustained attention on it than on almost any other sin. Hosea, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel — all of them return again and again to the charge that Israel has gone after other gods, has exchanged the glory of God for images that cannot see or hear or save. And the contemporary church has largely filed this material in the ancient history section — relevant to people who literally bowed before carved images, interesting as a window into the Old Testament world, but not particularly pressing for people who do not own a golden calf and are unlikely to encounter one.

This filing is a catastrophic misreading of what the prophets were actually addressing — and what the New Testament, particularly Paul, was addressing when he carried the diagnosis forward. Colossians 3:5 makes the contemporary application explicit: greed which is idolatry. Not greed which is like idolatry, or greed which is as serious as idolatry. Greed which is idolatry — the same category, the same sin, the same fundamental orientation of the soul away from God toward something else as the source of value, security, and ultimate satisfaction. And if greed is idolatry, then the culture that has produced the most economically anxious, financially driven, material-accumulation-focused generation in human history is a culture saturated with idolatry — including the church that exists within it and has absorbed its values more thoroughly than it has recognised.

Exodus 20:3–4 gives us the foundational definition: you shall have no other gods before me. The phrase before me in Hebrew literally means in my face — no other gods in the presence of God, no competitor for His position allowed in the vicinity of the relationship. The commandment is not primarily about the list of acceptable and unacceptable objects of worship. It is about the exclusivity of the relationship — the total, uncompeted-with, undivided allegiance that the God who delivered Israel from Egypt demands and deserves. The first commandment is not a restriction on religious freedom. It is the definition of the relationship that makes the ten commandments possible — the relationship of a people who belong to the God who acted for them, who are bound to Him by gratitude and covenant, and who have no basis for divided loyalty because the One they are bound to has done everything that would be required to secure it.

Ezekiel 14:3 gives us the most important extension of the idolatry diagnosis: son of man, these men have set up idols in their hearts. Not only on the high places, not only in the carved images, not only in the temples of foreign gods. In their hearts. The idol of the heart is the thing that functions as god in the interior life — the thing that is trusted more than God is trusted, that is feared more than God is feared, that is desired more than God is desired, that gives meaning and worth and security in a way that displaces God from His proper position at the centre. The idol of the heart does not require a temple or a carving or a formal act of worship. It requires only that the heart has organized itself around something other than God — that the ultimate loyalty, the deepest trust, the most fundamental source of identity and security, is located somewhere other than in the living God.

Matthew 6:24 gives us Jesus's most direct statement on the modern form of idolatry: you cannot serve both God and money. The word serve is the word for the service of a slave to a master — the total, exclusive, unconditional availability of a person to the one who owns them. Jesus is not saying that money is evil or that having it is sinful. He is saying that money, like any other master, demands a totalising loyalty — and that the heart which has given its deepest loyalty to money has no remaining capacity for the totalising loyalty that God requires and deserves. The two masterships are incompatible. And the heart that tries to maintain both will not succeed — it will serve the one and despise the other.

The diagnosis of idolatry in the modern Christian life requires the specific, honest, uncomfortable inventory of what is actually functioning as god in the interior life — what is trusted most when security is threatened, what is desired most when freedom is available, what is feared most when it is at risk, what gives the deepest meaning when it is present and the deepest emptiness when it is absent. For many believers, the honest answer to these questions does not name the living God. It names financial security, relational approval, professional success, physical comfort, or the esteem of the communities they belong to. And the naming of these things as functional gods — not as sins to be condemned but as competitors to be identified and dethroned — is the beginning of the recovery that the first commandment has always been pointing toward.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I come to You today with the honest inventory that Ezekiel 14 demands — the idols of the heart, not the carved images on the high places but the interior masters that have been competing with You for the position that belongs only to You. I bring them to the light not in condemnation but in the honest recognition that You are the only God, the only source of security and meaning and worth that is actually adequate to the position the heart was designed to give it.

Show me what is functioning as god in my interior life — what I trust more than I trust You, what I fear more than I fear You, what I desire more than I desire You. And give me the grace to put to death, as Colossians 3:5 says, whatever belongs to the earthly nature that has organised itself around these competitors rather than around You.

You shall have no other gods before You. In Your face. In Your presence. I choose today to bring everything that has been standing in Your face and set it aside. You alone.

## Daily Practices

- Read Ezekiel 14:1–11 in full — the complete passage on the idols of the heart. Note that the people come to Ezekiel to inquire of the Lord — they are going through the religious motions — while maintaining the idols of their hearts. God's response is to answer them Himself, according to their idolatry. Ask: is it possible to maintain religious practice while simultaneously maintaining interior idols that God is already responding to — answering the idolatry rather than the inquiry?
- Read Matthew 6:19–34 in full — Jesus's complete teaching on money, treasure, the divided heart, and the impossibility of serving two masters. Note the practical instructions He gives: store up treasures in heaven, do not worry about food and clothing, seek first the kingdom. Ask: which of these specific instructions most directly challenges the functional god in your own interior life?
- Journal: "What is the honest answer to these four diagnostic questions — what do I trust most when security is threatened? What do I desire most when freedom is available? What do I fear most when it is at risk? What gives the deepest meaning when it is present? Do the answers name the living God, or do they name something else?" Write the honest audit without self-condemnation — the goal is identification, not shame.
- Read 1 John 5:21 — the final verse of John's letter: dear children, keep yourselves from idols. It is the last word of the most sustained New Testament treatment of love and abiding. Ask: why does John end his letter about love with a warning about idols? What is the connection between love for God and freedom from idols — and what does their relationship say about the nature of both?
