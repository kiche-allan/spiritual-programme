## Revelation

The book of Ruth opens with three losses in rapid succession — Naomi's husband, then her two sons, including Ruth's husband Mahlon. Three funerals. Two widows left in a foreign land. Naomi, the mother-in-law, releases her daughters-in-law to return to their own families, their own people, their own gods — and offers this release not as rejection but as the most honest love she has: go, find another husband, have a future. Orpah kisses her goodbye and returns. Ruth clings to her.

The word clung — dabaq in Hebrew — is the same word used in Genesis 2:24 for the husband who leaves father and mother and clings to his wife. It is the word of covenant commitment, of a bond that holds even when the circumstances argue for its release. Ruth had every legitimate reason to leave. She had no legal obligation to stay. The covenant that had bound her to Naomi's household was dissolved by her husband's death. And she clung anyway. Where you go I will go. Where you stay I will stay. Your people will be my people and your God will be my God. This is not a romantic speech. It is a covenant declaration — the voluntary, costly, utterly unnecessary choice of a Moabite widow to bind herself to a destitute Israelite mother-in-law with no visible future and nothing to offer.

This is hesed — the untranslatable Hebrew word for the covenant love that goes beyond what is required, that keeps faith when faith has not been earned, that remains loyal when loyalty has no remaining obligation. It is the word used of God's love for Israel across the entire Old Testament — the love that did not give up, that kept pursuing, that kept showing up with new mercies every morning even when Israel had done nothing to deserve new mercies. Ruth embodies hesed toward Naomi in exactly the way God embodies hesed toward His people. She is not merely a loyal daughter-in-law. She is a living parable of the character of God.

Proverbs 31:10–31 describes a woman of noble character — the eshet chayil, the woman of valour — and the portrait that emerges is not primarily a domestic one, despite how the passage is often read. It is the portrait of a woman of extraordinary strength, generosity, wisdom, industry, and dignity who manages a household and a business and a community of dependents with a competence and a grace that produces blessing everywhere it reaches. Ruth embodies every quality in the list. She is industrious — she goes to the field and gleans from morning until evening without stopping. She is generous — she brings home not only what she has gleaned but the extra that Boaz has instructed his workers to leave for her, and she shares it with Naomi. She is courageous — she goes to the threshing floor at night, a vulnerable and potentially dangerous act, and presents herself to Boaz as a candidate for redemption. She is a woman of noble character, as Boaz himself declares in Ruth 3:11. She is clothed with strength and dignity.

And she is a Moabite widow with no children and no property and no standing in the community she has chosen. The Proverbs 31 woman is not primarily a woman of impressive resources. She is a woman of impressive character — and character, the book of Ruth demonstrates with exquisite clarity, is available to everyone regardless of circumstance, origin, or the weight of loss that has preceded the moment of choosing.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today the portrait of Ruth — the woman who chose hesed when she had no obligation to, who clung when every circumstance argued for release, who went to a foreign people and a foreign land and brought nothing with her except the character that turned out to be the only thing that mattered. Let her story speak to me today about the kind of faithfulness that is available to me regardless of what I have lost and regardless of what I do not have.

Where I have been waiting for better circumstances before I live with the kind of integrity Ruth demonstrated in the worst of hers, correct me. The strength and dignity of Proverbs 31:25 is not the product of good circumstances. It is the product of good character — and good character is available now, today, in this season, in this specific situation, with what I currently have.

Let me be someone whose hesed toward others reflects Your hesed toward me.

## Daily Practices

- Read the book of Ruth in full — it is only four chapters and takes about twenty minutes. Read it as a story rather than a study, letting the narrative carry you. Note every specific choice Ruth makes and ask: what does each choice reveal about her character?
- Read Proverbs 31:10–31 immediately after reading Ruth. Note every quality in the Proverbs 31 portrait and find its expression in Ruth's story. The industriousness, the generosity, the wisdom, the courage, the dignity. Ask: which of these qualities is most present in my own life, and which is most needed?
- Journal: "Ruth chose hesed — covenant love beyond obligation — toward Naomi. Who in my life is the equivalent of Naomi — the person I have a legitimate reason to release but to whom I am being called to cling instead? What would choosing hesed toward them look like practically?"
- Read Matthew 1:5 — Boaz was the father of Obed, whose mother was Ruth. Ruth the Moabite widow is in the genealogy of Jesus. Ask: what does her inclusion in the lineage of the Messiah say about the God who honours faithful hesed regardless of origin, history, or the weight of the loss that preceded the faithfulness?
