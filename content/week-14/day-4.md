## Revelation

The English word wait carries a connotation of passivity — standing still, doing nothing, simply letting time pass until something changes. This is almost the opposite of what biblical waiting describes. The Hebrew word most often translated wait is qavah, and its root meaning is to twist or stretch, like a rope being tightened or a cord being pulled taut with expectant tension. It is the image of something straining forward, fully engaged, not slack. Biblical waiting is not the absence of activity. It is a specific kind of intense, expectant, postured activity directed toward God while the circumstances remain unresolved.

Isaiah 40:31 gives us the clearest picture of what this active waiting produces: those who hope in the Lord will renew their strength. They will soar on wings like eagles; they will run and not grow weary, they will walk and not be faint. Notice that the verse moves from the most dramatic image to the most ordinary one — soaring, then running, then walking. This is deliberate. The person who learns to wait on the Lord is not promised constant soaring. They are promised the capacity to walk without fainting — the ability to keep moving through the most ordinary, unremarkable, undramatic stretches of the wait without collapsing. This is often the most needed promise. Not the eagle's flight, but the unfainting walk through Tuesday.

Psalm 27:14 gives the instruction in its most direct form, and it repeats itself for emphasis: wait for the Lord; be strong and take heart and wait for the Lord. The repetition is not redundant — it is the structure of a command being given twice because it needs to be heard twice, because the waiting is hard enough that the instruction has to be reinforced. And notice that be strong and take heart sit between the two instances of wait for the Lord. The strength and the courage are not the prerequisite for waiting. They are the content of it. Waiting on the Lord is not what you do until you feel strong enough. It is itself the practice that produces the strength.

Lamentations 3:24–26 gives us the most settled posture available in the middle of genuine devastation — this book was written in the aftermath of Jerusalem's destruction, among the deepest grief in the Old Testament. And in the middle of that grief, the writer says: the Lord is my portion; therefore I will wait for him. The decision to wait follows directly from the decision about what God is to him — not a reward, not an outcome, not a resolved circumstance, but a portion. The thing you actually possess. If God Himself is your portion, the waiting is not waiting for something else to arrive. It is the practice of staying close to the portion you already have while the circumstances around it remain unresolved.

## Morning Prayer

Father, I receive today a fuller understanding of what it means to wait on You. Not passivity. Not the empty marking of time until something changes. The taut, expectant, strained-forward posture of someone who is actively engaged with You even while the circumstances remain exactly as they are.

Renew my strength today — not necessarily for soaring, but for the walking that does not faint through the ordinary, undramatic stretch of this wait. Be strong and take heart, You say, and I receive that the strength and the courage are not what I need before I can wait well. They are what the waiting itself is producing in me.

You are my portion. Not the resolution I am waiting for — You. Let that truth be settled enough in me that the waiting becomes something other than mere endurance. Let it become closeness.

## Daily Practices

- Read Isaiah 40:27–31 in full. Note the context — Israel is complaining that their cause is disregarded by God, that He has forgotten them. The promise of renewed strength is given directly into that complaint, not after it has been resolved. Ask: what does it mean that this promise was given to people who felt exactly as discouraged as I currently do?
- Read the Hebrew word study on qavah if you have access to a concordance or study tool — the imagery of a taut cord or rope being stretched. Journal on how this reframes your understanding of what you have been doing in your own waiting. Has it felt more like slack passivity or taut expectancy?
- Journal: "What would it look like, practically, for my waiting this week to be active rather than passive? What specific postures — prayer, worship, service, gratitude — could I bring to the waiting rather than simply enduring it?"
- Memorise Psalm 27:14 today. Say it aloud whenever the wait feels heaviest this week: wait for the Lord; be strong and take heart and wait for the Lord. Let the repetition in the verse become a repeated practice in your own life.
